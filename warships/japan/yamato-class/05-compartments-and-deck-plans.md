# Yamato class: compartments and deck plans

Status: partial (see gaps.md)

No booklet of general plans for these ships survives in a form that could be fetched. The Japanese destroyed the design drawings in August 1945 [S11, p. 7]. What follows is what tier 1 and tier 2 sources actually document: the deck naming, the spaces around the main battery turrets as US Naval Technical Mission report O-45(N) drew them from Japanese sketches, and the machinery and storage spaces named by frame number in the damage narratives of report S-06-2.

## Deck naming

Figure 15 of report O-45(N), a section at frame 96 with plans of the 1st and 2nd Hold Decks, labels the decks from the top down as [S11, p. 23]:

| Deck, as labelled | Notes |
|---|---|
| Flying Br. (flying bridge) | |
| Upper Dk. | |
| Middle Dk. | |
| Lower Dk. | the ring bulkhead of the main turret was secured to the ship at lower shell handling room level on this deck [S11, p. 19] |
| Platform Dk. | |
| 1st Hold Dk. | shell rooms, upper and lower main gun magazines, powder supplying rooms |
| 2nd Hold Dk. | lower main gun magazine, hydraulic pipe passages, air bottle room |

Report S-06-2 uses a different and coarser set of names for the same ship, and glosses them for an American reader. The Japanese "flying deck" is the US upper or forecastle deck [S12, p. 11]. The report also names the second (armoured) deck, the upper deck and the main deck [S12, pp. 8, 12]. Report O-45(N) calls the 200 mm armoured deck at the turret the main deck [S11, p. 19].

The design stability criteria treat everything above the armoured second deck, both ends of the ship, and all voids outboard of the side armour as unprotected structure, and the calculations included volume up to the flying (forecastle) deck [S12, p. 8].

## Spaces around a main battery turret

Figure 12 of O-45(N) is a side view of the Type 94 18-inch triple mount showing the spaces it occupied, from the gunhouse down to the centre pivot on the triple bottom [S11, p. 20]. Read from the top down the mount contained the gunhouse with its rangefinder, shell bogies and rammers; the turntable; the upper shell handling room; the lower shell handling room and the path for the shell transfer bogie; the upper cordite handling room; the lower cordite handling room; and the centre pivot. Outside the revolving structure, on the same levels, sat the shell room with its push-pull gear, the upper magazine entered through the ring bulkhead, and the lower magazine, with the cordite transfer bogie and the cordite hoist between them [S11, p. 20].

The relationship the report states in words: magazines were fitted around both the upper and lower cordite handling rooms; shell rooms and shell handling rooms were above the magazines [S11, pp. 18, 19]. Half the shells were stowed in the revolving structure and half in the shell rooms, from which they were supplied to the lower shell handling room only [S11, p. 18].

| No. 1 Main Gun Magazine (upper) | 1st Hold Deck |

The magazine spaces are ringed by compartments the drawing marks W.T.C., watertight compartments, running to the shell on both sides and forward [S11, p. 23]. The magazines and handling rooms were protected from underwater explosion by a triple bottom of 75 to 80 mm plating [S11, p. 19].

When Yamato was first commissioned there were no watertight fore-and-aft bulkheads between the magazines; they were fitted after the Battle of the Philippines for damage control [S11, p. 19].

The cordite hoist trunk was 3 ft 1 in by 9 ft 5 in internally and ran about 55 feet from the cordite handling rooms to the gunhouse in "A" turret, which gives the height of that part of the structure [S11, p. 29].

## Machinery and storage spaces by frame number

The damage narratives in S-06-2 name spaces as the Japanese officers named them and locate hits by frame. Taken together they give the arrangement of the machinery box and of the spaces at the ends.

Machinery spaces named for Yamato and Musashi [S12, pp. 12 to 22]:

- Firerooms numbered at least Nos. 1 to 12, arranged inboard and outboard on each side. Yamato lost Nos. 8 and 12 to port to torpedoes and had Nos. 3 and 11 to starboard deliberately flooded [S12, pp. 13, 14].
- Engine rooms: port outboard, port inboard, starboard outboard, starboard inboard. Yamato's last surviving space was the starboard inboard engine room [S12, p. 14]. Musashi's chief engineer fought the ship from the port inboard engine room control booth, which the report calls the control engine room [S12, p. 17].
- Hydraulic machinery rooms, port and starboard. A bulkhead at frame 143 separated Yamato's port outboard engine room from her port hydraulic machinery room [S12, p. 13].

Yamato's own damage summary of 25 December 1943, decrypted by US codebreakers, located the hole from frame 163 to frame 170 [S1].

## Superstructure levels

The Technical Mission located battle stations by level above the flying deck [S12, p. 11]:

| Station | Position |
|---|---|
| Upper combat bridge | eight levels above the flying deck; the Chief of Staff was there |
| Staff gunnery officer's position | about the sixth level, where the officer in tactical command and staff usually were |
| Conning tower | executive officer and assistant gunnery officer; the inclinometer was here |

Both the executive officer and the assistant gunnery officer received damage control reports by telephone and by messenger [S12, p. 11]. Musashi's executive officer's battle station was on the third level behind the conning tower [S12, p. 17].

## Other spaces named in the record

combinedfleet's Yamato record names spaces hit or wrecked during the Sibuyan Sea attacks of 24 October 1944: the anchor deck, the port chain locker, the mess deck, the crews' quarters, the forecastle and the main breakwater [S1]. The NHHC essay adds the dispensary, where a bomb impact on 7 April 1945 killed many of the wounded who had been gathered there along with medical personnel [S24]. S-06-2 records a bomb passing through the after secondary battery fire control station and wrecking the after director, then penetrating the flying and upper decks and detonating above the second (armoured) deck, gutting the after 15.5 cm mount [S12, p. 12]. Another bomb hit near frame 150 on the starboard side of the flying deck and wrecked a 12.7 cm mount, making holes 18 to 22 feet across [S12, p. 12].

Yamato was fitted with an elevator, which the German naval attaché admired during his visit of 16 July 1943 along with the bridge, the air defence centre and the main gun director [S1].

## The Yamato Museum 1:10 model

The Kure Maritime Museum's 1:10 model of Yamato is 26.3 metres long and was reproduced in as much detail as possible from design drawings, photographs and underwater survey footage [S29]. The museum records that the model was overhauled and altered on the basis of survey and research in the 2024 to 2025 refurbishment, twenty years after the museum opened in 2005 [S31]. No deck-by-deck description of the model was published on the pages fetched.

## Conflicts between sources

- Deck naming. O-45(N) Figure 15 uses Flying Bridge, Upper, Middle, Lower, Platform, 1st Hold and 2nd Hold Decks [S11, p. 23]. S-06-2 uses flying deck, upper deck, second (armoured) deck and main deck, glossing the flying deck as the US upper or forecastle deck [S12, pp. 8, 11, 12]. O-45(N) calls the 200 mm armoured deck the main deck [S11, p. 19]. The two schemes have not been reconciled by any source fetched.
