## Machinery arrangement

The plant sits in eight compartments between turrets II and III. Four fire rooms and four engine rooms alternate, each fire room feeding the engine room abaft it [S57]. The museum crew handbook gives the frames and the shaft each engine room drives (museum handbook, undated) [S62, p. 54].

| Engine room No. 1 | 95 to 103 | starboard outboard shaft (No. 1) |
| Fire room No. 2 | 103 to 111 | steam for engine room No. 2 |
| Engine room No. 2 | 111 to 119 | port outboard shaft (No. 4) |

The December 1954 liquid-loading diagram shows the same eight hold compartments on its inboard profile, numbered B-1-1 through B-8-1 from forward to aft between frames 87 and 151 (1954 diagram) [S67, sheet 1]. The 1984 booklet of general plans labels them Fire Room No. 1 (B-1-1), Eng Room No. 1 (B-2-1), Fire Room No. 2 (B-3-1), Engine Room No. 2 (B-4-1), Fire Room No. 3 (B-5-1), Engine Room No. 3 (B-6-1), Fire Room No. 4 (B-7-1), and Engine Room No. 4 (B-8-1), with uptakes No. 1 to No. 4 rising from the fire rooms and an escape trunk from each space (1984 configuration) [S68, sheet 4]. The same sheet places a Forward Emer Diesel & Dist Plant (A-58E) at hold level immediately forward of fire room No. 1 and an Aft Emer Diesel Gen Rm (C-701E) immediately aft of engine room No. 4 (1984 configuration) [S68, sheet 4].

Naval Gazing's author, citing Sumrall, confirms the pairing: engine room 1 drives shaft 1, engine room 2 drives shaft 4, engine room 3 drives shaft 2, engine room 4 drives shaft 3 [S57, author comment of 24 January 2024]. His essay says any boiler can supply any turbine, that Iowa sometimes steamed on as few as two of her eight boilers, normally on four, and lit all eight when full speed might be needed [S57]. In the later comment he narrows the cross-connection to a forward group (boilers 1 and 2 with engines 1 and 2) and an aft group (boilers 3 and 4 with engines 3 and 4) [S57, author comment of 24 January 2024]. Both statements are listed under conflicts.

The subdivision was a detail-design change. The ships were first planned with unit machinery on South Dakota lines, four rooms each holding boilers and a turbine set. At 212,000 SHP the unit rooms would have been 64 ft long, so a torpedo hit on a dividing bulkhead would flood 128 ft. In November 1939 the scheme changed to separate boiler and engine rooms, halving the floodable length, at the cost of weight and a foot of beam [S59]. The machinery weighed 4,444 tons (essay undated; it cites Sumrall and Garzke and Dulin) [S57].

## Boilers

Eight Babcock and Wilcox M-type water-tube boilers, two per fire room, make steam at 600 psi and 850 °F (454 °C) [S57] [S62, p. 54]. Polmar, via a Wikipedia footnote, gives the same 600 psi operating pressure and a maximum superheater outlet temperature of 850 °F [S70]. navypedia lists "8 Babcock & Wilcox boilers" [S45]; HyperWar's ship page lists "8 600 psi boilers" [S49]; the Pacific Battleship Center tour page counts eight boilers and four turbines [S63].

Naval Gazing describes the water side. Feedwater passes through an economizer in the exhaust, into the steam drum at the top, down the downcomers to the water drums at the bottom, up the generating tubes to the steam drum, through moisture separators, then into the superheater. The steam and water mix leaves the tubes at about 485 °F, and the superheater raises the steam to 850 °F. A boiler technician at the steam drum controls water level by hand [S57]. On the fire side each boiler has nine oil burners, five to evaporate and four to superheat. Steam atomizers spray the oil. The firing rate is set by cutting burners in and out and by swapping atomizer plates of different orifice. The atomizer sits in a gooseneck through the center of an air register, and a periscope with a lamp across the exhaust lets the operator judge smoke. Soot blowers spray steam over the tubes [S57]. A cold boiler takes about an hour to bring up [S57].

Forced draft blowers: no Iowa-specific source found; see the gaps file.

In August 1985 a minor boiler casualty held Iowa to seven boilers, so full power was not reached on the measured mile [S52, p. 14].

navypedia gives "4 sets General Electric geared steam turbines" for the whole class [S45]. The crew handbook gives General Electric for Iowa [S62, p. 55]. naval-encyclopedia splits the class the same way NavSource does [S65].

Each engine room holds one cross-compound set, a high-pressure and a low-pressure turbine, driving one shaft through a double-reduction gear [S57] [S62, pp. 55 to 56]. Polmar, via a Wikipedia footnote, calls the sets double reduction, double helical, locked train, cross-compound geared turbines [S70].

The crew handbook gives 24,400 SHP from the HP turbine and 28,600 SHP from the LP turbine, 53,000 SHP per engine room at 202 rpm, with the HP turbine at 4,905 rpm and the LP turbine at 3,195 rpm (museum handbook, undated) [S62, pp. 56, 59]. Sumrall, via a Wikipedia footnote, has the HP turbine turning at up to 2,100 rpm, no more than 50 psi at the LP inlet, and propeller shafts at up to 225 rpm [S71]. These rpm values disagree; see conflicts.

Rated power is 212,000 shaft horsepower on four shafts (as designed) [S41] [S45] [S48] [S49] [S57] [S62, p. 59]. FTP 218 calls 212,000 s.h.p. the designed shaft horsepower [S50, p. 80]. The 20% design overload gives 254,000 SHP [S53] [S54].

The handbook describes astern blading of opposite pitch at each end of the LP assembly and gives a maximum reverse speed of 17.5 knots (museum handbook, undated) [S62, pp. 56, 59]. The throttle board carries a red wheel for the reversing turbine and a green wheel for the ahead turbine, never opened together, and the throttleman had a mirror to watch the shaft leaving the gearbox [S58]. The condenser sits under the LP turbine at 12 to 14 psi below atmospheric, and condensate goes to a deaerating feed tank in the fire room before returning as feedwater [S57]. At low speed a pump (the "Bilge Dragon") supplies the condenser cooling water that ram effect supplies at speed [S58].

Naval Gazing gives the same shaft lengths, 340 ft, 243 ft, 179 ft, and 277 ft for shafts 1 to 4 [S57]. A New York Navy Yard newspaper of 27 August 1942, quoted in the handbook, put the total shafting at 1,074 ft (as built, 1942) [S62, p. 13]. Each shaft is built of 23 in diameter hollow sections with 4.25 in walls, bolted together [S62, p. 57]. The 1985 torsionmeters sat on a solid section of shafting 23 in in outside diameter [S52, p. 16]. The Pacific Battleship Center gives 24 3/8 in [S64]. Port shafts turn counterclockwise and starboard shafts clockwise, and the inboard screws run 2 to 10 rpm faster [S62, p. 59]. A plaque at the 04-level lee helm gives 219 rpm for 31 knots (museum handbook, undated) [S62, p. 59].

The inner shafts run in skegs [S59]. An NHHC caption on a photograph of New Jersey before launch notes "inboard propeller shafts incorporated in the hull skegs, and twin rudder posts directly aft of them" [S48].

Rudders. Two [S52, p. 4] [S69], semi-balanced [S69]. Projected area 335 ft² (31 m²) per rudder [S52, p. 4]. The museum gives 340 sq ft, 21 ft tall, and 116,840 lb (58.42 tons) each [S64], and its handbook "twin rudders 21 feet tall" [S62, p. 61]. Tactical diameter is 814 yd at 30 knots and 760 yd at 20 knots (Friedman, via Wikipedia footnote) [S69]. The 1985 tactical trials ran nine right turns at nominal 15°, 25°, and 35° rudder at nominal 15, 22, and 30 knots, plus three left-rudder checks at 35°; right rudder gave less advance and a smaller tactical diameter than left; the report gives results only as ratios to the largest circle [S52, p. 15]. The 1943 New Jersey tactical trials took the 20-knot turn with 15° right rudder as the reference circle [S52, p. 14].

## Electrical plant

Ship service generators. Each engine room holds two ship's service turbine generators (SSTGs) of 1,250 kW, eight in all, giving 10 MW of non-emergency power at 450 V, 3-phase, 60 Hz AC; Friedman, via a Wikipedia footnote, adds 2,005 A per generator and 4,000 A on the bus [S69]. The handbook gives two turbo-generators per engine room at 450 V nameplate, 3-phase, 60 Hz, for the ship's 440/110 VAC needs, with a panel in each engine room to control and monitor its two generators (museum handbook, undated) [S62, p. 58]. Naval Gazing's photographs show two 1,250 kW SSTGs across the engine room from the main turbines, each with its own seawater-cooled condenser, and "the main switchboards in Engine 2" [S58]. A reader who states he served as an Iowa electrician in the 1980s adds four main switchboards, one per engine room; two emergency switchboards, one in each auxiliary machinery room; Westinghouse SSTGs rated 480 V, 1,250 kW, 2,005 A, 3-phase, 60 Hz; a plant normally run split fore and aft; and ten 400 A shore-power receptacles after the first Norfolk yard period (reader comment, 1980s configuration) [S58, reader comment of 7 February 2025].

Emergency generators. Two 250 kW emergency diesel generators [S69] [S65]. The 1984 plan places a Forward Emer Diesel & Dist Plant (A-58E) forward of fire room No. 1 and an Aft Emer Diesel Gen Rm (C-701E) aft of engine room No. 4 (1984 configuration) [S68, sheet 4].

Installed capacity. The Navy Yard newspaper of 27 August 1942 gave generator capacity as 10,500 KW, "the industrial and domestic load of a city of about 20,000", with 900 motors, 5,300 lighting fixtures, 1,091 telephones, and 250 miles of electric cable (as built, 1942) [S62, p. 13]. Friedman's 10 MW covers the SSTGs alone [S69]. Both figures sit under conflicts.

Casualty power. A casualty power system of large three-wire cables and wall outlets called "biscuits" lets damaged circuits be bypassed [S65] [S72].

Turret load. navweaps lists the 16-inch/50 Mark 7 turret motors: a 300 hp electric motor driving hydraulic training gear; per gun a 60 hp elevating motor, a 60 hp rammer motor, a 75 hp projectile hoist motor, and a 100 hp powder hoist motor; and a 40 hp motor per shell ring [S56]. The reader comment above records that lighting off a turret training motor tripped the ship off shore power until the receptacle count was raised (1980s) [S58, reader comment of 7 February 2025]. A turret load in kW: no source found; see the gaps file.

## Fuel

Fuel type. The ships burned "black bunker oil" thick enough to need steam heating in the tanks; the 1980s reactivation removed the heaters, cleaned and resealed the fuel system, and converted the ships to Distillate Fuel, Marine (DFM), so they could take fuel from oilers and pass it to escorting destroyers [S57]. The handbook has "heavy fuel oil but converted to Navy Standard Distillate fuel in the 1980s" [S62, p. 60]. FTP 218 names the two fuels "fuel oil" and "diesel oil" and converts at 277 gal per ton of fuel oil, 314 gal per ton of diesel oil, and 42 gal per barrel [S50, p. 81]. New Jersey's 1943 trial fuel weighed 8.09 lb/gal and Iowa's 1985 fuel 7.08 lb/gal [S52, pp. 14, 17]. An official grade designation for the wartime oil: no source found; see the gaps file.

Tank locations and the torpedo defense. The December 1954 liquid-loading diagram for BB 61-64, drawn by the New York Naval Shipyard for the Bureau of Ships, colors fuel oil, fuel oil tanks to be ballasted, service and contaminated fuel oil, diesel or fog oil, fresh water, reserve feed water, and voids [S67, sheet 1]. On its first-platform plan the wing tanks along both sides of the machinery spaces, in the portion read (frames 103 to 151), are fuel oil tanks (B-numbers with suffix F) alternating with voids (suffix V), with "F.O. SERVICE P/S" and "CONTAMINATED OIL" tanks among them; the inner-bottom plan under the machinery spaces shows water tanks (suffix W) and voids, while the inner bottom forward of frame 87 and aft of frame 151 holds fuel oil tanks (A-9xxF and C-9xxF) [S67, sheet 1]. Each tank carries its capacity in tons and the list it induces when flooded [S67, sheet 1]. The diagram's flooding data rest on 55,649 tons displacement and 35 ft 2 in draft (1954) [S67, sheet 1]. Its note 7 refers tank capacities in gallons and tons for battle condition C and full load condition D to the "BB 62 Damage Control Book Part II(a)", a document not located [S67, sheet 1].

The diagram's liquid loading instructions, paraphrased:

- Fuel oil storage tanks marked for ballasting are filled with salt water to the 35-foot waterline or to their tops, whichever is lower, as soon as they are emptied, and not more than two such tanks per side are slack or empty at one time [S67, sheet 1].
- Fuel oil storage tanks not so marked are left empty once oil has been drawn from them [S67, sheet 1].
- Fuel oil service tanks are used so that no two adjacent service tanks are slack or empty at the same time [S67, sheet 1].
- Slack or empty storage and service tanks in the underwater defense system are spaced as far apart along the ship as practicable, to limit list from underwater damage, and at least one layer of liquid is kept throughout the length of the underwater defense system to limit penetration [S67, sheet 1].
- These limits may be relaxed while fueling at sea to speed refueling, but the number of empty fuel oil wing tanks is kept to the minimum the operation needs [S67, sheet 1].

Naval Gazing's author states that the US Navy used oil in all of its torpedo defense systems, and his essay carries a section drawing of Iowa's armor and TDS at frame 101 [S61] [S61, author comment of 21 March 2023].

Underway replenishment. Wisconsin's DANFS entry records the practice in 1945. On 2 January she fueled the destroyers Lewis Hancock and Hunt while taking fuel from the oiler Taluga. On 22 February she refueled Mertz and Monssen. On 23 February she took fuel from Merrimack and refueled Colahan, Hunt, and Remey. Over her WWII service she fueled her screening destroyers on some 250 occasions [S47]. In 1991 the same entry calls her an "armored oiler" that passed over one million gallons to more than a dozen US and allied ships [S47]. The Naval Gazing author states that Iowa had the equipment to UNREP destroyers, did so frequently, and carries the kingpost for it to starboard [S60, author comment of 25 June 2018]. The handbook says the kingpost on the starboard side supported the highlines that bore the weight of the fuel-transfer hoses and that Iowa "often was used in a support role to refuel other ships" (museum handbook, undated; it names the later STREAM rig) [S62, p. 77]. Naval Gazing's account of the alongside method: a phone-and-distance line is fired across first, then shot lines, messengers, and the spanwires that carry the hoses; several hoses go over at once; WWII separation was often 50 ft or less against about 160 ft with modern rigs; modern rigs pump up to 180,000 gal per hour per hose [S60]. The DANFS entry also records the December 1944 typhoon catching ships "refueling and with little ballast in their nearly dry bunkers" [S47].

## Auxiliary systems

Distilling plant. Sumrall, via a Wikipedia footnote, gives three evaporators making 60,000 US gallons per day, feeding the boilers first and then the potable systems, with urinals and all but one toilet flushed with salt water [S71]. naval-encyclopedia repeats the three evaporators and 60,000 gallons and adds a fresh water capacity of 210,000 gallons [S65]. The handbook gives one 20,000 gpd and two 40,000 gpd distillers, 100,000 gallons a day for boilers and crew (museum handbook, undated) [S62, p. 60]. The 1984 plan labels a Forward Emer Diesel & Dist Plant (A-58E) forward of fire room No. 1 (1984 configuration) [S68, sheet 4].

Steering gear. Turning the wheel sends electrical signals to two hydraulic ram steering engines near the stern on the 4th deck. The primary helm is on the 04 level inside the armored conning tower and the secondary on the 08 level of the main battery fire control tower. Two after-steering stations beside the ram engines can take control, and as a last resort the rudders move by block, tackle, and muscle [S62, p. 61]. The steering gear sits in a 13.5 in Class A armored compartment [S62, p. 61] [S65]. The 1984 plan shows Steering Gear Rm No. 2 (C-436E) and Steering Gear Pwr & Cont. Room No. 2 (C-432E) at the stern, on the sheet's frame scale between about frames 190 and 205 (1984 configuration) [S68, sheet 4]. The lee helm on the 04 and 08 levels carries an engine order telegraph and an engine revolution indicator wired to all four engine rooms [S62, p. 60]. Pumps, motors, and ram count: no source found; see the gaps file.

Air compressors. No source found; see the gaps file.

Refrigeration. The 1942 Navy Yard newspaper counted 80 miles of piping, 17 miles of it for refrigeration and cooling, and 14,140 valves (as built, 1942) [S62, p. 13]. The 1984 plan labels a Refr Mchry Rm (C-428E) beside the provisions storerooms aft (1984 configuration) [S68, sheet 4].

Ventilation, heating, and air conditioning. The same newspaper counted 16 miles of ventilation ducts (as built, 1942) [S62, p. 13]. Bucket ventilators on deck are waterproof; mushroom ventilators are hand-operated [S62, p. 22]. The 1984 plan labels fan rooms, air intakes, and a plenum chamber in the superstructure (1984 configuration) [S68, sheet 4]. The DTRC report attributes Iowa's higher 1985 fuel rates partly to a greater hotel load, "air conditioning, electronics suites, etc.", than New Jersey carried in 1943 [S52, p. 18]. Air conditioning in the WWII configuration: no source found; see the gaps file.

Engine room conditions. Temperatures often exceeded 120 °F, and the machinist's mates drank a lot of water and often went shirtless [S58].
