# Littorio class: operations and doctrine

Status: partial (see gaps.md)

## Employment doctrine

The Regia Marina's fighting in the Mediterranean was not a Mahanian search for a decisive battle. It was sea control over the communications with North Africa and, for some months, with Albania and Greece, that is protection of the sea lines of communication; losses in that kind of war came mainly by attrition from submarines, aircraft and mines and only secondarily from enemy surface action [S14].

Battleships were used more sparingly than cruisers, both to save them from unprofitable loss and to save fuel [S14]. Missions flown by the three completed ships of the class, from the Italian official statistical volumes:

| Ship | Missions 10 June 1940 to 8 September 1943 | Miles | Missions 8 September 1943 to 25 April 1945 | Miles | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Littorio | 49 | 12,452 | 3 | 2,427 | [S14, table 1] |
| Vittorio Veneto | 56 | 17,828 | 3 | 2,397 | [S14, table 1] |
| Roma | 17 | 2,523 | lost 9 September 1943 | | [S14, table 1] |

That works out at about 1.2 missions a month for Littorio over the period of belligerency, against 1.6 a month for the light cruiser Duca d'Aosta [S14].

The Marina Militare's own summaries give Littorio 46 war missions, of which 9 were searches for the enemy and 3 were protection of national traffic, and Vittorio Veneto 56 war missions of which 11 were searches for the enemy [S7; S8].

The gunnery and armour-penetration limits the fleet worked to were set out in the Navy's Direttive e norme per l'impiego della Squadra Navale, which is the source of the armour-penetration tables by obliquity for the 381 mm and 152 mm guns, and of the projectile weights preferred over the secondary literature [S1; S2].

The opening-fire ranges used in the penetration tables are defined by band: maximum range for opening fire in exceptional visibility, 26,000 m; in normal visibility, 24,000 m; and the typical battle range band once engaged, 19,000 to 20,000 m [S1].

Fire at low elevations was restricted after 1942 to protect the weather deck and the 152 mm mountings from main-battery blast, by barring elevations between 0 and +2 degrees except over set arcs for each turret [S1].

## Air defence organisation

The anti-aircraft lookout was divided by sector: on Roma, twelve stabilised lookout stations, six a side, each watching a 30 degree spherical sector and reporting by acoustic signal and dial pointer to the command bridge and to the director turret on its side [S19].

Every man's four-figure badge number fixed his station for air defence as well as for action, fire-fighting and abandon ship [S24].

## Damage control

The centrale di galleggiamento, an armoured compartment beneath the conning tower, was where the executive officer in action or under air attack ordered out the fire parties and balanced the ship by flooding chosen compartments on the side away from a hole [S19].

Counter-flooding was used in practice: after the Wellington torpedo hit on Littorio's starboard bow in June 1942, about 1,500 long tons of water entered and the crew counter-flooded 350 long tons to take off the list [S26, p. 402].

## The attack on Taranto, 11 to 12 November 1940

Twenty-one Fairey Swordfish took off from the carrier Illustrious and attacked the Italian fleet in two waves; they scored three hits on Littorio, one on Duilio and one on Conte di Cavour [S32, p. 47; S15].

Lying in the Mar Grande with Cavour were the battleships Littorio, Vittorio Veneto, Caio Duilio, Andrea Doria and Giulio Cesare, the heavy cruisers Zara, Fiume and Gorizia and eight destroyers, with Trieste, Bolzano and four more destroyers in the Mar Piccolo and Pola, Trento, Garibaldi and Abruzzi also present [S15].

The Italian account gives the defences as the anti-aircraft guns of the base and of each ship, 22 large searchlights, 13 aerophones and 87 barrage balloons, of which 60 had been lost in the previous days to persistent wind and bad weather. Torpedo nets had been laid for only 4,200 m, and they hung only 10 m below the surface, which left a gap between the foot of the net and the bottom through which the British could drop torpedoes [S15].

The British lit the first flares over the south-eastern side of the harbour at about 23.00, dropped by Swordfish also carrying bombs, to unsettle the Italian anti-aircraft fire and light the larger ships for the torpedo aircraft coming in from the west. At about 23.15 Cavour was hit by a Swordfish, which was shot down and fell into the sea minutes later. Littorio was torpedoed on the bow to starboard and aft to port. The second wave came in at about 23.50. In all the British dropped eleven torpedoes, hitting Caio Duilio and Littorio a third time. Sixty bombs were dropped, four of which did not explode, among them one that struck Trento. The Italians shot down two aircraft and captured the pilot and observer of the Swordfish that hit Cavour. Losses were 58 dead and nearly 600 wounded. The operation ended at 01.20 [S15].

Damage to Littorio, and the fourth unexploded torpedo found under her keel, are in the hull and armor facet [S28, p. 175].

## Cape Matapan, 26 to 29 March 1941

Vittorio Veneto sailed on 26 March 1941 to attack British convoys to Greece [S32, p. 65]. Germany had pressed for the operation, believing two of the three British Mediterranean Fleet battleships disabled [S32, pp. 64-65]. In the battle Vittorio Veneto engaged British cruisers, then was attacked by torpedo bombers from Formidable; the first wave failed and the second put one torpedo into her and one into the heavy cruiser Pola. She shot down one aircraft, flooded with about 4,000 t of water, got under way again after ten minutes and reached Taranto on 29 March. Repairs lasted until July [S28, p. 174].

The night action off Cape Matapan on 28 March 1941 turned Italian suspicion that the Royal Navy had radar into certainty and restarted the Italian radar programme [S21].

## The sortie of 9 September 1943 and the loss of Roma

After telephone exchanges with Rome and two meetings aboard his ships at La Spezia, Admiral Bergamini ordered the Battle Fleet to sail for La Maddalena, passing west of Corsica as the Anglo-Americans had asked. Most of the significant ships of the Italian fleet left Genoa and La Spezia during the night of 8 to 9 September, which defeated the German plan to capture them [S13].

The force was the 9th Division (Roma, Vittorio Veneto and Italia), the 7th Division (Eugenio di Savoia, Duca d'Aosta, Montecuccoli), the 8th Division (Duca degli Abruzzi, Garibaldi, Attilio Regolo), the 12th and 14th destroyer squadrons and the torpedo boat Libra, preceded by four torpedo boats that had sailed from La Spezia about an hour ahead [S13, note 18].

The temporary German occupation of La Maddalena made Supermarina order the fleet to reverse course and steer for Bona, passing west of Sardinia. During that manoeuvre eight German aircraft of III/KG 100, flying from Istres in France, attacked the formation and hit Roma, under Captain Adone Del Cima, with two FX 1400 radio-controlled bombs. She sank quickly. Admiral Bergamini, the whole Battle Fleet staff, all the senior officers and much of the crew were lost: 1,393 of the 2,021 men aboard. Survivors were picked up by the light cruiser Attilio Regolo, the destroyers Mitragliere, Fuciliere and Carabiniere and the torpedo boats Orsa, Pegaso and Impetuoso, which steered for the Balearics and were interned [S13]. Italia was hit by one of the same bombs and took several hundred tonnes of sea water [S13, note 19].

The damage sequence aboard Roma is in the ship file at ships/roma.md.

The Marina Militare's own account gives the time of the attack as about 16.00 and the sinking as taking 28 minutes from the explosion of a radio-controlled bomb inside the 381 mm magazine of turret B [S12].

## Fuel as an operational constraint

Vittorio Veneto burned 48 t of oil an hour at 28 knots [S14, note 3]. Roma never went on a combat mission because of the fuel shortage, and by the end of 1942 the three ships of this class were the only battleships the Navy could keep ready [S26, pp. 392, 404; S28, p. 178].
