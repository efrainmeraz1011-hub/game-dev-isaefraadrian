# Iowa class: hull and armor

Status: partial (see gaps.md)

Ships: Iowa (BB-61), New Jersey (BB-62), Missouri (BB-63), Wisconsin (BB-64); Illinois (BB-65) and Kentucky (BB-66) were never completed [S33]. Source numbers refer to `sources-01.md`.

## Documents behind this facet

Four yard drawings carry most of the tier 1 numbers here. The docking plan for BB-61 to BB-64 is drawing BB616-0700AA of the Planning Division, Navy Yard New York; it is stamped Preliminary, its title block lists all six hulls, and its reference list ends with a Bureau of Construction and Repair letter of 15 January 1940 [S7]. The damage control diagram 1 (liquid loading) for BB 61-64 was prepared by New York Naval Shipyard for the Bureau of Ships in December 1954 and was declassified under EO 13526 section 3.3 [S8]. Plate 2 of the Iowa booklet of general plans carries revision A of 27 November 1951 (corrected to agree with the ship's installation by San Francisco Naval Shipyard and designated a selected record drawing), revision B of 15 February 1956, and revision C for the 1984 reactivation [S9]. Plate 2 of the Missouri booklet is a finished plan approved 19 April 1948 and corrected to suit ship on 20 January 1950 [S10]. Hull dimensions on the 1950 and 1984 plates match each other and the docking plan [S7] [S9] [S10]; the 1954 diagram gives the only official stability figures found [S8].

That table is the closest thing to a published lines plan for the class that this search found; no block coefficient or prismatic coefficient appears on it (see gaps.md).

### The Panama Canal constraint

Rogers writes that the width of the class was limited by the Panama Canal locks, 110 feet wide, that the ships used a beam of 108-1/6 feet, leaving less than 2 feet of clearance, and that the waterline length of 860 feet came from multiplying that beam by the empirically derived battleship length-to-beam ratio of 7.96 [S25]. The New Jersey museum gives the same picture: a 108 ft 2 in waterline beam that fits the canal "with barely a foot to spare on either side" [S21]. The Iowa crew handbook says the 108 ft 2 in beam "is narrow enough to fit through the 110 foot-wide Panama Canal" [S23]. Naval Gazing states that a foot of beam was added during detail design to keep draft from increasing, "resulting in the 108'6"-wide Iowas being the widest vessels ever to transit the Panama Canal" [S13]. The canal limit also decided the belt's location: the Iowa Class Preservation Association explains that the 19-degree incline needed for protection against 16-inch shells would, on an external belt, have required a wider beam for stability and so precluded canal passage, and that an internal belt was therefore adopted [S22]. Naval Gazing adds that the sloped internal belt was "forced by the treaty limits and the need to get through the Panama Canal" and that the never-built Montanas would have reverted to an external belt [S12].

### Bow, stern, and hull form

Rogers describes the Iowas as "the first capital ships fitted with bulbous bows", credits the bulbous bow concept to Admiral David W. Taylor's hull form tests of 1910 to 1911 at the Washington Navy Yard, and says (citing Breyer 1970) that the bulbous bow reduced drag by 6% at speeds above 25 knots; the class achieved its speed "by lengthening the forecastle and amidships" [S25]. Naval Gazing says the ships had "twin skegs on the inner shafts for hydrodynamic reasons, and a bulbous bow to reduce resistance", and that the "unusual hull form, which concentrated buoyancy amidships, where the heavy weights were" left a lack of buoyancy near the bow that made the ships very wet forward [S13]. A NavSource caption (text credited to Friedman) on a bow-on photograph of New Jersey at her 1945 inclining says the hull form has "a long, narrow entry and then a sudden widening to a parallel midbody", that it "was not altogether successful", and that "the combination of displacement and the Panama Canal limit made the long parallel (constant-beam) section inevitable" [S30]. Friedman at page 324 is the source behind the statement that "the long fine bow and sudden widening of the hull just in front of the foremost turret contributed to the ships being rather wet" [S27, p. 324]. Slade makes the same point from the stability side: the fine lines forward meant that digging the bow in displaced little water and so gave little righting arm, "which shows why the Iowa's were so wet forward" [S17]. Garzke and Dulin (pages 144 to 148) are the source for the statement that the ships have a double bottom that becomes a triple bottom under the armored citadel, with armored skegs around the inboard shafts [S28]. The New Jersey museum describes the inboard propellers as mounted "in the ship's twin-skeg hull" [S21].

The docking plan draws the hull sections at frames 186 to 189 with the skeg around the inboard shaft; its section at Fr. 121 is labelled "showing extreme beam" and dimensions the maximum beam at 108'-3" [S7].

### Subdivision and structure

The New Jersey museum says the frames and other bulkheads give the ship "approximately 1,100 individual compartments" [S21]. The crew handbook says the frames run from the bow to frame 216 at the stern [S23]. Naval Gazing explains one subdivision choice: with 212,000 SHP the Iowas would have needed 64-foot machinery rooms, so a torpedo hit on a bulkhead between two rooms would flood 128 feet; putting engines and boilers in separate rooms cut the floodable length in half [S13]. The docking plan's section at Fr. 121 labels the shell strakes A to P with their plate weights: A 35#, B 35#, C 45#, D 35#, E 45#, F 30#, G 25#, H, J, K, and L 25#, M 60#, N 60#, O 25#, P 25#, with strakes M and N lying either side of the normal waterline [S7]. Budge gives the convention: a 40# plate weighs 40 pounds per square foot and is about an inch thick [S31].

### Barbette diameter

Naval Gazing records that the 16"/50 Mark 2 turret would have needed the barbette diameter increased from 37'3" to 39'4", and that the lighter 16"/50 Mark 7 gun let the ships keep the 37'3" barbette [S13].

## Armor scheme

### Materials

Budge: in the US Navy, cemented armor was Class A; thick homogeneous armor was Class B; thinner homogeneous plate under 4 in (102 mm) for splinter protection was Special Treatment Steel (STS), similar to Class B but with a little hardness given up for the ductility needed to use it as structural steel, and plate was often specified by weight, a 40# plate weighing 40 pounds per square foot and being about an inch thick [S31]. Okun lists BuShips STS among the "extra-strong construction materials" that provided both protection and structure, with about 25% elongation [S32]. The Iowa Class Preservation Association (ICPA) article describes the whole scheme as "essentially the same as that of their predecessors, the four South Dakota class battleships", both with an internal belt adopted "only with reluctance" because an internal belt is costly to install and hard to reach for repair [S22].

The ICPA article also records the supply side: in 1939 the United States could make 75,000,000 tons of steel a year but only 19,000 tons of armor, the armor for BB-61 and BB-62 was already on order when the 2,700-pound projectile replaced the 2,240-pound one, so the armor was not enlarged for the heavier shell, and "small changes were made to the armor of BB63 and newer battleships" [S22].

### Citadel extent

The ICPA article: the armored box or citadel "extends from just forward of Turret 1 to just aft of Turret III"; its top, sides, and ends are armored but "the bottom is not ballistically protected"; turrets, conning tower, and directors are "armored extensions of the citadel" [S22]. The crew handbook puts the ends at frame 50 forward of turret 1 and frame 166 aft of turret 3, with the belts as sides, the 2nd deck as overhead, and the triple hull as bottom [S23].

Naval Gazing: aft of the citadel "the armored deck drops down to the 3rd deck and runs aft to protect the steering gear", 6.2" Class B over the gear itself and 5.6" over the steering gear leads [S12]. ## Torpedo defense system

### Layers

The docking plan's section at Fr. 121 names the four longitudinal bulkheads of the side protection, reading from the shell inward: torpedo bulkhead No. 1, torpedo bulkhead No. 2, armor bulkhead No. 3, and holding bulkhead No. 4; inboard of them it labels longitudinals No. 1, 2, 4, and 5 and docking longitudinal No. 3, and outboard it shows the bilge keel [S7]. The ICPA article describes the same system as four tanks on the outboard side of the hull extending from the 3rd deck to the bottom of the ship, the two outboard tanks liquid loaded with fuel oil or ballast and the two inboard tanks void; the liquid layers deform to absorb the shock and hold most of the fragments, the innermost void contains leakage, and the armor belt is "designed to stop fragments that penetrate the second torpedo bulkhead" [S22]. It gives torpedo bulkheads No. 1 and No. 2 and the inner holding bulkhead as 5/8" steel, and bulkhead No. 3 as 12.1" armor tapering to 1" at the bottom, attached to a 1.5" STS plate [S22]. Naval Gazing says the South Dakota and Iowa classes "had their angled armored belts extended down to the bottom of the ship, forming the third bulkhead of the TDS", and that the initial plan had been "a traditional void-liquid-liquid-void, but it was changed to liquid-liquid-void-void when tests showed that that arrangement would be more effective" [S14]. The New Jersey museum sums it up as "an extension of the armor belt to the triple bottom, an internal bulge, and torpedo bulkheads" [S21]. The crew handbook counts three bulkheads between the outer hull and the interior forming four voids, with bulkhead No. 3 the 19-degree inclined armored belt [S23].

The 1954 diagram shows the arrangement as loaded. On the first platform plan between frames 103 and 111, starboard side, the four wing tanks read from the shell inward B-29F (capacity 160 tons), B-27F (84 tons), B-25V (71 tons), and B-23V (95 tons), then the machinery space B-2-1; the two outer tanks are coloured as fuel oil and the two inner as voids, and the sheet's flooding data give a list of 0.9 degrees for B-29F and 0.3 to 0.4 degrees for each of the others [S8]. The sections at Fr. 99 1/2 and Fr. 123 show the same four sloped layers running from below the third deck to the bottom, with the voids inboard and with side compartments (B-209V, B-210V, B-317V, B-318V, B-319A, B-320A) stacked above them between the third deck and the main deck [S8].

The diagram's liquid loading instructions treat the tanks as protection: "at least one layer of liquid should be maintained throughout the length of the underwater defense system to minimize the penetration of underwater damage"; slack or empty storage and service tanks in the system should be spaced as far apart longitudinally as practicable to limit list after damage; fuel oil storage tanks marked for ballasting are to be filled with salt water "to the 35 foot water line or to the tops, whichever height is lower, as soon as emptied", with not more than two such tanks per side slack at one time; and these limits may be relaxed while fueling at sea [S8].

### The known weakness

Friedman at page 285: the Navy found in caisson tests in 1939 that the initial design was less effective than the North Carolina system "due to the rigidity of the lower armor belt" [S27, p. 285]. Czarnecki: the United States used this variety of system, with a heavy rigid armored bulkhead running to the double bottom, in the South Dakota and Iowa classes, and "came to the conclusion that the heavy bulkhead was too rigid, resulting in a modest down-grading of the system's explosive resistance rating" [S16]. The ICPA article: "additional tests in 1943 showed certain structural defects in the system", and changes made to BB-65 and BB-66 "would have improved system performance by as much as 20 percent" [S22]. Naval Gazing gives the same 20% figure for the modified BB-65 and BB-66 system [S14]. Sumrall at page 132 is the source for what the change was: knuckles along certain bulkheads were eliminated, for an estimated 20% gain [S29, p. 132]. The ICPA article rates the system above North Carolina's, which a Long Lance hit "essentially defeated" at the narrowest part of the system, and expects an Iowa "would have taken lighter damage" [S22].

### Triple bottom

Garzke and Dulin: the double bottom becomes a triple bottom under the armored citadel, with armored skegs around the inboard shafts [S28]. The ICPA article treats the "side protective and triple bottom systems" together as protection against mines, near-miss bombs, and torpedoes, both sized for the 700-pound charge [S22]. The crew handbook: the triple bottom protects against near-miss bombs, mines, and torpedoes exploding under the ship, and forms the floor of the citadel [S23]. The 1954 diagram labels the hold, third bottom, and inner bottom on its profile and carries an inner bottom plan [S8]; the Missouri booklet has separate third bottom and inner bottom plates [S10].

Other hydrostatic figures on the 1954 diagram, for the same 55,649-ton condition: center of flotation 52.4 ft aft of amidships; tons per inch immersion 154; moment to change trim one inch 6,600 ft-tons; moment to change list one degree 7,724 ft-tons [S8]. The sheet's worked counterflooding example takes a 191-ton flooding that lists the ship 0.9 degrees to starboard and corrects it by flooding voids B-72V, B-80V, and B-88V (62, 74, and 61 tons) [S8].

Inclining experiment: New Jersey was inclined at Puget Sound on 24 June 1945 after her refit; BuShips photographs 94333 to 94342 record it, and the caption explains that rails on deck carried the inclining weights and that measuring the change of list as the weights moved let the naval architects calculate displacement and stability [S30]. No numerical result of that experiment was found (see gaps.md).

Two qualitative points from the technical essays: the internal belt leaves the damaged waterline narrower than the undamaged one, "compromising damaged stability" [S12]; and the fine bow gave little righting arm when the bow dug in, which Slade offers as the reason the ships were wet forward [S17].

The Iowa crew handbook prints the same four figures and percentages as the "displacement distribution at 45,500 tons" [S23]. No source gives fuel, stores, ammunition, or equipment groups, and no dated official weight report was found; see gaps.md. Related figures: the New Jersey weight table of 1943 showed a full load displacement of 58,132 tons [S18]; original fuel oil capacity was over 7,250 tons [S21], or 7,621 tons of oil per navypedia [S6].

Design-stage weight changes recorded by Naval Gazing: a faster ship needed more freeboard forward and a deeper belt, "which would push up weight by about 1,375 tons directly"; the 16"/50 Mark 2 turrets would have added about 400 tons per turret and 2,000 tons in all once structure and propulsion were adjusted, taking the sketch to 46,551 tons [S13]. Friedman at page 311 records the fall from 46,551 to 44,560 long tons after the lighter Mark 7 turret and other savings [S27, p. 311].

BuShips war damage reports: the HyperWar index of reports 1 to 63 lists no Iowa-class ship; the battleship reports are Pennsylvania, Nevada, California, and Tennessee (7 December 1941), South Dakota (14 to 15 November 1942), and North Carolina (15 September 1942) [S11]. No official damage report with Iowa-class structural detail was therefore available for this facet.
