# Littorio class: crew and daily life

Status: partial (see gaps.md)

Nothing found in this session describes daily life aboard a Littorio-class ship in particular. The messing, watch and berthing practice below is Regia Marina practice of the period, taken from the Navy's own 1938 regulation on embarkation allowances and from an Italian magazine photo-essay made aboard a warship in June 1941. It is recorded here as navy-wide practice, not as a statement about these four ships.

The civilians in the Italian breakdown are the mess staff: the 1938 regulation provides for borghesi employed in the ships' messes and for their daily wages [S18, art. 5 n. 5; S20].

## Messing system

The Navy's 1938 regulation on embarkation allowances defines the food allowances. They are of three kinds: the ration in cash (razione viveri in contanti), the table allowance (trattamento tavola), and the food-improvement allowance (assegno miglioramento vitto), the last split into ordinary and a special one for solemn occasions [S18, art. 4].

Food allowances belong to the mess a man is assigned to, not to the man. They are paid to the messes under the rules that govern how shipboard messes are formed and run, and they run from the day of embarkation if the man takes at least one meal aboard, otherwise from the next day [S18, art. 6]. The ration in cash goes to everyone aboard who does not draw a ration in kind, and only for days actually spent aboard [S18, art. 7].

The ordinary food-improvement allowance is a cash supplement to the ration, and the money may be spent only on food, flavourings, condiments and drink to round out the daily ration [S18, art. 14]. On solemn occasions and gala days fixed by the discipline regulation, a special allowance of half the normal improvement allowance is paid for each man at the crew's mess [S18, art. 15].

Allowances rose when the ship was abroad: by 25 per cent in the Mediterranean colonies and the Aegean islands, by 50 per cent in colonies outside the Mediterranean or abroad within Europe, and by 100 per cent abroad outside Europe [S18, art. 5]. A collective allowance funded prizes to the messes of the crew's mess deck [S18, art. 4 n. 4].

## The rancio

In the Regia Marina the word rancio meant both the crew's meal and the social unit that ate it. The unit was 8 or 9 men of one department belonging to one watch section, and the ranci were numbered from 1 up [S24].

Space aboard was small against the size of the crew. Benches and tables were introduced early, at least 14 men to a table three and a half metres long, and the tables were brought out for the meal and struck away afterwards, stowed for instance against the deckhead. Bread was issued at 900 grams a day in 1866 and 500 grams in 1917, while the rest of the menu grew richer. The gamella was the soup container the meal was served from and the gamellino the man's own; crockery and cutlery and beakers were of tinned plate or aluminium, and wine was kept in tinned cans. Plate, gamellino and beaker were still in use in 1940, and glass and glazed crockery came only in 1962. Utensils were numbered and personal, washed in sea water at taps at the lavagamelle. From the early twentieth century an officer tasted a meal by a regulated draw, the prova del rancio, to hold the quality up. Officers messed separately. Refrigerators were introduced in Regia Marina ships from 1905, replacing live animals carried for slaughter on deck, though live meat stayed for long passages [S24].

## Berthing

The branda was not a cot but a hammock, a cloth stretched between two ends with rings hooked up at each end, which kept the man level as the ship rolled. It was personal and paid for by the man himself until 1943. On iron ships, once the guns had gone into turrets, hammocks were slung in spaces that had other uses by day, the mess deck among them, and were taken down every morning. The mattress was thin and short, rolled up it had to serve as an emergency float for at least 24 hours, and there was neither pillow nor sheet. Hammocks and mattresses were unhooked and rolled on command in the morning and stowed in places left open so they could be reached in an emergency. Airing them on deck for hours, unrolled, was called the sciorino di brande. From 1943 the hammock cloth and the mattress covers stayed personal issue while the mattress itself became barrack stores [S24].

Washing was by bucket in cold water in the open until the ships of the 1930s brought washbasins for the crew, which let the morning wash come before rather than after the coffee [S24].

## Organisation by number and the daily routine

Every man aboard wore a metal badge with a number, of four figures in the large ships. The number was not a serial but a description of his place. The crew was divided by trade into departments (cannonieri, elettricisti, fuochisti, aggiustatori and so on), each with a figure from 1 up; by working hours into four numbered squads covering the whole crew, each holding about a quarter of every department, which rotated through guard duty and shore leave and through the first and second standby sections; and into the ranci. A man's four figures were his squad, his department, his rancio and himself, and from them followed his action station, his station under war steaming, his air-defence and fire-fighting stations, his washing and cleaning duties, his hammock billet, his locker, his shower and leave turn, his abandon-ship station, which lifejacket to wear and which raft to go to [S24].

The routine photographed aboard a Regia Marina warship in June 1941 ran: 06.10 hammocks rolled and stowed in the bastingaggi; 06.15 showers for the squad that had been ashore the evening before, personal wash for the rest; 06.20 finish dressing at one's locker; 06.30 one man of each rancio draws the coffee from the food warmer; 08.00 colours; after 08.00 the medical officers hold sick call; 08.30 officers take reports and give orders; 10.30 a break in the drills for the barber, and a second man of each rancio draws bread rolls and mortadella for the snack; 11.30 the cooperative shop; 13.15 assembly, the reading of the bulletins, and the tables and benches set up for the meal, with the officer of the watch tasting the dishes; 13.30 the meal; 14.00 the day's man of each rancio washes up at the lavagamelle; 15.00 personal laundry; 18.00 letters before going ashore; 19.00 the library for those not on leave; 20.00 the ship's drama group; 21.00 personal wash, then the Silence sounded on the bugle [S24].

## Sick bay, laundry, barber, ship's store, chapel

The 1941 photo-essay records sick call taken by the medical officers, a barber, a cooperative shop, a library and a drama group aboard [S24]. Nothing specific to the Littorio class was found; see gaps.md.
