The quadruple mounting saved turret and belt weight under the treaty limit, at the price that one hit could disable half the main armament; the two-half arrangement and the armoured bulkhead were the answer to that risk [S1] [S10, pp. 99, 101-102, 106]. The choice of two quadruple turrets forward was settled at the end of October 1934 after three-turret alternatives all came out heavier, needing power cut to 100,000 ch and 2.5 knots less speed [S12, pp. 8-9, 11].

### Loading cycle, magazine to breech

Each turret has two sets of shell rooms and powder magazines. Each shell room and magazine pair sits on the same deck at opposite ends of the barbette; the pairs are stacked on two decks, and each pair feeds the two guns of one half turret. Two dredger hoists lift from the magazines to a handling room below the turret. From the handling room an upper hoist runs to each gun. The upper hoist cage has three levels: the shell rides on the lower level and two powder bags on each of the two upper levels [S1].

The propellant is in quarter charges, unusually heavy. Four are needed per round [S10, pp. 101-109], weighing 228 kg together [S10, pp. 101-109] or 288 kg (635 lbs) of SD21 for the 830 m/s charge [S1]. The bags are rammed two at a time: inertia holds the first pair in place while the second pair is rammed [S1].

Each cradle carries an extension with a "semi-flexible" chain rammer, which allows loading at any angle. Shells are "vigorously rammed" so that the driving band holds them at high elevation. A spanning tray runs in and out automatically to protect the breech threads. Complete ramming takes 13.5 seconds; breech opening and closing take 3.5 seconds each [S1]. Hoist speed limited the rate of fire [S1]. In July 1940 the ammunition handling gear was "barely functional" [S1]; the shell and propellant lifts had never been made to work correctly at Dakar and were rebuilt, with new wiring, at New York in 1943 [S10, pp. 186-187].

### Stowage

104 rounds per gun [S1]; 832 APC in all [S10, pp. 99, 101-102, 106] [S10, pp. 101-109]. Jean Bart as completed in 1950 carried 368 APC and 386 HE; by 1957, 328 APC, 406 HE and 3,000 quarter charges [S1].

### Dispersion and the firing delay

Bombardments in 1945 showed excessive dispersion when both guns of one half turret fired together; the cause was not found aboard [S10, pp. 199-200]. Test firings in late 1947 led to 60 millisecond delays on the outer guns of each turret, which stopped the shells disturbing each other in flight [S10, pp. 201-204]. NavWeaps places the delay coils on the centre guns, fitted 1947 to 1948, and gives the Mers el-Kébir test of May 1948: at 26,500 m, average dispersion fell from 525 m without delay to 300 m with a 0.060 second delay, with guns that had each fired over 200 rounds [S1]. The French article's test list gives 450 m at 16,000 and 25,000 m with the closest guns firing together on 22 June 1945, 270 m firing singly, and 300 m average and 577 m maximum at 25,700 m with the delay against 950 m average and 1,710 m maximum without it on 8 May 1948 [S12, pp. 43-44].

Each gun sits in its own cradle, with electric motor and hydraulic drive and remote power control for training and elevation [S2]. The Model 1936 mounting was designed to load at any angle with hydro-pneumatic chain rammers on cradle extensions, but loading was difficult or impossible above +45 degrees, so the maximum elevation was cut to +75 degrees and the guns probably had to come down to +45 degrees to load [S2]. Three hoists serve each gun: separate pusher hoists for AA and SAPC shells rise to the left of each gun and a cartridge hoist to the right. The hoists rotate with the gunhouse and run down to a handling room fitted with rotating transfer mechanisms that take ammunition from the fixed structure [S2]. The triple-hoist system jammed, the planned rate of fire could not be held, and the slow train and elevation could not track fast aircraft, which is why some mounts were replaced by 100 mm guns in 1939 [S2]. The two amidships mountings would have been 28 m from their magazines and would have needed a horizontal transfer between the armoured decks; they were removed before completion [S2]. Jordan and Dumas: loading trouble above 45 degrees, an unsatisfactory remote-control system, and a turret 50 t heavier than the Model 1930, left it too slow in train and rate of fire against dive bombers [S10, pp. 101-109]; in practice the turrets served only for long-range barrage fire against low torpedo bombers [S10, pp. 99, 106-107].

At Dakar in July 1940 the secondary guns were ready ten days after arrival but had no director able to track aircraft, so they could engage surface targets only [S10, pp. 125-126].

Postwar, Richelieu's turret VII received three Canadian-built monobloc guns and the six guns of turrets V and VI were relined at Ruelle; delay coils went on the centre gun of each mount around 1948; Ward Leonard remote power control was fitted to both ships postwar [S2].

## Heavy anti-aircraft battery: 100 mm/45 Model 1930 in twin mounts Model 1931

The Model 1930 guns came off the old battleship Lorraine in 1939 and replaced the unsuccessful 152 mm DP mounts amidships on Richelieu [S3]. Four of the six twin mounts came from Lorraine and two from the Niolon battery near Marseille [S3] [S12, p. 12]; they were fitted half in mid April and half at the end of May 1940, two on a platform on each side where the 152 mm barbettes had been and one a little higher on the side [S12, p. 12]. The decision to remove turrets III and IV, fit 100 mm guns instead and store the 152 mm turrets for Gascogne was taken in November 1939 [S12, p. 12].

## Light anti-aircraft battery as completed: 37 mm and 13.2 mm

Twelve 37 mm ACAD Model 1935 automatic guns in twin mounts were planned [S12, p. 12] [S10, pp. 109-111]. The Model 1935 was a 70-calibre gun designed by the Artillerie Navale at Ruelle, with a pusher hoist for six-round magazines, a director with a 2 m rangefinder and Sautter-Harlé servos giving remote control in train only; for Richelieu each director was to control two twin mounts. It fired 165 to 172 rounds per minute cyclic and a 0.816 kg HE shell at 825 mps, but barrel life was only 600 rounds and development was unfinished at the surrender; the twin prototype ACAD Model 1936 weighed 17,800 lbs (8,070 kg) and elevated -10 to +85 degrees [S5]. Barrel wear from the high muzzle velocity delayed the gun for the Richelieus; the only example served on the aviso Amiens [S10, pp. 166-170].

In its place Richelieu received the semi-automatic 37 mm CAIL Model 1933 in twin mounts CAD Model 1933: a 300 kg gun 2.007 m long, 30 to 42 rounds per minute theoretical and 15 to 21 practical per barrel, fixed 37 x 278 mm rounds of 2.8 kg with a 0.725 kg HE Model 1925 or incendiary Model 1924 shell at 810 mps, range 7,175 m at 45 degrees and effective range 5,000 m, mount elevation -15 to +80 degrees [S4]. NavWeaps describes them as slow-firing, manually controlled and "not considered to be very potent" [S4]. The four unshielded twin mounts stood by the aft tower in June 1940 [S12, p. 12].

The 13.2 mm/76 Model 1929 Hotchkiss machine gun weighed 30 kg, fired a 51 g bullet at 790 to 800 mps from a 30-round magazine at 450 rounds per minute cyclic and 200 to 250 practical, with a ceiling of 4,200 m; the quad mount CAQ Model 1929 weighed 1.14 tons (1.16 mt) and both twin and quad mounts were hand-worked, -10 to +90 degrees [S6]. Four quads went atop the fore tower and four twins atop the aft tower in June 1940 [S12, p. 12].

## Anti-aircraft battery after the 1943 refit: 40 mm Bofors and 20 mm Oerlikon

The 100 mm guns stayed. The new light battery was fifty-six 40 mm Bofors in fourteen quadruple mounts, each with its own Mk 51 director: two abreast the superfiring turret, two either side of the forward tower, two per side of the aft tower and four on the quarterdeck where the catapults had been. Fifty 20 mm Oerlikons in single and twin mounts went nine on the forecastle aft of the breakwater, four on the superfiring turret, nine on the former hangar and the rest around the towers and shelter deck [S10, pp. 186-188]. Firing the main battery forward on 29 August 1943 destroyed two forecastle 20 mm guns and their ready lockers, so a blast screen was added [S10, pp. 186, 188]. At Car Nicobar on 29 April 1945 the bow 20 mm guns were again damaged by main battery blast when firing nearly dead ahead [S10, pp. 198-199]. At Durban in August 1945 thirteen 20 mm were replaced by four single 40 mm because the lighter gun was ineffective against kamikazes [S10, pp. 199-200]. Before leaving Indochina in December 1945 Richelieu landed her four single 40 mm guns and most of her 20 mm guns with their ammunition [S10, pp. 200-201].

## Jean Bart's emergency battery at Casablanca: 90 mm Model 1926

The 90 mm/50 Model 1926 was "fitted as emergency AA on Jean Bart": a fixed-round gun of 4.707 m firing a 9.51 kg HE shell at 850 mps, 10 to 15 rounds per minute, loading difficult above 60 degrees, range 15,440 m at 45 degrees and ceiling 10,600 m at 80 degrees; the twin mount Model 1930 elevated -5 to +80 degrees and Jean Bart carried five [S7]. The first pair came from the net layer Gladiateur on 18 June 1940 [S10, pp. 152-154]; two new twins were added in April 1942, two more in June 1942, and a fifth in early November 1942 [S10, pp. 155-156]. On 9 November 1942 the 90 mm guns fired on advancing ground troops, and they were still serviceable after the bomb hits of 10 November [S10, pp. 159, 161].

## Torpedoes

No torpedo tubes appear in any source consulted; the design studies of 1927 to 1928 that carried triple tubes were not built [S14, p. 21]. No source states the absence explicitly; see gaps.md.

## Aircraft and catapults

Two catapults offset en echelon, the starboard one further forward, and a 37.5 m hangar aft for two Loire 130 seaplanes with wings folded, a third on the hangar roof and two more ready on the catapults; a large folding crane recovered the aircraft [S10, pp. 99-101, 120-121]. Dumas gives four aircraft, two in the hangar and one on each catapult [S12, p. 13]. Roberts gives four Loire 130 and a steam catapult [S11, p. 260]. The French article gives compressed-air catapults 22 m long throwing a 3,500 kg aircraft at 103 km/h, a crane lifting 4.5 t, and the Loire 130 as a single-engined 720 ch flying boat of 3,500 kg, 210 km/h, three crew, two 7.5 mm guns and two 75 kg bombs [S10, pp. 34-35]. Turret VII stands at frame 68.85 instead of 44.30 on Dunkerque, which moved the hangar forward and lengthened the quarterdeck from 30 to 37 m for the second catapult [S14, p. 23] [S12, p. 27]. Richelieu's three Loire 130 reached Dakar in July 1941 as HDR 1, 2 and 3, based at Bel-Air in the baie de Hann; the first catapult launch was on 7 October 1941 [S10, p. 149]. The aircraft installation was removed at Dakar in January 1943 before the New York refit [S10, p. 150] [S35]. Gascogne was to have an internal hangar with a single catapult and three SNCAC NC.420 floatplanes [S10, p. 172].

- August 1945 changes: 13 x 20 mm out, 4 single 40 mm in [S10, pp. 199-200] against 9 out and 11 in [S35].
- 13.2 mm count in 1940: 24 in six quads [S10, pp. 99, 109-111] against 20 in four quads and two twins [S10, p. 99] [S11, p. 260] and 32 in eight quads planned [S12, p. 12].
- OPfK Mle 1936 weight: 884 kg or 890 kg, both in Jordan and Dumas [S1].
- Quarter-charge weight: four charges 228 kg total [S10, pp. 101-109] against 288 kg SD21 [S1].
- Maximum range: 41,700 m [S1] against 41,500 m theoretical and 37,800 m practical [S10, pp. 101-109].
- 152 mm turret weight: 224 tons (228 mt) [S2] against 227 t [S10, pp. 101-109]; surface range 26,474 m [S2], 26,500 m [S10, pp. 99, 106-107], 24,500 m [S10, pp. 101-109].
- Delay coils: on the outer guns [S10, pp. 201-204] against the centre guns [S1].
- Aircraft: three plus two on catapults [S10, pp. 99-101, 120-121] against four [S12, p. 13] [S11, p. 260]; compressed-air catapults [S10, pp. 34-35] against steam [S11, p. 260].
- Jean Bart dye colour: orange assigned [S1], but "does not appear to have ever been implemented" [S2]; a yellow near-miss splash drenched Augusta's bridge on 10 November 1942 [S22].
