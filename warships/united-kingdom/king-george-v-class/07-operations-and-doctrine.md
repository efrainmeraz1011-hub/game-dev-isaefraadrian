# King George V class: operations and doctrine

Status: partial (see gaps.md)

The doctrine below is Royal Navy general practice from the Admiralty's own Gunnery Pocket Book, B.R. 224/45, unless a ship is named. That book is the governing document a rating or junior officer in these ships would have been taught from, and it names the fuller publications behind it: B.R. 974, the Handbook of Gunnery Organisation, for the whole gunnery organisation of a ship [S68]; the Naval Magazine and Explosives Regulations for magazine and ammunitioning rules [S66], [S68]; B.R. 292, Instructions for the Maintenance of Naval Ordnance and Gun Mountings, and the Register for Non-Transferable Gun Mountings, for upkeep of the heavy mountings [S17]; B.R. 254, the Eyeshooting Pocket Book, for close-range sighting [S68]; and B.R. 980 for the range tables [S6].

## Degrees of readiness

Four degrees of readiness are allowed for in both the low-angle and the high-angle armaments, and a ship may be in different degrees for each [S68]:

| Degree | Low angle | High angle | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1st | complete readiness for action against surface craft and submarines | complete readiness for action against aircraft | [S68] |
| 2nd | "stand by" state for complete readiness | "stand by" state for complete readiness against aircraft | [S68] |
| 3rd | action against surface craft or submarines on a two-watch organisation, sometimes called Defence Stations, the usual state at night | action against aircraft on a two-watch organisation, sometimes called A.A. Defence Stations | [S68] |
| 4th | anti-ship armament cleared away, anti-submarine look-outs stationed, one gun manned | action against aircraft on a four-watch organisation | [S68] |

The first degree is assumed if enemy movements make contact possible at any moment; the second if contact is possible but a limited number of men may be fallen out in turn, with arrangements for officers and men to rest at their quarters; the third by day or night when contact is possible but not imminent, or when a considerable air threat exists over a long period; the fourth when the disposition of friendly forces gives security from surprise, or in harbour [S68]. Manning the anti-aircraft armament fully while in the fourth degree of low-angle readiness means training the extra men out of the low-angle armament [S68].

## Action stations

Warning alarm hooters are fitted throughout the ship and worked from the bridge, sounding a series of hoots followed by the bugle call for Action Stations, Night Action Stations or Repel Aircraft; every man goes to his action station at once, and a man already manning a gun other than his own stays until relieved. If fire must be opened quickly after a sudden alarm, the order "Alarm" is followed by short rings on the fire gong, meaning load if not loaded and come to the Ready [S68]. A photograph of Anson at Scapa Flow in October 1942 catches the moment: action stations sounds and the men jump to it in anti-flash gear and steel helmets [S47].

## Gunnery procedure, target to salvo

Once the captain has pointed out the enemy ship, the fire control team's job is to hit her quickly and keep hitting her [S67]. The control officer leads from the centre rear of the director control tower, with powerful stereoscopic binoculars in front of him and telephones to the captain, the officer in charge of the transmitting station, the spotting group and the rate group. The primary spotting officer heads a spotting group that includes himself in the director control tower, a spotter in 'B' turret and a spotter in the after director; in the transmitting station the group is the officer in charge, the spotting plot operator and the deflection spotting operator. Each spotter has a box with three pushes, OVER, STRADDLE and SHORT, whose lights appear on the spotting plot operator's lamp box. The rate officer heads the rate group [S67]. Ranges from every rangefinder in the ship are cut onto the range plot and the range tuning operator sets the pen in the middle of the marks to tune the table to the best true range [S67].

The main armament could be fired from the director control tower, from 'B' turret as the tertiary control position, or locally from each turret using an Admiralty Fire Control Box Mark IV in the silent cabinet [S7].

What that looked like in a real engagement is set out in Prince of Wales' gunnery narrative of the Denmark Strait action [S14]. The gunnery policy had been signalled the day before: if concentration of fire was required the policy would be G.I.C., and if the ships were spread when the enemy was met they were to be prepared to flank mark as described in H.W.C.O. 26 [S14]. Target designation was by fire control signal: Hood signalled GSB/337/L1, engage the left-hand ship bearing 337 degrees, then GOB1, shift target one ship right, just before opening fire [S14]. Prince of Wales opened fire at 0553, half a minute after Hood, at a true range of 25,000 yards, and ceased at a true range of 14,500 yards [S14].

The spotting sequence is worth setting out because it shows the method in use [S14]. Salvoes 1 and 2 were spread for line; 3 and 4 were fired as a down ladder and fell over; 5 and 6 as a further down ladder, 5 over and 6 straddling. Because the ship's spreads were of the order of 350 yards, it had been decided after her last practice firing to use a 200-yard zigzag rather than a 100-yard one, so salvoes 7 and 8 were fired as a 200-yard zigzag about salvo 6's range. Both fell over; 9 and 10 were a regaining down ladder, 9 straddling and 10 short; 11 and 12 a 200-yard zigzag about salvo 9's range, both short; 13 and 14 a regaining ladder up, 13 straddling and 14 over; 15 and 16 a zigzag about salvo 13's range, both short; 17 and 18 an up regaining ladder whose fall is uncertain. Line was held throughout, though a large line spread appeared temporarily in salvoes 11 and 12, and 17 and 18 are thought to have been ragged because the ship was under full wheel and listing heavily with considerable movement on both elevation and training pointers [S14]. In the second action that evening fire was opened at 30,300 yards with the table tuned to the fore director control tower's rangefinder, using fine inclination spotting rules with each double salvo spread one unit apart; a correction of UP 800 was ordered twice; fire was checked by the cruiser squadron commander when the enemy turned away and there was a danger of forcing him westward [S14].

When the fore director was wooded during the turn away after salvo 18 and the after director could not see through the ship's own smoke, the officer of 'Y' turret went into local control on his own initiative and fired three salvoes, being able to see under the smoke [S14]. That is the local control chain of facet 03 in use.

The report also records the enemy's method as it appeared from Prince of Wales: Bismarck fired the whole of her fore group, 'A' and 'B' turrets, then the whole of her aft group, and towards the end of the morning action guns within a group were seen to ripple as if one or more turrets were in gunlayers' firing [S14].

## High angle and close range air defence

The high angle control team and the mechanics of the HACS table are in facet 04. Doctrine for the close-range weapons is distinct because the engagement is too short for a calculating solution [S68]. Close-range weapons are used against low-flying or diving aircraft at 3,000 yards and below, and control must be simple and if possible under one man [S68]. The maximum effective ranges taught are 1,700 yards for the multiple pom-pom and the Bofors in local control, 1,000 yards for the single 20 mm Oerlikon, 800 yards for the 0.5-inch machine gun and 400 yards for 0.303-inch and 0.30-inch weapons, with fire opened when an approaching target is some 200 or 300 yards outside those figures [S68].

Aiming by eye uses foresight rings calibrated in aim-off speed, the inner ring 100 knots, the next 200 and the third, where fitted, 300; the aimer estimates the target's aim-off speed, points so that the aircraft flies towards the centre of the sight with its nose at the corresponding distance out, and increases the offset as the attack develops [S68]. The speeds taught for judging are 450 knots for a fighter, 350 for a dive-bomber, 350 for low-level bombing and 250 for a torpedo attack [S68].

The book is blunt about the crews and their faults. They are "the commando troops of the ship", unprotected from weather and splinters, stationed in the most exposed positions because those give the best arcs; and in air battles men excited by the action forget to aim accurately and miss astern, forget that friendly fighters are about, and watch events that have nothing to do with them until a fresh attacker dives on them from another direction. Crews are told not to rely on the Air Defence Position to see the enemy or on the bridge to tell them when to open fire [S68].

## Damage control

The object of damage control is to preserve the ship's offensive power, which means first keeping her afloat, second keeping her moving, third keeping her upright, and fourth reducing the risk of fire and smoke [S68]. Subdivision into many small watertight compartments is limited by the size of essential spaces such as boiler rooms and magazines, and by the need to live and move in the ship, so a compromise is struck and the resulting openings are marked with the red and blue colours and the X, Y, Z and O letters set out in facet 05 [S68]. Damage control telephones are painted in three colours that say where they lead: red for fire and repair, green for electric, yellow for pumping and flooding, all running to the nearest damage control base or headquarters [S68]. In cruisers and above, counter-flooding parties form part of the damage control organisation and are controlled from damage control headquarters; a list or trim is corrected by counter-flooding selected compartments, by transferring fuel and water, or by jettisoning topweight [S68].

Magazine flooding and spraying are two different orders with two different costs [S68]. Flooding admits sea water through a flood valve workable from three positions, one just outside the magazine and the others at special flooding positions; it takes about twenty minutes in a cruiser, and once a magazine is flooded the gun and turret it serves are out of action, so the order is normally given only by the captain or the damage control officer and only for a dangerous fire. Spraying plays fire-main water through roses along the magazine deckhead over the cordite charges; it can be ordered by the officer of quarters or the captain of the magazine, and although ammunition supply stops while it runs, it does not put the gun out of action. The primary positions are grouped in locked cabinets, manned in action by an engine room rating, with the handwheels normally pinned; to work a valve from a secondary position the cotter above the wheel is unlocked and removed so that the lower shafting moves while the upper stays still [S68]. These systems are separate from the turret drenching system, which for 15-inch and 14-inch turrets works off the hydraulic pressure main [S66].

### What that looked like in Prince of Wales, 10 December 1941

The single torpedo aft at about 1144 is described in facets 01 and 02 and in the Prince of Wales ship file. The damage control response is doctrine under stress [S24].

The engineer officer of 'B' Engine Room, finding his bulkhead stuffing gland wrecked and water rising faster than the emergency pump, the fire and bilge pumps on bilge suction and finally the main circulator changed from sea to bilge suction could carry it, set the turbines to run under flooded conditions, evacuated the space and was the last man out. Water oozed around the hatch after he dogged it; his experience as damage control officer during the Bismarck encounter months earlier had taught him that the dogs around watertight doors and hatches had to be hammered up really tight, so he had the shipwrights shore the hatch down as well. Eighteen minutes had passed since the torpedo struck [S24].

Where that care was not taken the flooding spread. In 'Y' Action Machinery Room and No. 6 Diesel Dynamo Room the water rose slowly enough for the men to escape, but the flooding then expanded upward and inboard because some of them left watertight hatches open or insufficiently dogged; water came up the port shaft alley trunk and poured onto the Middle Deck, and the doors to that trunk were secured by a damage control party only with much difficulty because the deck already carried a large amount of water [S24]. The hatch from 'Y' Action Machinery Room to No. 12 Breaker Room was still open when a diver inspected the space in 2008, probably because steam had collected in the forward end of the space and made securing it untenable [S24].

Counter-flooding worked and cost what doctrine says it costs. Within six minutes of the hit, counter-flooding the outboard voids of the starboard side protection system had brought the port list down from 11.5 degrees to 9 degrees by 1210, with a trim by the stern of 5 feet (1.5 m) [S24]. The captain later, reluctantly, flooded the outer voids abreast 'B' turret as well when the first phase proved ineffective, knowing that it would reduce the effectiveness of the torpedo defence system there, because reducing the list and trim was essential to let the anti-aircraft battery work [S24]. When a torpedo then struck at that very point, the water already in the voids meant much of the explosion vented into the ship [S24].

One order was given on a false reading. The crews of the magazines serving the two after port 5.25-inch mountings, P3 and P4, believed the noise of the port shaft failing was another torpedo hit in their area and flooded all their powder and shell magazines, which contributed to the 11.5-degree list [S24]. The ship hoisted the "Out of Control" signal at 1210 [S24].

## Shore bombardment

The fire control table carries two scrolls used in bombardment for correcting gun elevation for the height of the target [S67]. No bombardment procedure document was found; what survives for these ships is the record of the shoots themselves. King George V and Howe bombarded Trapani, Favignana and Levanzo on 12 July 1943 in support of the Sicily landings [S1], [S18], [S5]. On the night of 17 to 18 July 1945 King George V carried out a night radar bombardment of the Hitachi works with a United States task unit, firing 267 rounds of 14-inch [S1]; the NavWeaps account of the same mission dates it 14 July and records that she was to fire 10 rounds of HE per gun and missed 15 through flashtight door and interlock trouble [S6]. On 29 July 1945 she bombarded Hamamatsu, opening at 2319 at a range of 20,075 yards and ceasing at 2356, firing 265 rounds of 14-inch for seven hits on the target; it was the last time she fired her main armament in anger [S18], [S1].

## Fleet handling: fuel as the governing constraint

The Bismarck operation turned on fuel more than on gunnery, and the Commander-in-Chief's despatch says so repeatedly [S15]. It was desirable that the cruiser patrols and the heavy ships alike be as nearly complete with fuel as possible when Bismarck was located, and arranging that over the long wait between her being found at Bergen and her sailing was not easy [S15]. Once the chase was running, Repulse was short of fuel and had just enough to fight a short action and then reach Newfoundland; all destroyers had left for Reykjavik to fuel by midnight on 24 May; Suffolk was too short of fuel to steam at the speed needed to come up with Bismarck; Edinburgh had to leave for Londonderry and arrived with 5.5 per cent remaining [S15]. By the evening of 26 May King George V had 1,200 tons, 32 per cent, and Tovey reduced her to 22 knots at 1705 to economise, having already decided that unless the enemy's speed was reduced she should turn for home at midnight to refuel [S15]. He broke off the final action on 27 May with Bismarck still afloat because the shortage had become acute, because he had to allow for a fuel tank being opened by a near miss or a torpedo in an area where U-boats were concentrating and heavy air attack was expected, and because further gunfire would do little to hasten her end; he instructed any ship still carrying torpedoes to use them [S15].

## Night action

The North Cape action of 26 December 1943 was fought in the Arctic winter dark and is described set by set in facet 04. Duke of York opened at 13,000 yards at 1717 under radar control and settled into a stern chase; when the range opened to 16,000 yards at 1808, radar observation of shell splashes became difficult and Fraser signalled his destroyers to report her fall of shot instead [S3]. She checked fire at 1929 after 77 broadsides, with Scharnhorst a blazing wreck sinking by the stern; it was the last time a Royal Navy battleship engaged an enemy battleship [S3]. Survivors were taken aboard; an IWM photograph shows Scharnhorst's survivors under guard on Duke of York's catapult deck at Scapa Flow [S65].

## Landing parties

The Gunnery Pocket Book carries a chapter of notes on boarding parties [S68]. A photograph of Duke of York in northern waters shows a Royal Marine bugler sounding the "Fall In" for a practice landing party, with two 5.25-inch turrets behind him [S48].

## Underway replenishment

Fuelling at sea is covered in facet 02. King George V completed her first refuelling under way on the passage to Sydney in January to February 1945, taking 10 hours by one account and 12 by another [S1], [S18], and in July 1945 became the first Royal Navy warship to refuel at sea abeam of the tanker rather than with the hoses streamed astern [S18].

## Conflicts between sources

- Date of King George V's first bombardment mission against the Hitachi works. The chronology gives the night of 17 to 18 July 1945 and 267 rounds [S1]; the NavWeaps account gives 14 July 1945 and a planned 10 rounds of HE per gun [S6].
- Rounds fired at Hamamatsu. 265 rounds of 14-inch in both accounts, but the chronology and the museum guide differ on nothing else about the shoot [S1], [S18].
