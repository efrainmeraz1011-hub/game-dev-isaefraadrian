## What differed from the class baseline

### Hull built in three pieces

The Brest slipway was too short for the ship. The main hull section of 197 m was built on the slipway; a bow section of 43 m and a stern section of 8 m were built elsewhere and joined on after the launch [S10, pp. 98, 122].

### The state she left Brest in, June 1940

She sailed with 198 quarter charges aboard, powder enough for 49 shots, and with material that had not been installed loaded loose for fitting at Dakar. No floatplane had been embarked, so she could not search for the convoy she was later ordered to meet [S10, pp. 124-126]. Her anti-aircraft outfit was still incomplete [S10, pp. 124-125].

### Torpedo damage and a repair that ran three years

The torpedo hit of 8 July 1940 tore a hole 9.3 by 8.5 m between the propeller shafts and bent the starboard shafts [S10, p. 127]. Dakar had no dock that could take her, so the hull was sealed with a cofferdam, welded plates and cement by 28 February 1941, and the permanent repair waited for the Brooklyn Navy Yard in 1943 [S10, pp. 147-149] [S10, p. 150].

### Main battery barrels

Gun No. 7 was destroyed and No. 8 badly damaged on 24 September 1940, and No. 5 failed afterwards when the loaded rounds were cleared [S1]. During the 1943 refit the three ruined guns were replaced by guns taken out of Jean Bart's turret I [S1] [S10, pp. 186-187]. The cradles were sound and were kept [S10, pp. 186-187].

### The 1943 refit

The refit was worked by three shifts totalling about 2,000 men, around the clock, for five months, and added about 3,000 t to her displacement. The US Navy refused to release its newest radar, so the improvement was concentrated in the anti-aircraft battery and in auxiliary equipment [S10, pp. 125, 181-182, 186]. A US Navy National Museum of Naval Aviation photograph, number 1993.501.073.061, shows her from the air off New York in 1943; the file record argues from the 40 mm and 20 mm guns, the repaired forward turret and the removed aircraft installations that it was taken in September or October 1943 rather than on her arrival in February [S46].

### Radar fitted at Casablanca, late 1944

The Casablanca refit of October 1944 added a US SG-1 search set, a British Type 281B air search set and Type 285P fire control sets, an FV1 jammer and high-frequency direction finding gear [S10, p. 195] [S17, p. 217].

### The dispersion fix

Bombardment firing in 1944 and 1945 showed excessive dispersion of the main battery shells, worst when both guns on one side of a turret fired together [S10, p. 199]. Shooting trials in late 1947 led to 60-millisecond delays in the firing circuits of the outer guns of each turret, which separated the shells enough to stop them disturbing each other in flight [S10, pp. 201-204] [S12, pp. 43, 74].
