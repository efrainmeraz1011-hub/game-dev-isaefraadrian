# Yamato class: crew and daily life

Status: partial (see gaps.md)

The Imperial Japanese Navy's own subsistence regulations were not reached in this session. What follows separates three things that are often blurred: the Japanese **Army** ration scale, which a US War Department manual tabulates in full; the Japanese **Navy**'s calorie and staple standard and Yamato's own galley, which a Japanese naval periodical describes; and the class's complement, which comes from the Yamato Museum and from combinedfleet.com. Nothing here is transferred from one category to another.

The Musashi figure of 22 October 1944 is the only officer-and-rating split found for either ship [S2].

Musashi's crew grew during the war with her anti-aircraft battery. After the April 1944 refit that left her with 115 25 mm guns, she gained two anti-aircraft gunner divisions, No. 6 for the forward batteries and No. 7 for the stern area batteries; combinedfleet does not say how many men that added [S2]. Earlier, in April 1942, her commanding officer Captain Arima formed a skeleton crew of selected officers and about a hundred sailors to man the anti-aircraft guns in an emergency, permanently stationed aboard from 20 April; the permanent crew was transferred to the ship on 7 May 1942 [S2].

Printed with the table: fuel 2.8 oz per man, and water for drinking and cooking 4 to 6 litres (4.2 to 6.3 US quarts) per man [S37, p. 177]. Calorie content as calculated on the same page: standard ration 3,470; special ration 3,540; reserve ration (A) 3,140; reserve ration (B) 3,000 [S37, p. 177].

The manual adds that the weight of the Japanese ration, excluding the emergency ration, is approximately two thirds that of the American ration, or slightly above 4 pounds [S37, pp. 175 to 176], and that the standard field ration weighed about 4 1/8 pounds, the special field ration 3 pounds, reserve ration Class A 2 1/4 pounds and Class B 1 3/4 pounds [S37, p. 177].

The Pacific War Online Encyclopedia, which names the same Handbook among its references, gives the garrison ration as about 28 ounces (800 grams) of rice or a rice and barley mixture meant to prevent beriberi, 7.4 ounces (210 grams) of fresh meat or fish, 21.2 ounces (600 grams) of vegetables and condiments, for roughly 3,000 calories a day [S38]. It gives no Imperial Japanese Navy ration scale [S38].

## The Navy's own standard and Yamato's food

A Japanese naval periodical's account of the class's cooking arrangements gives the naval figures [S39, p. 106]:

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Calories laid down for a rating on normal duty | 2,500 to 2,600 kcal per day |
| Staple | barley rice: 5-bu polished rice in the proportion of 7 to 6 parts, mixed with 3 to 4 parts barley |
| Quantity of rice | 6 go per day, that is 180 ml x 6 |

The same source states that standard quantities were also fixed for the meat, fish and vegetables that made up the side dishes, but does not print them [S39, p. 106].

Meal pattern: breakfast Japanese-style with miso soup and pickles; lunch frequently fish, with beef and pork also served; dinner Japanese, with Western dishes and sometimes Chinese food. Grilled fish was avoided as too labour-intensive, so simmered fish predominated, and tempura, curry and happosai appeared at dinner [S39, p. 106].

One recorded day's menu aboard Yamato, credited by the periodical to research by the writer Hemmi Jun [S39, p. 106]:

| Meal | Dishes |
|---|---|
| Breakfast | miso soup with onion and Chinese cabbage; kombu tsukudani; freshly pickled daikon |
| Lunch | teri-ni of yellowtail; simmered uzura beans; clear soup of yellowtail, onion and Chinese cabbage; pickled aubergine |
| Dinner | ham salad of ham, potato, carrot, onion, green peas and canned mandarin; fruit; pickled cabbage |

Ratings ate from four pieces of tableware, a rice bowl, a soup bowl, a side-dish plate and a teacup, in enamelled ironware or aluminium [S39, p. 106]. The same account contrasts the shipboard ration with civilian rationing in Japan at the time, where the staple ration was 2 go 1 shaku a day, and records Army troops carried aboard Yamato asking the crew in disbelief whether they got meat and fish like that every day [S39, p. 106].

## Galleys and messing

The ratings' galley was on the upper deck amidships, starboard side. It belonged to the Paymaster branch, 22nd Division, under the supervision of the clothing-and-provisions warrant officer, with cooking under the direct command of a senior petty officer of the Paymaster branch [S39, pp. 106 to 107]. The periodical prints a plan of the Yamato-class upper deck amidships with the galley shaded [S39, p. 107]. It fed more than 2,000 men and was far more spacious than that of other warships, as befitted the flagship of the Combined Fleet [S39, pp. 106 to 107].

Refrigeration is likewise given two ways. The naval periodical says meat, fish, ham and vegetables were kept in a refrigerator on the No. 2 hold deck and raised by lift to the upper deck, and that the refrigerator had a volume more than about two and a half times that of Nagato's, which per crew member came to 1.5 times [S39, p. 107]. MAMOR gives the electric freezer-refrigerator a capacity of 223,400 litres [S40].

Provisions, about three months' worth of rice, barley, miso, soy sauce, sugar and canned goods, were held in provision storerooms sited at more than forty places across the upper, middle, lower and lowest decks [S39, p. 107].

Cooks were organised in four watches of about ten men each. The duty watch cooked the rice and the side dishes; the second tidied the provision rooms; the third did the rice washing and preparation; the fourth cleaned the Paymaster division's own living space and mess tables. The duty rotation came round once every four days [S39, p. 107], [S40]. The galley was hot, humid and tile-lined, light burns from escaping steam were a daily occurrence, and a tempura night meant frying continuously for 2,000 men [S39, p. 107]. Because they had to work the newest equipment, Yamato's galley staff were picked from the best men of the Paymaster branch and put through a specialist course before joining the ship [S40].

## Officers versus ratings

Officers and ratings were fed separately, by different staff, in different compartments [S39, p. 107].

- The officers' galley was on the port side, against the ratings' galley to starboard. The wardroom and the 1st gunroom shared one galley; the 2nd gunroom and the warrant officers' mess shared another. Both were in the same compartment, merely separately partitioned [S39, p. 107].
- Officers' food was cooked by hired civilian cooks, not by Paymaster ratings [S39, p. 107], [S40]. The cooks embarked had worked in ryotei and restaurants [S41].
- At mealtimes stewards fetched the food to each mess's pantry, plated it and laid it on tables under pure-white cloths in the wardroom or gunroom; once the members were assembled the meal began. The executive officer sat at the head of the wardroom table; the Captain of the Gunroom did so in the gunroom [S39, p. 107].
- Ratings' food went into food canisters and was issued to the mess-table orderly of each division [S39, p. 107].
- The menu differed by rank: omurice could be eaten only by officers, which made it the aspirational dish of the petty officers [S40].
- On fine days officers sometimes dined on the weather deck at cloth-covered tables with the ship's band playing [S40]. The Commander-in-Chief's meals were Japanese in the style of a ryokan breakfast in the morning, a full Western course at lunch and a Japanese tray at night; at his lunch the band struck up the moment he picked up his utensils and played through the ship for the whole 40-minute lunch period [S41].

## Manufacturing plant aboard

Yamato had equipment able to produce natto, konnyaku, bean sprouts, udon, tofu, ohagi, shiruko, ramune and ice cream [S41]. Using the carbon dioxide produced for firefighting, up to 5,000 bottles of ramune a day could be made [S40]. The ramune machine was not unique to the class; cruisers and larger ships carried them too, and it was in origin an accessory to the CO2 firefighting installation rather than equipment for the crew [S41].

## Air conditioning

The cooling most envied by other ships' crews was confined to the officers' living quarters. It was a luxury that in Japan at the time was found only in some department stores and hospitals, and it was not installed for people in the first place [S41].

The reason was magazine safety and gunnery accuracy. The Navy had suffered repeated ammunition explosions and fires, including the loss of the battleship Mutsu; the class stowed a great quantity of ammunition; and because the condition of the propellant alters a shell's range, muzzle velocity and trajectory, and therefore its accuracy, the magazines had to be held between 7 and 21 degrees C and below 80 per cent humidity. A cooling plant made by Tokyo Shibaura Electric was shipped for the magazines, and the accommodation cooling and the food refrigerators ran off its spare capacity [S41].

Report O-45(N) Figure 15 shows a No. 2 magazine cooling machinery room on the 1st Hold Deck next to the No. 2 main gun magazine, which is the plant that arrangement implies [S11, p. 23].

## The bridge elevator

The bridge stood thirteen storeys above the middle deck and ten above the weather deck, so a lift made by Mitsubishi Electric was fitted, said to carry three to five people. It was manually operated, the operator working the doors and having to accelerate and decelerate to level the car with the landing; it ran fast and rode badly. It was in principle for senior officers, though ammunition carriers for the Type 93 13 mm twin machine gun beside the bridge and the pilots waiting near the first bridge were apparently allowed to use it in an emergency [S41]. The German naval attaché admired the elevator during his visit of 16 July 1943 [S1].

## "Yamato Hotel"

The Army fighting across the theatres, and the crews of other ships sortieing busily from Truk, said behind their backs that Yamato and Musashi, sitting immobile at anchor in every luxury, were the "Yamato Hotel" and the "Musashiya Inn" [S41]. The same account records an Army officer visiting Yamato asking "Navy, does this thing actually move?" [S41]. No source fetched names an individual who coined the phrase.

combinedfleet records the basis for the jibe without attributing the nickname: Yamato remained at Truk as a "hotel" from 29 August 1942 until 8 May 1943, the reasons advanced for her taking no part in the Solomons campaign being shortages of fuel and bombardment ammunition, the restricted, shallow and uncharted waters near Guadalcanal, her slower getaway speed than the Kongo class, and Admiral Yamamoto's reluctance to commit her to battle [S1].

## Living spaces named in the record

Musashi's staff accommodation was rearranged for flagship duty in February and March 1942: the operations room and the telephone and code rooms were extended, and the layout of staff officers' cabins, offices and wardrooms was changed, several of them moving from the port side to the starboard [S2].

Compartments named in the damage narratives include the officers' wardroom, crew space No. 5 on the upper deck, the sickbay examination room and a middle anti-aircraft gun crew shelter on Musashi [S2]; the mess deck, the crews' quarters and crew's spaces forward on Yamato [S1]; and the dispensary, where a bomb killed many of the wounded gathered there along with medical personnel [S24].

## Watch and daily routine

The only routine recorded for a battle day is the Technical Mission's: at 1800 on 6 April 1945 Yamato went to a material readiness condition with one third of the crew at battle stations and the remainder sleeping in the vicinity of their battle stations, and on 7 April reveille and breakfast were completed by 0700 [S12, p. 11]. Musashi's regular routine included two drills a day on setting a complete condition of closure [S12, p. 17]. No general watch bill or peacetime daily routine for either ship was found; see `gaps.md`.

## Conflicts between sources

- Yamato's complement on her last sortie. The Yamato Museum and combinedfleet both give 3,332 [S9], [S1]; the Technical Mission was told about 2,400 officers and men [S12, p. 11].
- Yamato's casualty arithmetic. combinedfleet prints both 3,055 lost of 3,332 and 276 rescued, which sum to 3,331 [S1].
- Musashi's complement. 2,399 crew of 2,417 embarked [S2]; 2,500 sailors [S25].
- Steam rice cookers in Yamato's ratings' galley. Six 6-to kettles [S39, p. 107]; two 6-to cookers [S40].
- Yamato's refrigeration capacity. More than about two and a half times Nagato's by volume, 1.5 times per crew member [S39, p. 107]; 223,400 litres [S40].
