# Bismarck class: armament and ammunition

Status: partial (see gaps.md)

Battery summary for Bismarck as she sailed in May 1941: 8 x 38 cm SK C/34, 12 x 15 cm SK C/28, 16 x 10.5 cm SK C/33, 16 x 3.7 cm SK C/30, 20 x 2 cm, 9 depth charges, 4 Arado Ar 196 (source undated) [S1]. Tirpitz added 8 x 53.3 cm torpedo tubes in two quadruple mounts and grew to 78 x 2 cm by 1944 [S19]. Two sources carry most of the numbers: the KBismarck.com armament page [S4] and the NavWeaps gun pages [S9 to S13], which cite the Kriegsmarine ammunition regulations M.Dv. Nr. 190 and 198 [S9, sources]. The 1941 interrogation report gives what prisoners knew of the drills and spaces [S15]. The AVKS acceptance report of 31 May 1941 records how the hoists behaved under test [S16].

### Turrets: Drh LC/34

Four twin turrets, Anton and Bruno forward at frames 192.55 and 174.35, Cäsar and Dora aft at frames 64.35 and 46.15 [S4]. Each turret sat on a roller track with levels running from the upper deck to the bottom of the ship [S4]. NavWeaps says the turrets rode on ball bearings, not tapered rollers [S9, mount data]. Prisoners gave the arc of fire as 300 degrees and the maximum elevation as 40 to 45 degrees, and the recoil as 1.50 m (prisoner statements, 1941) [S15, §VII(5)(A)]. Each turret had its own electrically driven hydraulic pump; either forward pump could work both forward turrets and either after pump both after turrets, but not across the pairs [S15, §VII(5)(A)]. NavWeaps: main training, auxiliary training, auxiliary elevation, auxiliary hoists and reserve power for some loading gear were electric; the rest hydraulic from two electrically driven pumps per turret; run-out pneumatic; emergency hand elevation provided [S9, mount data]. The AVKS recorded the traversing machinery as the first started by push-button relays, a small dead zone, and current surges of 623 and 1,180 amps on start-up [S16, ¶5]; the elevation gear showed play between rack and barrel of 1.5 mm at the loading position and erosion wear on the rack and pinion, with a proposal to run them in a bath of grease [S16, ¶9]. Heating capacity was 50 kW in turret Anton and 13.5 kW in turret Cäsar; 50 kW was requested for all [S16, ¶33]. Three 10 W loudspeakers per turret did not reach every part of the machinery platform [S16, ¶28]. Gun sights: only one binocular sight per turret during the tests, at the left laying position, with a monocular intended for the right; shear pins of 2.5 mm in the sight drive broke twice under direct laying, mis-aligning barrel and sight by several degrees; 4.2 mm pins as in the 15 cm turrets were requested [S16, ¶4]. Barrel embrasure gaskets and bearing-telescope seals let water into the forward turrets in moderate seas [S16, ¶26]. Turret Anton alone had a rotary cartridge-case ejector drum instead of the flap cover used in the other three turrets and in Scharnhorst and Gneisenau, whose flaps had let water in during the action with Renown on 9 April 1940 [S9, mount data]; the AVKS called the drum an improvement but wanted a hinged housing because its weight-saving cutouts still admitted water [S16, ¶21]. The casing catcher net was too small and was bent by wind and sea at turret Bruno [S16, ¶22]. Smoke evacuation from the turrets was "entirely unsatisfactory" [S16, ¶24].

Turret armour is in 01-hull-and-armor.md. A warning system to stop the 15 cm turrets firing into the 38 cm turrets was installed but not working at the end of the tests [S16, ¶27].

### Turret crew

Prisoners described the gunhouse: a Turmkommandant (lieutenant or sub-lieutenant) with a Turmführer (chief petty officer) under him; laying and training normally from the left gun position with the right as reserve; a telescopic sight on each gun; No. 5 worked the lock (safe/ready), No. 6 the loading cage and spent-case removal down a chute out of the rear of the gunhouse, No. 7 the breech and rammer; three rangefinder numbers at the back worked the 10 m rangefinder; about one officer and 25 ratings in the gunhouse; some 15 men under a gunner's mate in the magazine and a like number in the shell room; about 70 men per turret including artificers (prisoner statements, 1941) [S15, §VII(5)(A)]. Turrets fired locally (Turmabfeuerung) or from the director (Zentralabfeuerung); local firing used a blow-pipe ("Mundabfeuerung"); klaxons signalled firing and lamps showed loaded and fired [S15, §VII(5)(A)]. Sub-calibre practice used a 12.5 cm insert (prisoner statement) [S15, §VII(5)(A)]; NavWeaps records a practice gun (breech and short barrel) mounted between turrets C and D in both ships [S11, mount note 8b].

NavWeaps notes that the base-fused HE shell carried an AP cap, making it closer to a SAPC round, that nose-fused shells had a rod between nose and fuze for oblique impact, and that ballistic caps had a 10 calibre radius [S9, ammunition notes 2a, 3a]. An AA version of the nose-fused shell with the time fuze Z.Z. S/60 nA and no windshield (about 1,740 lbs, 789 kg; maximum AA range 22,000 yards, 20,100 m) was supplied to Tirpitz after 3 April 1944 and fired at the Lancasters of 12 November 1944 from 12,500 yards (11,200 m) [S9, ammunition note 4a]. Prisoners said AP shells were painted blue, HE yellow ("Gefechtsmunition"), practice shells red, and that base fuses (delay) and nose fuses were set by the gunner's mate in the shell room; no tracer or gas shells (prisoner statements, 1941) [S15, §VII(5)(A)].

Outfit: 353 AP, 338 base-fused HE and 313 nose-fused HE, total 1,004 [S4]; NavWeaps gives the design figure as 108 rounds per gun for shell-room stowage, Gröner's 112 to 120 and Whitley's 130 per gun including ready rounds, and the same ammunition list total of 1,004 rounds or about 125 per gun [S9, ammunition note 17a]; a prisoner said 120 to 150 per gun [S15, §VII(5)(A)]. Bismarck fired 93 AP rounds in the Denmark Strait [S4; S17, 24 May, 0620 entry].

### Propellant charges

The charge was in two parts: a fore charge (Vorkartusche) in a silk bag and a main or rear charge (Hauptkartusche, Hülsenkartusche) in a brass case that sealed the breech, rammed together [S9, ammunition note 11a]. Weights:

|---|---|---|
| Fore charge | 99.5 kg (219.3 lbs) | 219.4 lbs (99.5 kg) RP C/32 (840/800 x 16/7) or RP C/38 (810/800 x 17/7), each with an 800 x 175/163 Pulvermittelrohr (central igniter tube) |
| Main charge | 112 kg (246.9 lbs) | 245.8 lbs (111.5 kg) RP C/32 (850/820 x 16/7) or 248.0 lbs (112.5 kg) RP C/38 (850/820 x 17/7), each with an 826 x 92/80 Pulvermittelrohr |
| Cartridge case | not given | brass, 380 x 900 mm, rim 420 mm, 154 lbs (70 kg) |

Prisoners gave the main charge as about 176 lb in a brass case, protected in the magazine by a sheet-iron case, and the fore charge as 90 lb in a linen bag; the propellant was a nitroglycerine and nitrocellulose mix, not flashless; both parts were rammed together by a five-section hydraulic telescopic rammer (prisoner statements, 1941) [S15, §VII(5)(A)]. The AVKS reported that fore charges without conical lid rings banged against the barrel support and broke apart, stopping full-charge firing until modified, and that main-charge lids came loose in the transport system, one lid wedging in the cartridge elevator shaft and dropping to the loading station, which stopped the whole elevator [S16, ¶35].

### Magazines and shell rooms

Shell rooms were below the magazines [S9, mount data]. Prisoners placed the 38 cm shell rooms (Granatkammer) on the middle platform deck and the magazines (Pulverkammer) above them on the upper platform deck, with the loading chamber (Beladeraum) occupying both decks under the turret; for turret Dora the after shell room was in Section III and the forward one in Section V, both on the middle platform deck, the action magazines in Sections III and V on the upper platform deck abaft and before the loading chamber, and four reserve magazines in Section IV outside the loading chamber, two each side, one on the upper and one on the middle platform deck (prisoner statements, 1941) [S15, §VII(5)(A)]. The AVKS describes shell chambers with a reloading transfer head, a "Ringwagen" (ring carriage), overhead trolley with hoist and transport winches, and a windlass for the forward shell stores of turrets A and B; cartridge chambers (Kartuschkammern) in upper and lower tiers with a turntable in turret D's upper chamber that the AVKS replaced by an extended pass-through table; Demag transfer winches installed away from the elevator shafts [S16, ¶15 to ¶18, ¶34]. Magazine cooling, flooding in two minutes, roof spraying from the fire main, ventilation, and the 25 degree C limit are in 07-operations-and-doctrine.md [S15, §VII(12)].

### The loading cycle from magazine to breech

The chain as NavWeaps describes it [S9, mount data]: each gun had a shell cage driven by hydraulic cylinders with rack and pinion drive of a wire drum; on its way up it picked up the charge cage, carrying main and fore charges end to end on a single tray; the hoists came up between the guns; rammers transferred the shell to the loading tray while the charges moved to a waiting cage; after the shell was loaded the waiting cage dropped to the level of the loading tray, a ramp bridged the gap, the charges rolled into the tray and both were rammed together; auxiliary hoists lifted shell and charges one after the other in a vertical position to the rear of each gun into a tiltable cage, from which the main rammer could load them; a manual rammer needing ten to fourteen men backed the whole [S9, mount data]. Five to perhaps ten ready rounds were kept at the back of each turret [S9, mount data]. Prisoners added that the single hydraulic main hoist per gun carried projectile and charges together in a flash-proof cage and that the electric auxiliary hoist per gun sent the shell first and then the charges, the main hoists being between and to the rear of the guns and the auxiliary hoists further aft (prisoner statements, 1941) [S15, §VII(5)(A)]. The loading angle was 2.5 degrees [S4; S9], so every round required the gun to return to that angle; the AVKS listed "loading capability at any elevation" as a development aim [S16, ¶117].

Under stopwatch in port and in calm water the AVKS measured the main elevators at about 23 to 25 shots per minute for all four turrets [S16, ¶14]; NavWeaps states this is the equivalent of 3 rounds per gun per minute [S9, gun characteristics note 3]. Two failures of fundamental relevance occurred: the cartridge elevator parked on a single guide pin at the cartridge loading station and could run past it to seat at the shell loading station, which stopped the elevator for long periods each time; and the by-pass of the hoist and transport winches in the shell chamber did not open properly, so the overhead trolley crashed into the partly lifted stop, the safety bolts having lost their nuts [S16, ¶14, ¶15]. The transfer head was too small in diameter and dented the main-cartridge lids when pushing cartridges out of the elevator into the loading locker [S16, ¶19]. No interlock existed between rammer and loading conveyor, judged desirable but not vital [S16, ¶20]. Receptacles used by the auxiliary elevators to return empty cases to the cartridge chambers lost their bottoms twice [S16, ¶23]. Tests while rolling were not possible [S16, ¶13].

Rate of fire as stated: 2.4 or more rounds per minute per barrel [S4]; 2.3 to 3 rounds per minute, with a note that Krupp documents cite 26 seconds at 4 degrees elevation, that the AVKS hoist figure equates to 3 rounds per gun per minute, and that in the Denmark Strait the ship fired 93 rounds in thirteen minutes, under one round per gun per minute [S9, gun characteristics note 3]; one round per gun per minute (prisoner statements, 1941) [S15, §VII(5)(A)].

The guns were individually sleeved and the centre turret on each side was a command turret with a 6.5 m rangefinder able to pass data to the other 15 cm turrets [S10, mount note 2b]. Ammunition: 622 base-fused HE (Spgr. L/4,5 Bdz), 666 nose-fused HE (Spgr. L/4,6 Kz) and 240 star shells (Lg. L/4,6), 1,288 rounds; no AP listed [S4]. NavWeaps: 105 to 150 rounds per gun for Bismarck, APC rarely carried, outfits of APC, base and nose fused HE with and without tracer, and illumination; an AA time-fuzed version without windshield was used at least once by Tirpitz on 12 November 1944; no fore charge was used [S10, ammunition notes 5a, 11a, 12a]. NavWeaps burster weights: HE L/4,5 base fuze 7.17 lbs (3.253 kg) or 7.32 lbs (3.322 kg); HE L/4,6 nose fuze 9.01 lbs (4.086 kg) or 8.95 lbs (4.060 kg); Illum L/4,3 90.4 lbs (41.0 kg) [S10, ammunition]. Prisoners said the 15 cm turrets trained and elevated electrically, elevated to 45 degrees, had arcs of 150 degrees (120 degrees for the midship turrets), fired a 45 kg shell about 60 cm long with two copper driving bands and a 14 kg charge in a 90 cm brass case, took 9 seconds for both guns, recoiled 50 to 60 cm, carried no rangefinder, and used a 5 cm sub-calibre; projectile and charge came up together in one electric cage with a hand-worked reserve hoist; 175 rounds per gun were carried, the brass cases in polished sheet-iron outer cases removed before hoisting; the crew was one officer and 20 ratings with numbered duties (layer and trainer petty officers, telephone, elevation assistant, breech worker, projectile loader, charge loader, two rammers, case handler); the magazine and shell stowage for each turret were two compartments adjoining the loading chamber: turret No. 1 (S and P) in Section XIV upper platform deck, No. 2 in Section XII upper and middle platform decks, No. 3 in Section IX upper and middle platform decks; six ratings in the loading chamber and six in the magazine under a Feuerwerkersmaat (prisoner statements, 1941) [S15, §VII(5)(B)].

The AVKS praised the layout of three twin turrets a side but criticised the aft mounts, which reached only to the armoured deck and needed a chain conveyor hoist from the ammunition chamber to the armoured deck plus a horizontal transport, consuming manpower; it recorded seven shear connectors broken in full-calibre firing because 3.5 mm pins had been fitted instead of the specified 4.2 mm, putting the turrets out of action; fractures of the main control slider piston in the elevators of the short turrets (II and III) with practice ammunition; chain-hoist latches riveted with aluminium that broke off; wooden guide troughs that would splinter; and turret heating and fume extraction as necessary but lacking [S16, ¶36 to ¶47]. The preferred firing method was full salvoes, with an alternative ranging sequence of 2 shots (turret I), 4 shots (turrets II + III), 2 shots (turret I) [S16, ¶36c].

The C/31d mounting derived from the C/31gE, which put each gun in its own cradle after Spanish Civil War experience, trained by electrically driven hydraulic gear with all-electric elevation and cross-levelling, partial RPC in elevation and full RPC in cross-levelling, and loaded with a continuously running roller above the bore and an idler in the breech block that helped ram the round; the C/37 put both guns in one cradle with closer barrels and a partial shield over the breeches; fuze-setters stood on the outside of the shield on both types; twin mounts had six men on-mount plus passers; the Kriegsmarine numbered the mountings Stb I to IV and Bb I to IV from forward [S11, mount notes 1b to 10b]. NavWeaps suggests that mixing C/31 and C/37 mounts, with different training and elevating characteristics apparently not allowed for in the fire control, may partly explain Bismarck's poor AA showing [S11, mount note 7b]. The AVKS recorded the same objection and asked for early uniformity; it also found the C/37's sight end-position switches so mis-set that a 0 degree setting was really minus 4 degrees and the barrels had to be raised to 28 degrees to regain two-way remote control, making direct fire at sea targets or low aircraft "practically impossible"; the C/37 edge-angle drive jammed until the motor brake and coupling spring were removed; a lateral-lead converter was missing so the C/37 could not fire by slider indicator at aerial targets after a director failure; the platforms of the four aft C/37 mounts had to be enlarged [S16, ¶90 to ¶94]. Prisoners described hand training and elevation assisted by small motors, a sliding-block breech, splinter shields, 70 degree maximum elevation and 10 degree depression, a forward arc of 0 to 130 degrees for the No. 1 mounts, recoil 25 to 30 cm, effective range 6,500 m, burst radius about 50 m, fixed rounds 90 cm long of which the brass case was 65 cm, time fuses graduated 0 to 60 with 20 as a typical setting, and a firing interval of 6 to 7 seconds; each mounting had an electric endless dredger hoist at two speeds and a hand hoist, both delivering to the superstructure deck, with a three-round ready container (Stellbecker) per gun; the crew was twelve under a Geschützführer (range setter, deflection setters, layer, trainer, two fuse setters with headphones from the Rechenstelle, fuse correction setter, two loaders, firing number); magazines were in Section VIII (upper platform deck, S and P), Section XII (middle and lower platform decks, amidships) and Section XIV (middle platform deck, S and P); one crew fired 500 rounds in a series of attacks and over 7,000 rounds were fired by these guns during the cruise (prisoner statements, 1941) [S15, §VII(5)(C)(a)].

The gun was semi-automatic, each round loaded by hand, with a vertically sliding breech; the Dopp LC/30 mount had a third, stabilising axis and six men on-mount plus passers; a mine round with 125 g filler appeared later [S12, description; mount notes]. Prisoners said the 3.7 cm magazines were in Section XIV (upper platform deck, amidships) and Section VIII (same deck, S and P) with no direct hoist to the guns, so supply parties on the main deck unloaded the magazine hoists and reloaded others toward the guns (prisoner statements, 1941) [S15, §VII(5)(C)(b)]. The AVKS grouped the light Flak into four groups of two 3.7 cm twins each, judged the forward No. I twins exposed to the sea and blanked ahead by the turrets, and the No. II twins so hemmed in by the forward rangefinder dome and Flak directors that their transfer to the upper bridge deck had already been approved [S16, ¶102].

Both guns were fully automatic with 20-round magazines, the C/30 prone to jamming; the naval quad mount (unofficially C/35, with C/30 guns, later C/38 guns) was tri-axially stabilised with layer and trainer in front and a cross-roll operator behind, and the gunner fired one pair at a time by foot pedals or both together [S13, description; mount notes 1a to 3a]. The AVKS welcomed the two quads on the forward searchlight deck but called their ammunition supply completely insufficient: the load had to be transferred twice and the elevator, ending on the lower mast deck, was fouled by the forward rangefinder so that only its upper level could be unloaded [S16, ¶102, ¶103]. The general data table gives 20 x 2 cm [S1]; the comparison table in the same page gives 18 x 20 mm for 1941 [S1]; prisoners spoke of about fifty short-range weapons in all (prisoner statements, 1941) [S15, §VII(5)]. Tirpitz carried 20 x 2 cm in 1941 and 78 in 1944 [S19].

## Torpedoes and depth charges

No torpedo tubes were fitted in Bismarck and no mines were carried [S15, §VII(1)]; nine depth charges are listed [S1]. Tirpitz received two quadruple torpedo mounts on the upper deck, one each side amidships in section X, at Gotenhafen in October 1941, giving 8 x 53.3 cm tubes [S19].

## Aircraft

Four Arado Ar 196 seaplanes were carried, with room for six; two in the 120 m³ double hangar under the mainmast and two in single 60 m³ hangars either side of the funnel, wings folded [S7]. The aircraft belonged to 1./Bordfliegergruppe 196 with Luftwaffe crews; the four were Werk.Nr. 052 (T3+IH), 110 (T3+AK), 123 (T3+DL), all A-2, and 150 (T3+MK), an A-3 [S7]. The 32 m double catapult amidships between mainmast and funnel (sections X to XI) extended telescopically to 48 m and could launch to either side; aircraft were recovered by one of the two 12-ton cranes with the ship nearly stopped, and two small cranes under the funnel searchlight platform moved them into the hangars [S7]. Bomb stowage: 40 SC50/SD50 and 54 LC50F bombs on the upper platform deck in section IX (frames 91.3 to 95.55), port [S7, footnote 1]. Ar 196 A-3 data: span 12.4 m, length 11 m, height 4.4 m, empty 2,335 kg, loaded 3,300 kg, two 20 mm MG FF, one forward and two twin 7.92 mm MG, two 50 kg bombs, BMW 132K 960 hp, 320 km/h, 497 nm range, crew two [S7]. Prisoners gave petrol capacity as 800 litres per aircraft in the floats, petrol stowage in Section X on the lower platform deck outboard of the turbine rooms behind empty cells, catapults worked by compressed air with rails forced about 8 m outboard, launches possible at 20 knots with all four airborne within 30 minutes, four pilots, two Luftwaffe observers, two naval observers, six mechanics and an instrument mechanic, six ratings under a petty officer on the catapults, and the aircraft as 5/196 from Cherbourg (prisoner statements, 1941) [S15, §VII(8)]. The aircraft were not flown on the sortie because of the sea state and the need to slow for recovery; the attempt to launch the war diary at about 0500 on 27 May failed when the catapult jammed and the aircraft crashed into the sea [S15, §VII(8), §V(3); S7]. The 14-inch hit through a boat on 24 May damaged the catapult [S23, p. 7].
