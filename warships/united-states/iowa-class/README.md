The two draft figures reconcile in the New Jersey museum's statement that mean draught is 28 ft 11 in at 45,000 tons and 37 ft 9 in at a full displacement of 57,540 tons [S309]. The complement figures do not reconcile and are left side by side. NavSource's class line gives 212,000 shp on General Electric geared turbines and four screws (source undated) [S288]; facet 02 carries the primary figures.

## Design lineage

The only fetched account of the treaty context is the Battleship New Jersey museum history, a tier 3 source [S309]. It states that in April 1937 the allied powers invoked the escalator clause of the Second London Naval Treaty, which allowed them again to build battleships with 16-inch guns; that the first American response was the North Carolina and South Dakota classes at 35,000 to 38,000 tons with nine 16-inch/45 guns and 27 to 28 knots; that design studies for a faster ship began early in 1938 in the Design Division of the Bureau of Construction and Repair; and that the Naval Act of 17 May 1938 permitted capital ships over 35,000 tons to be laid down if the President found the national defense required it, the first three Iowas being built under that authority with the first two ordered in July 1939 [S309]. The same source describes the design as the South Dakota armor scheme applied to a longer hull sized at 108 ft beam and 860 ft waterline length to fit the 110 ft Panama Canal locks, with the main battery changed from 16-inch/45 to 16-inch/50 and displacement over 45,000 tons [S309]. A NavSource caption drawn from Friedman says the design model shows the kinship between this class and the South Dakotas [S288].

The two canceled hulls differed in construction. NavSource states that Illinois and Kentucky were designed for all-welded construction, unlike the riveted-and-welded hulls of the four completed ships [S290], and that their lower and upper armor belts were joined by pin brackets where the first four used key ways [S291].

## What distinguishes the class

- Speed. Every DANFS entry lists 33 knots [S281] [S282] [S283] [S284]. The museum history calls the four Iowas the only battleships fast enough to keep up with the fast carriers [S309].
- Role. War Instructions 1944 states that fast battleships and cruisers are best suited to support carrier operations [S292, para. 1208]. The International Journal of Naval History article on multicarrier formations records that the South Dakota class had shown the value of a fast battleship's antiaircraft batteries with the carriers and that the arrival of the Iowa class produced impressive results in that role [S308].
- Flagship service. New Jersey served as fleet flagship for Spruance (from 4 February 1944) and for Halsey (24 August 1944 to 27 January 1945) [S282] [S298] [S309]; Missouri was fitted out at San Francisco as fleet flagship and carried Halsey from 18 May 1945 through the surrender [S283]; Iowa carried the President in November 1943 and Halsey's flag at the surrender ceremony [S281].
- Surface action. The Truk sweep of 17 February 1944, when Iowa and New Jersey engaged Katori, Maikaze, and Nowaki, was the class's only gun action against enemy warships [S298] [S309].
