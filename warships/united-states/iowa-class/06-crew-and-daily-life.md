## Ship's organization, departments and divisions

New Jersey's wartime cruise book prints the whole structure: nine departments and twenty-seven divisions [S241, p. 58].

|---|---|

| Engineering | A, B, E, M |
| Navigation | N |
| Construction and Repair | R, EX |
| Combat Intelligence | K, L |
| Communications | CR, CS |
| Medical | H |
| Supply | S, SM, SS |
| Chaplains | Catholic and Protestant |

The 1944 Bluejackets' Manual describes the same scheme as the typical organization of a battleship. The commanding officer is responsible for the ship and every act of the crew; the executive officer runs general operation and all routine business clears through him; the officer of the deck, while on watch, assumes the duties of the commanding officer and stands his watch on the bridge under way and on the quarterdeck in port [S242, p. 261]. The gunnery officer heads the gunnery department, whose primary divisions are those of the big guns, numbered 1, 2 and 3, with the secondary batteries in divisions 4 and 5; the manual notes that anti-aircraft batteries had grown until in some cases they amounted to half the gunnery department [S242, p. 261]. The first lieutenant and damage control officer heads construction and repair and directs the R division, which contains the repair shops, the band and ship's service [S242, pp. 261 to 262]. The navigator heads the N division; the communications officer the C division; the engineering officer the M, B, E and A divisions, M for the main engines, B for the boilers, E for electrical, A for auxiliaries including water purifying systems; the supply officer the S division; the senior medical officer the H division, with the dental officer responsible to him [S242, pp. 262 to 263]. The chaplain is responsible to the executive officer for spiritual, educational and moral welfare, maintains the ship's library, produces the ship's newspaper if there is one, and is responsible for entertainment [S242, p. 263]. An X division is often maintained to hold and train drafts of new men before assignment [S242, p. 263].

The 1944 booklet of general plans marks division boundaries on the deck plans themselves. On the main deck and on the second deck the plans carry lettered bands reading "A" Division, "B" Division and "C" Division across the length of the ship, with the A and B boundary near frame 87 and the B and C boundary near frames 135 to 146 [S240, plates 7 and 8].

### Watch, quarter and station bill

On assignment to a division a man's first task is to learn his stations from the Watch, Quarter and Station Bill, one of which is posted on the bulletin board in each division's part of the ship. Beside his name and rate he finds his billet number and his station for general quarters, fire, collision, abandon ship, fire and rescue, plane crash and salvage, gun station, mess and berth. A billet number has four digits: the first the division, the second the section, the third and fourth the billet [S242, p. 274]. Some ships instead issue a station billet slip carrying name, rate, watch number, division and section, part of ship, locker number, mess number, where the man stows his hammock and his bag, and his gun, boat, clear ship for action, battalion, fire, collision and abandon ship stations; the filled slip goes back to the executive officer [S242, pp. 274 to 275].

The 1945 orientation manual describes the documents behind the bill. Before commissioning each ship prepares a Ship's Organization Book, a Battle Bill and a Watch, Quarter and Station Bill. The Organization Book has the force of regulations and contains the fire, collision, abandon ship, fueling, visit and search and towing bills. The Battle Bill lists every station needed to fight the ship, the duties at each, and the succession to command at each [S255, p. 94]. The station bills are drawn from the Battle Bill by division officers and posted in the division's part of the ship; they show name, rate, billet number and locker number, and each man's battle station under conditions 1, 2 and 3, his fire, fire and rescue, collision and abandon ship stations, his special station for getting under way and anchoring, his sea watch and port watch stations and his cleaning station [S255, pp. 94 to 95]. The same manual records that men are generally berthed and assigned training stations adjacent to their battle stations, and that the older practice of berthing men of like rating together was abandoned so that damage to one part of the ship would not remove all the technicians of one branch [S255, p. 94].

Marines have their own compartment A-210-1L on the second deck forward, with a Marine sergeants' stateroom A-210-1L, a Marines' storeroom, a Marines' press shop, a Marines' washroom and the office of the first sergeant of Marines [S240, plate 8].

The museum handbook lists where the racks are concentrated in the preserved ship: main deck port and starboard aft of the wardroom; second deck forward, over 400 racks; second deck amidships port and starboard; third deck forward, over 200 racks; third deck aft, over 400 racks [S245, p. 81]. It states that the wartime enlisted complement of 2,527 sailors and 110 Marines slept in racks stacked three, four and five high [S245, p. 81]. The museum-ship volunteer agrees that some bunks were four and five levels high, that the top bunk was prized because no one above could be seasick, and that in the 1980s refit new three-high racks were fitted with storage under the mattresses where deck height allowed, each with a curtain, a reading light and a small locker for a breathing apparatus [S246]. In the preserved 1980s fit only the bottom rack has the under-mattress "coffin" locker where the deckhead is low, and all three have it where the deck is higher [S250]. Marine berthing in that fit wraps around the barbette of turret 3 under the armored deck and carries extra lockers for field gear [S250].

### Chiefs, officers, the captain and the admiral

Chief petty officers berth aft on the second deck. The 1944 plans put C.P.O. Quarters C-208L, C-209L, C-210-1L and C-210-2L there, with a C.P.O. pantry C-208L, two chiefs' cafeterias labelled C.P.O. Cafeteria (29) in C-209L and C.P.O. Cafeteria (50) in C-208-1L, a C.P.O. washroom, and C.P.O. storerooms C-214A and C-215A [S240, plate 8]. The museum handbook places the chief petty officer quarters on the second deck aft of the crew mess at the stern, holding berthing, a lounge, a CPO mess and a galley, with meals prepared and served by enlisted men [S245, p. 82]. The volunteer adds that the space, the goat locker, is aft of the enlisted mess under the flight deck, that this made it uncomfortable at speed but kept it out of traffic, that no one who is not a chief may enter without permission, and that the chiefs' food usually came from the enlisted mess although they had their own serving space [S248].

Officer country is the main deck and the first superstructure deck. The 1944 plans name wardroom staterooms W.R.S.R. 103 to 116 on the main deck, assigned by title to the supply officer, the navigating officer, the chief engineer, the gunnery officer and others, with the executive officer's cabin, stateroom and washroom at the after end [S240, plate 7]. On the first superstructure deck they run W.R.S.R. 0102 to 0136, with titled rooms for the senior watch officer, the senior aviation officer, the assistant gunnery officer, the assistant engineer and the communication officer, and an officers' bunkroom B-0113L [S240, plate 6]. Further aft on the same level are W.R.S.R. 0137 to 0152, the chaplain's stateroom W.R.S.R. 0138 and a wardroom officers' bunkroom W.R.O. 0141 [S240, plate 6]. Warrant officers have their own block on the second deck: W.O.S.R. 202 to 226, a warrant officers' lounge, a warrant officers' messroom A-201-1L and a warrant officers' pantry A-214-2L [S240, plate 8]. In the preserved ship the staterooms hold between one and four officers each, with a desk and storage for uniforms and papers, and the officers' head doubles as a decontamination station [S249]. The museum handbook places junior officer staterooms on the 01 level port and starboard aft of the captain's in-port cabin, in passageways each fifty frames or 200 feet long, and senior officer staterooms on the main deck forward of the wardroom in an area called the horseshoe [S245, pp. 83 to 84].

The captain has three spaces. The 1944 plans put the captain's cabin, captain's stateroom and captain's pantry B-0102L on the first superstructure deck [S240, plate 6]. The museum handbook places the captain's in-port cabin on the 01 level starboard side aft of turret 2, in three compartments, stateroom, berthing area and head, and records that President Roosevelt used it on passage to and from the Tehran conference in 1943 and that a bathtub was fitted in the head because he could not use a shower; it places one sea cabin on the 04 level aft starboard of the chart house, holding a desk, bed and head, and another on the 08 level [S245, p. 82].

Flag country is the second superstructure deck. The 1944 plans name the admiral's cabin, admiral's stateroom, admiral's pantry B-0204L, the admiral's bath and a lobby, with a guests' cabin and guests' stateroom adjoining, a war plans officer's stateroom W.R.S.R. 0202, staff staterooms W.R.S.R. 0204 to 0210 and further staterooms W.R.S.R. 0203 to 0209 [S240, plate 6].

## Messing and galleys

The general mess is a supply department responsibility. The supply officer of the ship has charge of it, assisted by commissary stewards, cooks, bakers, butchers and jacks-of-the-dust; the crew is divided into messes of about twenty men, each made up so far as practicable of men of the same division and placed in that division's part of the ship, with a petty officer in charge of each mess and a mess cook detailed to it [S242, pp. 269 to 270]. The first lieutenant, not the supply officer, is in charge of the mess deck and of the service of the food; the supply officer's responsibility stops at the galley door; the master at arms supervises the mess cooks on the job [S243, p. 10].

Under the chief commissary steward five men report directly: the jack of the dust, the spud coxswain, the senior baker, the senior butcher and the senior cook [S243, p. 11]. The jack of the dust runs the issue room and is frequently also in charge of the cold storage compartments, taking thermometer readings at least once a day; ordinarily only one, two or three breakouts from the bulk storeroom are made each week [S243, pp. 13 to 14]. The senior butcher cuts and issues all meat from the cold storage compartment to the galley and to the special messes; the spud coxswain works the fruit and vegetable preparation room [S243, pp. 12 to 13]. Night rations may be issued only on the authority of the commanding officer [S243, p. 14].

The 1944 plans show the machinery of this on the second deck of Missouri. The crew's gallery C-201LA and the vegetable preparing room C-201LA sit forward of the barbette of turret 3, with five kettles, coffee kettles and steam table stations 1 and 2 drawn in place [S240, plate 8]. The bakery B-234-1L, the bread room B-235LA with bread trays and a slicing machine, a bakery storeroom B-235LA, and the butcher shop B-236AL with a meat rack, a meat block and a slicer lie further forward on the same deck, beside the provision issue room with its bins and racks [S240, plate 8]. The officers' galley B-202-4L is on the second deck with its own range, ovens, steam kettle and dresser [S240, plate 8], and the wardroom officers' pantry B-102L, cafeteria and lounge with dining tables are on the main deck [S240, plate 7]. Mess attendants have their own messing space on the main deck and their own quarters A-308-1L on the third deck [S240, plates 7 and 10]. Serving is through steam tables at four numbered stations, with plates and cutlery racks, sculleries C-203L and a scullery annex C-206L, dishwashing machines, soiled-dish stations, drinking fountains and two garbage grinder rooms, C-205-2L and C-205-3L [S240, plate 8]. The crew's messes named on that plan are C-201LA, C-205-1L, C-206L, C-207L, C-208-1L, B-231LA and B-237V [S240, plate 8]. A trash burner room B-234-4E with an air lock adjoins the bakery [S240, plate 8].

Meal hours follow the watch. The 1944 in-port routine calls mess gear at 0715 and breakfast at 0800, mess gear at 1145 and dinner at 1200, mess gear at 1745 and supper at 1800 [S242, pp. 264 to 265]; the at-sea routine calls mess gear at 0715 and breakfast at 0730, mess gear at 1145 and dinner at 1200, mess gear at 1745 and supper at 1800 [S242, pp. 265 to 266]. The museum-ship volunteer states that meals were normally served at 0730, 1200 and 1800, times that corresponded to the change of the watch so that men coming off and going on duty could both eat, and that the cooks put out leftovers or sandwiches around midnight as midrats, an informal meal for the men with the midnight watches [S252]. He describes four meals daily on Iowa, breakfast, lunch, dinner and midrats [S246] [S247].

Cafeteria messing replaced the older practice of drawing food to the berthing space: men came from their compartments, queued to fill trays and ate at permanent tables, and berthing compartments could then take fixed bunks instead of hammocks [S252]. On Iowa the two mess lines wrap around the barbette of turret 3 and join on the mess deck, and there were originally two mess decks [S247]. The mess tables, unlike those in older ships, did not have to be struck to let men sling hammocks [S246].

The chiefs eat separately, as shown above, and the wardroom is the commissioned officers' mess and lounge. The executive officer is president of the mess and Navy Regulations prescribes the seating: officers take permanent seats alternately by rank to the right and left of the presiding officer, with the seat opposite him taken by the mess treasurer [S255, p. 24]. Wardroom rules of the day include not sitting down before the presiding officer except at breakfast, asking to be excused if leaving early, never being late, avoiding a cap in the wardroom while shipmates eat, and paying mess bills promptly; gambling or drinking on board is a general court-martial offense; religion, politics and women are by custom not discussed [S255, pp. 23 to 24]. The museum handbook describes the wardroom on the main deck aft of turret 2 as the officers' dining and recreation space, where formal sit-down meals were served by stewards and officers customarily sat in order of seniority [S245, p. 83]. The steward's division prepared and served food in the wardroom and the warrant officers' mess, dressed the tables, kept the officers' staterooms and bunkrooms, carried laundry to the laundry and stood wardroom, galley and pantry watches; its primary battle duty was as stretcher bearers and as clip passers at the guns [S241, p. 91].

The soda fountain is part of ship's service, not of the general mess. On New Jersey its materials were paid for out of the profits of the ship's canteens and soda fountain, an average of 160 gallons of ice cream were made and sold each day, cokes, root beer and frozen candy bars completed the gedunk stand, and the ship had two canteens, one forward and one aft, with canteen and soda fountain sales running about $250,000 a year [S241, p. 92]. On Iowa the soda fountain produced 9,600 gallons of ice cream a month [S245, p. 93]. The ship's service store C-207L is on the second deck aft, with ship's store No. 1 B-236LA and ship's store No. 2 also on the second deck [S240, plate 8].

## Rations and menus

The Navy Ration as printed in the BuSandA Manual and reproduced in the 1945 cook's training course states what should be fed one man in one day. During the war emergency it was not a requirement on all ships or stations but remained the guide for menu planning [S243, p. 74].

Table: the Navy Ration, daily per man, from NAVPERS 10505 [S243, p. 74]

| Item | Allowance |
|---|---|
| Bread | 12 oz soft bread or flour, or 8 oz biscuit |
| Meat | 12 oz preserved, or 14 oz salt or smoked, or 20 oz fresh meat, fish or poultry |
| Vegetables | 12 oz dried, or 18 oz canned, or 44 oz fresh |
| Fruit | 4 oz dried, 10 oz canned, 6 oz preserved, 16 oz fresh, 6 oz canned juice, 1 oz powdered juice, or 6/10 oz concentrated juice |
| Coffee | 2 oz coffee or cocoa, or half an ounce of tea |
| Milk | half a pint fresh, 1 oz powdered, or 4 oz evaporated |
| Butter | 1.6 oz |
| Cereals | 1.6 oz cereals, rice or starch foods |
| Cheese | half an ounce |
| Eggs | 1.2 per day |
| Lard | 1.6 oz lard or substitute |
| Sauces | 2/5 gill oils, sauces or vinegar |
| Sugar | 5 oz |

Ashore the ration is issued in kind against that table; afloat it is a money ration. A ration is the food fed to one man in one day, meaning three meals; each ship may spend only a set amount per ration, and the cost is computed daily by dividing the total cost of all provisions issued that day by the number of men in the mess, carried to five decimal places and rounded to four [S243, p. 54].

The 1944 cook book carries the principles. It states that active men need 3,000 to 4,500 calories a day, and that in a balanced diet roughly 10 to 15 per cent of calories should come from protein, 55 to 70 per cent from carbohydrate and 20 to 30 per cent from fat [S244, p. 3]. Its Food Guide sets a daily target of one pint of milk or its equivalent, one egg, 1 to 2 ounces of butter, one or more servings of meat, fish or fowl, legumes once or twice a week, two or more servings of cereals and bread, two or more servings of fruit including fresh and citrus when possible, and two or more servings of vegetables besides potato, with a green or yellow vegetable each day [S244, p. 6]. Five ounces of American cheddar is reckoned about equal to a quart of milk in calcium, phosphorus and protein [S244, p. 6]. Menus are planned a week in advance so that stores can be ordered and costs figured [S244, p. 4], and the weekly bill of fare is prepared by the chief commissary steward about a week ahead, passed to the supply officer and then to the commanding officer, after which it cannot be changed without his approval [S243, p. 9].

Quantities are given per 100 portions, with a blank column on each recipe for the steward to write in his own mess's figure [S244, p. 14]. Large messes are told to cut the scaled amount: reduce ingredients 5 per cent for a mess of 500 to 1,000 men and 10 per cent for a mess of 1,000 men or more, so that the 7 pounds of beans in the Knickerbocker bean soup recipe for 100 portions becomes 63 pounds rather than 70 for a mess of 1,000 [S244, p. 13]. The cook's manual works the same arithmetic from the other end: Salisbury steak calls for 31 pounds of boneless beef per 100 men, buttered fresh peas for 60 pounds of peas per 100, and salmon salad for 20 No. 1 tall cans per 100 [S243, pp. 50 to 52].

Provisions stowage and daily consumption for Iowa come from the ship's own 1945 crew orientation handout, quoted in the museum handbook. Over 2,500 men consumed 7 tons of food per day costing $1,600; of the seven tons, one and a half tons were fresh, two tons frozen and three and a half tons dry; the storerooms held 100 tons of fresh fruit and vegetables, 84 tons of frozen meats and 650 tons of dry stores, 834 tons in all, enough for 119 days [S245, p. 93]. The museum-ship volunteer repeats these figures and adds that in October 1944 Iowa passed 45 tons of provisions to other ships, including 400 gallons of ice cream [S252]. The cook's manual states that it takes 100 five-ton truck loads to provision one of the new battleships, that dry provisions go to the bulk storeroom, meats to the cold storage compartments and fresh fruit and vegetables to the vegetable lockers, and that the dry provisions reach the galley by way of the storekeeper of the bulk storeroom and the jack of the dust in the issue room [S243, pp. 49 to 50].

A dated menu survives. Iowa's Fourth of July dinner in 1945 cost $1.05 per man [S245, p. 93]. The volunteer prints the menu quantities for that meal: 240 gallons of cream of tomato soup, 240 lb saltines, 2,849 lb roast young tom turkey, 18 lb dehydrated cranberry sauce, 6 lb sage dressing, 1,500 lb whipped potatoes, 480 lb buttered peas, 4,500 hot Parker House rolls, 20 gallons of ripe olives, 20 gallons of sweet pickles, cherry pie from 1,200 lb of cherries cut into 2,800 pieces, 2,800 packages of cigarettes, 2,800 packages of mixed candy, 200 gallons of ice cream and 640 gallons of iced lemonade [S252].

Battle rations at general quarters: no source found; see the gaps file.

## Services

Ship's service on any Navy ship of the period covers the ship's service store, laundry, tailor shop, cobbler shop, barber shop and photography service, all under the ship's service officer, with the nominal profit spent on the welfare of the crew; the ship's store sells toothpaste, shoe polish, stationery, blankets, toothbrushes, combs, pens and ink, clothing, soap, hairbrushes, candy, razors, shaving soap and brushes and nail clippers, for cash only; the post office usually handles all the functions of a civilian post office and sells stamps, money orders, postal savings stamps and war bonds; every Navy ship has a library [S242, pp. 53 to 54].

The 1944 plans place these spaces. The laundry occupies two compartments on the third deck aft, C-430E and C-341E, with an extractor, washing machines, a flatwork ironer, a laundry receiving room C-335EA and a laundry storeroom; the tailor shop C-336L and the cobbler shop C-336L sit forward of them across a passage, with a barber shop C-338L, the lucky bag C-338L, a print shop, the clothing and small stores issue room C-337AL and an athletic gear locker C-336L in the same block [S240, plate 10]. A separate officers' barber shop B-0118L is on the first superstructure deck [S240, plate 6]. The post office B-205L with its writing shelf is on the second deck, as are the band room B-221-1L, the master at arms' storeroom B-230LA, the general workshop B-216E, the carpenter shop B-231LA, the blacksmith, coppersmith and pipe shop B-225EL, the shipfitter, plumber and welders' shop B-225EL and the electrical workshop B-226EL [S240, plate 8]. The library A-103L is on the main deck beside the aviation workshop and the crew's quarters [S240, plate 7].

New Jersey's ship's service division states that its services were free to enlisted men and officers, the materials paid for from canteen and soda fountain profits, that the laundry was one of the best in the fleet, and that the tailors, cobblers and barbers were all experienced men; forty-six men and one officer ran it, and they stood regular watches and manned general quarters and torpedo defense stations as well [S241, p. 92].

The scale of the work on Iowa is given monthly in the 1945 handout: 540,000 pounds of clothing laundered, 3,600 officers' and chiefs' uniforms laundered and pressed, 12,000 pounds of linen washed, 7,400 haircuts turned out in an eight chair barber shop, and 650 pairs of heels and 250 pairs of soles replaced in the cobbler shop [S245, p. 93].

For recreation the manual lists athletics, off-duty education through correspondence and college extension courses, a war orientation programme on every ship and station, and the ship or station library [S242, pp. 53 to 54]. The band is part of the R division [S242, p. 262] and the daily routine sets band call and a band concert at 1230 to 1300 in port and at 1900 to 2000 at sea [S242, pp. 264 to 266]. The chaplain produces the ship's newspaper and runs entertainment [S242, p. 263]; New Jersey carried a Catholic and a Protestant chaplain as a department of their own [S241, p. 58]; the chaplain's office is on the main deck [S240, plate 7] and the chaplain's stateroom is W.R.S.R. 0138 on the first superstructure deck [S240, plate 6]. Smoking in any part of the ship during Divine Services is forbidden [S242, p. 270]. Movies were shown on Iowa from a pool of four films that would often all be played in different locations on the same night, and boxing and letter writing were the other common recreations [S246].

A dedicated chapel compartment: no source found; see the gaps file.

## Medical

Sick bay is on the third deck forward, inside the citadel and just abaft the barbette of turret 2 [S251]. The 1944 plans give its capacity in a dimension note: sick bay A-317-4L holds 2 surgical beds, 48 berths and 34 lockers, and the isolation ward A-339-2L holds 4 berths and 4 lockers [S240, plate 10]. Around them on the same deck are the operating room A-317-1L, the surgical dressing room A-317-5L, a sterilizing room, a scrub room, a battle dressing store room, a sick bay bath A-317-2L with a sitz bath, a diet pantry A-317-3L, a bedpan lavatory, a dispensary, a doctor's office with its own berth and locker, and a forward battle dressing station [S240, plate 10]. The dental office B-201L, a treatment room and an X-ray room A-217-1LA are on the second deck [S240, plate 8]. A first aid station A-103L is on the main deck aft [S240, plate 7] and another on the second superstructure deck [S240, plate 6]. In the preserved ship the wardroom lounge is equipped as an emergency surgery and triage area, with an autoclave on the starboard after bulkhead, operating table lights overhead, and wardroom tables usable as examination tables (museum handbook, 2017) [S245, p. 83].

The medical division's motto, as its own cruise book puts it, is "To keep as many men at as many guns, as many days as possible", and at general quarters it mans first aid, main and auxiliary collecting and battle dressing stations [S241, p. 89]. The 1944 manual states the same duty in the same words: the H division is to keep the maximum number of men at battle stations for the longest period of time [S242, p. 263]. Sick call is sounded at 0830; men may report to the sick bay at any time in case of accident or serious illness; the doctor is the only man aboard who can excuse a man from duty for illness [S242, p. 268].

The at-sea routine differs mainly in that the watch, the wheel and the lookouts are relieved through the night and the working day is fitted around them: relieve the watch at 0400, 0800, 1230, 1600 and 2000 and again at 2400, with the wheel and lookouts relieved at 0200, 0600, 1000, 1400, 1800 and 2200; idlers and the section of the watch sleeping in are called at 0500 with coffee; breakfast is at 0730 with men shifting into the uniform of the day during the meal hour; quarters are at 0915; a band concert runs 1900 to 2000; hammocks at 1930; at 2000 all but standing lights and the lights in officers' quarters and the chief petty officers' mess room are put out; at 2100 the smoking lamp goes out and the chiefs' mess room lights go out; at 2200 the officers' quarters lights go out unless an extension has been granted [S242, pp. 265 to 266].

Morning quarters is usually at 0915, signalled by assembly on the bugle with officers' call five minutes before; divisions muster in their own parts of the ship and the division officer inspects for neatness and cleanliness before reporting to his head of department [S242, pp. 267 to 268]. Engineering department men other than the auxiliary watch usually muster at their stations in working clothes at 0815 and go straight to work, attending only the all-hands drills [S242, p. 268]. Division officers and their petty officers inspect every part of the ship except the double bottoms daily; the first lieutenant inspects daily; the captain and executive inspect the lower decks weekly, usually on Friday, and the upper decks every Saturday; the Articles for the Government of the Navy are read once a month at general muster; the admiral inspects quarterly, one of those being the semi-annual inspection with the battle-efficiency inspection added and one the annual inspection including all drills [S242, pp. 268 to 269]. Liberty is usually granted by watches, alternating on weekdays and alternating 48 hour liberties at weekends, with special liberty granted by the executive officer at his daily mast for requests at 1130 [S242, p. 267].

### Watch rotation and dog watches

Table: the watches, 1944 Bluejackets' Manual [S242, p. 273]

| Watch | Time |
|---|---|
| Mid | 0000 to 0400 |
| Morning | 0400 to 0800 |
| Forenoon | 0800 to 1200 |
| Afternoon | 1200 to 1600 |
| First dog | 1600 to 1800 |
| Second dog | 1800 to 2000 |
| Evening | 2000 to 2400 |

The 1600 to 2000 watch is dogged to let men be relieved to eat the evening meal and to divide the recreation period [S242, p. 273]. One half of each division goes to the starboard watch and the other half to the port watch; on the larger ships each of those is divided in two, so each division has four sections numbered 1 to 4, the odd numbers starboard and the even numbers port [S242, p. 273]. All watches are four hours long; the time off depends on the condition of readiness [S242, p. 275]. Under condition three, attack being remote, the crew stands four hours on and twelve off except for the dog watches; under condition two, four on and four off; under condition one every officer and man is at his general quarters station [S242, p. 275]. The museum handbook prints the same seven watches and adds that the half hours of a watch are marked by striking the bell, the end of a watch being eight bells, and that on the preserved ship the bells run from 0800 to 2200 [S245, pp. 90 to 91].

## Pay and discipline

Base pay for enlisted men was fixed by law and set by the rates of 1942. The 1944 manual prints the grades and the monthly base pay of each [S242, p. 76], and the May 1943 summary of ranks and rates prints the same base pay with the longevity steps [S254].

| Grade | Base pay | Class or rating | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $138.00 | chief petty officers, permanent appointment | [S242, p. 76] [S254] |
| 1A | $126.00 | chief petty officers, acting appointment | [S242, p. 76] [S254] |
| 2 | $114.00 | petty officers first class; stewards first class; cooks first class | [S242, p. 76] [S254] |
| 3 | $96.00 | petty officers second class; stewards and cooks second class; musicians first class | [S242, p. 76] [S254] |
| 4 | $78.00 | petty officers third class; firemen first class; stewards and cooks third class | [S242, p. 76] [S254] |
| 5 | $66.00 | nonrated men first class; firemen second class; musicians second class | [S242, p. 76] [S254] |
| 6 | $54.00 | nonrated men second class; firemen third class | [S242, p. 76] [S254] |

The rating names that map onto those grades are printed in the same summary: chief boatswain's mate, chief turret captain, chief gunner's mate, chief torpedoman's mate and chief quartermaster in grade 1; boatswain's mate first class, turret captain first class, gunner's mate first class, torpedoman's mate first class and quartermaster first class in grade 2; the second class rates in grade 3; and coxswain, gunner's mate third class, torpedoman's mate third class and quartermaster third class in grade 4 [S254]. Pay in the steward's branch is the exception to the general table: cooks and stewards fall in pay grades 1 to 4 and steward's mates in 5 to 7, and there are no petty officers in the branch [S242, p. 76].

Base pay rises 5 per cent after the first three years of service and a further 5 per cent for each three years thereafter, to a maximum of 50 per cent [S242, p. 77]; the table of longevity steps runs from over 3 years to over 30 years, taking grade 1 from $138.00 to $207.00 and grade 7 from $50.00 to $75.00 [S254]. Men not furnished government quarters draw $1.25 a day, with another $1.25 for men of the first three grades whose dependents are prevented by orders from living with them; men not subsisted at government expense draw $1.50 a day [S254]. After one year in the Navy a man draws a $35.00 clothing upkeep allowance, paid in quarterly instalments of $8.75 [S242, p. 77]. Additional pay is given for the Medal of Honor, Distinguished Service Medal, Distinguished Flying Cross or Navy Cross at $2.00 a month, and for special services including crew's messmen, gun captains, gun pointers and gun director pointers, range finder operators, mail clerks, expert riflemen and divers [S242, pp. 76 to 77]. Officer base pay for the same date runs $333.33 a month for commodores and captains in the sixth pay period, $500.00 for rear admirals of the lower half and $666.67 for rear admirals of the upper half and above [S254].

Punishment for minor offenses is assigned by the commanding officer at captain's mast. He may reduce any rating he established, confine for not more than 10 days unless further confinement is needed pending court-martial, award solitary confinement on bread and water for not more than 5 days, take away liberty, award solitary confinement for not more than 7 days, or award extra duty [S242, p. 18]. Beyond that a deck court of one officer may award what a summary court-martial may award except discharge, and may not adjudge confinement or forfeiture of pay beyond 20 days; a summary court-martial of three officers and a recorder may award a bad-conduct discharge, solitary confinement on bread and water or diminished rations for up to 30 days, solitary confinement for up to 30 days, confinement for up to two months, reduction to the next inferior rating, loss of liberty, and extra duty and loss of pay up to three months added to any of those; a general court-martial of five to thirteen officers may award any punishment up to death [S242, pp. 18 to 19]. A man who considers himself oppressed may apply for redress of wrongs in writing through his commanding officer, and is held accountable if the accusation proves false [S242, p. 19].

The place of confinement is on the third deck aft. The 1944 plans name cells No. 1, No. 2, No. 3 and No. 4 in the prison C-338L, with a detention cell adjoining, immediately forward of the laundry and abreast the barber shop [S240, plate 10]. The museum-ship volunteer records that gambling and illicit distilling were the common offenses and that men caught were sent to mast, where the captain could impose extra duty or, occasionally, confinement in the brig on bread and water and loss of rank [S246].

## Crew accounts

Edward Wadington joined Missouri at the New York Navy Yard as a supply striker before commissioning, was aboard for the commissioning and the surrender, and was a second loader on 40 mm quad mount 17 when a kamikaze struck the ship [S253]. The transcript is a 2003 interview and its dates are the speaker's recollection.

On fitting out, before the ship was habitable, he says the crew was quartered ashore: "the Navy had a small hotel over on Flushing Avenue in Brooklyn that we used to walk back and forth every morning and evening" [S253].

Asked whether the food was decent, he answers "Oh, yes. Well, let's put it this way: As decent as it could be", and then explains the monotony: "if you have chicken, frozen chicken, and then they thaw it out and you get it for dinner, and then you get it for dinner again, and then you get it for dinner again, it does seem to wear on you a little bit" [S253].

Asked how the crew entertained itself on long passages, he answers "Define entertainment. What could you do? Work and nothing. Sleep", and of his own rate, "It was just work, that's all" [S253].

On the strain of the kamikaze period he says the general alarm itself became the trigger: "it got so intense for a while there that just anything that come over the loud speakers, you'd jump after a while. And that's just how tense it was. And they changed it. They changed the battle call" [S253].

On habituation to shipboard sleeping, he describes being found after his marriage "lying on the deck, on the floor. I was so used to sleeping on the deck, I couldn't stand the soft bed" [S253].

## Conflicts between sources

- Class complement. DANFS gives Iowa 2,800, New Jersey 1,921, Missouri 1,921 and Wisconsin 2,978 maximum in 1945, none with a stated condition except Wisconsin's [S231] [S232] [S233] [S234]. NavSource prints "Crew 1921" for New Jersey, Missouri and Wisconsin without a date [S235] [S236] [S237]. navypedia prints 1,921 and 1,518 in two undated columns [S238]. The museum handbook resolves the spread for Iowa by labelling 1,921 as the design figure and 2,788 as the wartime figure [S245, p. 21], but no fetched source applies that labelling to the DANFS entries themselves.
- Iowa in 1945. The museum handbook gives 151 officers, 2,527 enlisted and 110 Marines, totalling 2,788 [S245, p. 21]. The museum-ship volunteer gives 151 officers and 2,637 men [S246]. The two agree if his "men" is read as enlisted plus Marines, but neither source says so.
- Missouri and Wisconsin late-service manning. The engineering record gives Missouri 1,510 men in 1988 [S239, p. 12]; DANFS gives Wisconsin 1,515 in 1988 [S234]; navypedia gives 1,518 undated [S238]. These are different ships and possibly different dates and are not reconciled here.
- New Jersey medical department strength. The cruise book text names five medical officers, three dental officers, one Hospital Corps officer, thirty-seven corpsmen and four strikers [S241, p. 89]. A later reading of the same book counts six medical and three dental officers and forty-one men across both groups [S251].
- Meal hours. The 1944 in-port routine puts breakfast at 0800 and the at-sea routine at 0730 [S242, pp. 264 to 266]; the museum-ship volunteer gives 0730, 1200 and 1800 as the normal service times without distinguishing port from sea [S252].
