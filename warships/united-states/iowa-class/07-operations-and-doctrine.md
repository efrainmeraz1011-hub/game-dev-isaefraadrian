## Conditions of readiness

War Instructions 1944 does not print the numbered conditions. It sets out the principles a condition system had to satisfy and then points elsewhere for the conditions themselves. Its basic considerations are that ships are prepared at all times, at sea and in port, to take effective action against any possible attack, even by surprise; that provision is made for adequate rest; that the organization requires the minimum shifting of personnel, with no loss of fighting power in the transition from a lower to a higher condition; that it is flexible enough for minor adjustment without disrupting the ship's routine; and that the labour is divided equitably, to the end that during condition watches certain personnel are assigned to stations other than their regular battle stations [S292, para. 400]. It adds that rigorous standardization of battle bills and condition watch bills for each type of ship is not practicable, that the established conditions include a highest readiness condition with all hands at battle stations plus lesser conditions suited to high or low visibility and to the possibility of surface, air, or submarine attack, and that the currently effective conditions are found in appropriate publications [S292, paras. 401, 402].

The Handbook of Damage Control gives the numbering. Readiness condition one is general quarters, the condition in which battle stations are fully manned. Readiness conditions two and three call for less complete manning of stations. The handbook states that there are many modifications of these conditions on different types of ships or in different task forces, usually indicated by adding a letter to the condition number, and that many ships, destroyers and destroyer escorts among them, have only two conditions: condition one (general quarters) and condition two (war cruising) [S295, §22-8]. It stresses that conditions of readiness pertain to personnel manning their stations, while material conditions refer to states of closure, and that the two are not to be confused [S295, §22-9].

Two of the lettered modifications appear in the fetched sources. The engineering general quarters bill quoted in the handbook refers to "general quarters or condition I-Easy" lasting for an extended period [S295, ch. XXXVIII, part A]. The gunnery manual's standard command 37 is "Secure from General Quarters (Set Condition II/III Watch 1/2/3)", a command from conn repeated by control to secure or to set the condition of readiness designated [S294, appendix C, C1 item 37].

What a reduced condition meant at a fire control station is stated directly for the secondary battery. Ordnance Pamphlet 1719 describes the stand-by condition of Gun Fire Control System Mark 37 as the condition in which targets are expected momentarily but no specific target is yet known, and says the system must be ready for action both when fully manned at general quarters and when the ship is in a reduced condition of readiness, where fewer personnel are available to man it [S293, ch. 2]. The director's complement is six men when fully manned; with Radar Equipment Mark 25 Mod 2 fitted, the director can be placed in the stand-by condition with one operator, the trainer [S293, ch. 2]. One-man control of the director is described as an arrangement that "may be employed under such circumstances as condition 2 or condition 3 watches" [S293, ch. 3]. The pamphlet's title page prints 21 April 1949 and its promulgation letter prints 21 April 1945, so its procedures are labelled here as late-war or early postwar rather than as 1943 practice [S293].

The watch itself is a separate matter from the condition. The Watch Officer's Guide of 1941 lists what an officer obtains before relieving the deck: formation, prescribed distance and interval, order of ships and whether odd or even in column, identity and location of the guide, course by gyro and by standard compass, standard speed and speed being made, reserve speed available, station keeping data, readiness of position buoy, searchlights, signal guns, breakdown flag and recognition signals, limitations on lights and whistle, then the navigational picture [S314, ch. II]. It instructs the relieving officer to relieve promptly but not until thoroughly ready, because as soon as he says "I relieve you" he is responsible [S314, ch. II]. This is the 1941 edition and predates the class in service.

No Iowa-class condition watch bill was found. The 1944 Bluejackets' Manual named in the research brief is on the Internet Archive as an access-restricted item whose text returns an error [S313], and the HyperWar transcription of the Bluejackets' Manual is the 1940 tenth edition with a table of contents and no chapter text [S311]. See `gaps-07.md`.

## General quarters and battle stations

### Going to general quarters

What the general alarm sounded like is not in any source fetched for this facet. The Handbook of Damage Control refers only to the period "after general quarters has been sounded" [S295, §22-11]. See `gaps-07.md`.

The manning itself is documented from the engineering side. The handbook quotes a plan for relieving the watch and going to general quarters from a war cruising condition, offered for information and guidance rather than as a requirement [S295, ch. XXXVIII, part A]. Under it, the engineering section on watch stays on watch and is augmented from the relief sections to man the stations not manned in the cruising condition; the remaining men of the relief sections go to Repair V, to the engineering reserve, and to gunnery department battle stations [S295, ch. XXXVIII, part A]. The cruising watch is a full three-section watch with enough stations manned to supply full power continuously, so each man has three distinct battle stations and which one he mans depends on which watch section is on watch when general quarters sounds [S295, ch. XXXVIII, part A]. For fireroom, engine room, and generator crews the general quarters crews amount to about one and one-half steaming sections, with the remaining one and one-half sections manning Repair V and other relatively cool or inactive stations, which permits a watch-and-watch rotation through a prolonged general quarters [S295, ch. XXXVIII, part A].

Movement through the ship on the way to stations is governed by the closure system. Certain X and Y fittings, among them doors to magazines and handling rooms and accesses to battle stations, are marked with the classification letter in a black circle, and these may be opened without special authority only while proceeding to battle stations after general quarters has been sounded and while proceeding from battle stations after secure has been ordered [S295, §22-11].

### Battle stations by department

A ship's organization for battle is divided into four primary controls: fire control, ship control, engine control, and damage control; carriers add flight control [S295, §23-1]. The stations manned by officers and men are tabulated in the ship's battle bill, whose preparation is the executive officer's responsibility under the commanding officer, guided by fleet and type directives [S295, §23-1].

The gunnery side is organized under the chief fire control officer, called the gunnery officer, with three principal subdivisions: main battery, secondary battery, and anti-aircraft battery. The gunnery officer commands all three but normally performs only supervisory functions over the secondary and anti-aircraft batteries, controlling the main battery directly; in battle he is in immediate communication with the captain and with the officers in charge of the main battery plotting, turret, and spotting stations [S294, §22A7]. A separate air defense officer is detailed to coordinate the defense against air attack so that the gunnery officer can devote his attention to the offensive armament [S294, §22A8]. Medical stations at general quarters on a large ship include battle dressing stations, first-aid stations, repair parties, and decontamination stations, with medical and dental officers and corpsmen at the larger stations and corpsmen alone at the smaller [S295, §25-3]. The damage control stations are listed under "Damage control" below.

### From the call to manned and ready

The gunnery manual prints the sequence as standard commands, laid down for uniformity and intended to be used as a drill manual by every member of the control teams and gun batteries [S294, appendix C, C1]. In order [S294, appendix C, C1, items 1 to 16]:

| Command | What it means |
|---|---|
| "All Stations Report When Manned and Ready" | Given by control immediately on the control circuit being manned [S294, appendix C, C1 item 1] |
| "Ready" | Each turret or mount reports ready when enough men are on station to fire one gun at a reduced rate by local methods; control reports ready to conn as soon as one gun can be fired [S294, appendix C, C1 item 2] |
| "Manned and Ready" | Each station reports when fully ready with enough men to work it by primary methods; control reports to conn when all stations have reported [S294, appendix C, C1 item 3] |
| "Test Circuits" | Test telephones, voice tubes, salvo signals, cease firing, and firing circuits; guns report "circuits OK" or name only the circuits that fail [S294, appendix C, C1 item 4] |
| "Test Transmission" | Test transmission accuracy from director to battery: elevation, train, sight setting, fuze setting, battle order, shell order, shutter order [S294, appendix C, C1 item 5] |
| "Stations" | Ammunition parties prepare to break out ammunition, gun crews take their stations, start motors [S294, appendix C, C1 item 11] |

Damage control runs its own check on the same circuits. Testing begins as soon as a station is manned at general quarters, with damage control sending "All repair parties, damage control, testing" and the repair parties answering in numerical order, "Repair one, aye, aye", "Repair two, aye, aye", and so on until every station has answered; stations that fail to answer are ordered to shift to another means of communication [S295, ch. XXVIII].

### Material conditions of closure

Ships are classified by the number of progressive steps through which they pass in closing up for battle. Two-material-condition ships use baker and able; three-material-condition ships use x-ray, yoke, and zebra, and the larger types are usually three-condition ships [S295, §22-9]. Fittings are classified and marked X, Y, Z, and W so that a condition can be set quickly and accurately, the classification following the three steps of closure [S295, §22-10].

Table: material conditions and the fittings each closes [S295, §22-10]

| Ship type | Condition | Fittings closed |
|---|---|---|
| Three material condition | X-ray | X [S295, §22-10] |
| Three material condition | Yoke | X and Y [S295, §22-10] |
| Three material condition | Zebra | X, Y and Z [S295, §22-10] |
| Two material condition | Baker | X and Y [S295, §22-10] |
| Two material condition | Able | X, Y and Z [S295, §22-10] |
| Either | W fittings | opened or operating [S295, §22-10] |

Zebra and able are the final conditions for battle, giving the maximum resistance to damage consistent with operating the ship offensively; baker, x-ray, and yoke are minimum standards only, and commanding officers may set modified conditions to suit varying war cruising states, a practice the handbook says is more common in large ships [S295, §22-9]. FTP-170B directs that baker or yoke is normally maintained in port or at sea except when manning general quarters stations [S295, §22-9]. A modified zebra or able is one in which certain doors, hatches, and ventilation and flushing fittings are opened or operated to give relief or food to a crew held at battle stations for an abnormal length of time [S295, §22-10]. Circled Z fittings, such as ventilation fittings opened to ventilate interior battle stations periodically and the salt-water cooling cut-outs for the ice machines during protracted general quarters, are never opened during general quarters without special authority from damage control [S295, §22-11].

A three-letter scheme was not yet in force early in the war. The Bureau of Ships report on South Dakota (BB-57) at Guadalcanal records that on the night of 14 to 15 November 1942 the ship was at general quarters with Material Condition Afirm set and the fire main divided into four sections [S296, §6]. South Dakota is a South Dakota-class ship, not an Iowa; the report is used here only as dated evidence of fleet practice.

## Main battery gunnery procedure

The main battery directors, the Mark 8 rangekeepers, the stable verticals, and the four plotting rooms are described in `04-sensors-fire-control-and-communications.md`. <!-- no-claim --> What follows is the procedure that used them, from the fleet gunnery manual.

### The problem and who owns each part of it

The fire control problem begins with the designation of the target and the type of fire and ends when the target is destroyed. It is solved in five steps, all of them carried out continuously and simultaneously by the control party working as a team: positioning the target, determining sight settings, applying sight settings and firing, correcting sight settings, and maintaining correct sight settings [S294, §10B5]. Target position is established by range, bearing, and elevation, obtained by estimate, by repeated measurement, or by computation from previous measurement [S294, §10B5]. In director fire the computed sight settings are not set at the guns but are included in the signals transmitted to them, and the guns are fired by a master key at a remote point [S294, §10B5].

### Detection and target designation

The combat information center is defined as an agency for the collection, evaluation, and distribution of combat information, existing to assist command in planning a course of action and to assist command and fire control in executing it [S294, §22A6]. Among the things it can do for fire control are to designate or suggest suitable targets, coach gun control onto targets, provide an initial solution for the computing mechanisms in plot, control or warn the automatic weapons, and spot fall of shot with respect to the target when a gun control station asks it to [S294, §22A6].

The gunnery officer's broad duties are to inform the captain of the general gunnery situation and the practicability of opening fire; before opening fire with the main battery, to designate the targets, the fire distribution, the directors to be used, the firing method, and the controlling spotters; when directed by the captain, to give the commands "commence firing" and "cease firing"; to supervise the salvo signals and determine the rate of fire; and to keep in touch with the armament subdivisions, redistributing as casualties require [S294, §22A7]. For a main battery target there is likely to be a specific assignment for every ship of the battle line in the form of a fire distribution signal, so the designation may originate outside the ship and reach the gunnery officer through the captain; if more than one target is assigned, the captain specifies how fire is divided [S294, §22A7].

The designating command names the target by type and bearing, in the form "Target is Destroyer (Aircraft) (Submarine) Bearing ...". Conn must designate accurately by type and bearing, and control designates the target to the guns in the same way so that a turret can go intelligently to local control if it has to [S294, appendix C, C1 item 15].

### Director acquisition and tracking

The director trainer and pointer report to control when on target, using the expression "On Target" [S294, appendix C, C1 item 15]. The command "Start Tracking" puts the control party on the designated target, and after it the whole battery is in condition to open fire at a moment's notice by primary methods [S294, appendix C, C1 item 16].

The secondary battery pamphlet divides the engagement into three phases and marks the boundaries precisely. Target acquisition commences when a target is designated to the director and ends when the target is centered in the director scopes and the control officer informs plot that the director is "ON TARGET". Tracking is the time the director stays on target, and the fire control problem is solved during it. Firing starts with the "COMMENCE FIRING" order and ends with the "CEASE FIRING" order, with the tracking operations continuing throughout [S293, ch. 3].

Control method is named by command. "Director (One) (Two) Control" places the designated director in control, "Divide Control" places each director in control of its own battery, and "Local Control" and "Telescope Control" are given by the gun captain when a casualty makes them necessary [S294, appendix C, C2 items 1 to 5].

### The order to open fire

"Commence Firing" is a command from control to load and open fire, given after the trainer reports "On Target" [S294, appendix C, C2 item 30]. The separate command "Load" trips the salvo latches, opens the breech, and loads with the ammunition previously specified; it may be omitted, in which case loading happens at "Commence Firing" [S294, appendix C, C2 item 29]. "Shoot" fires a salvo or split salvo and is used when the control officer wants to control the rate of fire personally [S294, appendix C, C2 item 33].

The type of fire is ordered before or with it [S294, appendix B, B9]:

| Type | Definition |
|---|---|
| Salvo | Two or more shots fired at the same target at the same time or within a short interval such as the duration of a firing signal, on one salvo signal [S294, appendix B, B9 item 1; appendix B, B12 and §21A1] |
| Split salvo | Less than the full number of guns in a multiple gun mount fires on one salvo signal, to reduce dispersion, conserve ammunition, or shorten the salvo interval [S294, appendix B, B9 item 2] |
| Partial salvo | Less than the full number of turrets fires on one salvo signal, used for ranging shots or ladders, to conserve ammunition, or to shorten the salvo interval [S294, appendix B, B9 item 3] |
| Continuous turret fire | Each turret fires as soon as all its guns are ready, without regard for the other turrets [S294, appendix B, B9 item 5] |
| Slow fire | Fire deliberately delayed to allow corrections or spots to be applied, or to conserve ammunition [S294, appendix B, B9 item 6] |
| Rapid fire | Fire in which no check fire for applying corrections is used [S294, appendix B, B9 item 7] |

The manual notes that the salvo interval should be adjusted so that the guns do not fire at the same instant as, or just before, a salvo lands or detonates, to avoid blinding the spotters and director operators [S294, appendix B, B9 item 1].

### Spotting and salvo correction

The spotter's primary function is to correct the range and deflection errors of the mean point of impact to bring the shots onto the target, that is, to establish the hitting gun range and deflection, on the basis of his own observation [S294, §21B1]. Before firing he searches for and locates the enemy, describes the enemy force by general bearing line, number of ships, and deployment, and estimates the values needed to position and track the prescribed target; during firing he spots the fall of shot, reports changes in target angle and speed, reports changes in range if necessary, and keeps control informed of the tactical situation if required [S294, §21B1]. His detailed duties and spotting procedures are prescribed in the ship's battle bill and in fleet doctrinal publications, which the manual does not reproduce [S294, §21B2].

The terms he works in are defined. The mean point of impact of a salvo is the geometrical center of the points of impact of its several shots, excluding wild shots; a wild shot is one whose dispersion is abnormal compared with the other shots of the same salvo, in range or in deflection; the pattern in range is the distance parallel to the line of fire between the shot falling farthest beyond and the shot falling farthest short of the mean point of impact, excluding wild shots, and the pattern in deflection is the corresponding distance at right angles to the line of fire [S294, §21A1].

Spotting methods are named by command, "Spot Direct" and "Spot Halving" among them [S294, appendix C, C2 items 18, 19]. The direct method spots splashes directly to the target and is the most desirable but the most limited: it requires a spotting height of 120 feet for ranges up to 15,000 yards, the splash relatively close to the target, and a fairly accurate rangekeeper setup, which confines it to main battery fire at relatively short ranges [S294, §21B7]. Deflection spots can be made with equal accuracy at any visible distance, so when air spot is available and the plane spots in range while the ship spots in deflection, the direct method can be used by the main battery at any range at which part of the splash is visible from the ship [S294, §21B7]. Air spotters cannot spot accurately in deflection, because that would require a line of sight containing both the firing ship and the target [S294, §21B7]. The bracket or halving method is used at long range by the main battery when no air spot is available, on the reasoning that at great distance a splash cannot be called over or short unless it lines up with the target [S294, §21B8]. In halving, when the first salvo is all over or all short the spot is made large enough to cross the target, and succeeding spots are half the last one and applied in the opposite direction until the target is inside the pattern [S294, appendix B, B13 item 2].

Ranging shots are ordered as ladders and brackets. In an up ladder the first salvo is fired short of the best range and successive salvos increase until the target is located or crossed; in a down ladder the first salvo is over and successive salvos decrease; an initial ladder is a definite number of salvos greater than two, fired with large increments to locate the target within its limits; in a bracket two salvos are fired with ranges or deflections set either side of the best value to include the target between the two sets of splashes; barrage places the shots ahead of a fast-opening or closing target and holds the setting until the target passes through the zone of fire [S294, appendix B, B12]. The commands are "Down (Up) Ladder ... Hundred Yard Steps", "Bracket ... Hundred Yard Steps", "Ladder Out ... Yard Steps", "Ladder In ... Yard Steps", and "Rocking Ladder ... Yard Spread ... Yard Steps", the last three given by the controlling spotter to the control party [S294, appendix C, C2 items 15, 16, 21, 22, 23].

### Check fire and securing

"Check Fire" discontinues fire, probably temporarily; all other functions of the control party and gun crew continue, and the cease firing gong or howler is sounded [S294, appendix C, C2 item 31]. "Resume Fire" continues fire after a check fire [S294, appendix C, C2 item 32]. "Cease Firing" stops fire preparatory to shifting the method of fire or the target, is not a command signifying the end of action, sounds the cease firing gong, and requires gun captains to report to control the rounds fired and the condition of their guns [S294, appendix C, C2 item 34]. "Cease Tracking" resumes the fully ready condition with all stations alert, and "At Ease" lets men relax at their stations [S294, appendix C, C2 items 35, 36]. In a serious casualty or in doubt as to its seriousness, "Silence" freezes every member of the gun crew, director crew, and ammunition party in place without noise or confusion until further orders or the command "Carry On" [S294, appendix C, C3 item 1].

## Surface action doctrine

### The fast battleship in a major action

War Instructions defines a major action as a day battle in which all types of ships are engaged in large numbers, and states the assumptions governing the chapter: carrier air groups operate with the force, giving it greater and longer range offensive power; land-based aircraft are used to the maximum extent possible; aircraft attack before surface vessels come into contact; damage inflicted by air attack on either side may require a change of battle plan; fast and slow battleships do not ordinarily operate in the same battle line; and effective use of radar and aircraft eliminates the element of surprise [S292, ch. 12 preamble].

The normal task groups for a major action are the flagship of the officer in tactical command with its screen, which may be assigned to another task group for battle; the battle line, composed of battleships with their observation plane squadrons and screen and, if assigned, battle line carriers with their squadrons and screen, the battle line being composed of the heaviest ships available if no battleships are present; the detached wing, if employed, composed of fast battleships and large or heavy cruisers with their aircraft and screen; the center, the right flank, and the left flank, all of cruisers with their aircraft, attack destroyers, and light minelayers; the submarines; the air group of carriers with their aircraft and screen; and the train [S292, paras. 1200, 1201]. A detached wing may be employed with advantage against an enemy battle line and fast detached wing whose total strength is inferior to the battle line, and a detached wing of adequate strength is organized to defend a large train or convoy against enemy battle cruisers or other fast forces [S292, para. 1203].

During the approach the commander battle line orders a formation from which deployment can be made rapidly and maintains the line of bearing of the battleship formation accordingly [S292, para. 1226]. As soon as sufficient information of the enemy is available he issues the instructions for the fire distribution of the battle line [S292, para. 1227]. The screen of the battle line is normally maintained during the approach [S292, para. 1230]. When radar or sight contact is made with the enemy battle line, light forces are kept clear of destructive gun range and out of the area between the battle lines [S292, paras. 1235, and 1224 to 1240 generally].

The type doctrine that sat below these instructions is listed by title but not transcribed: Current Tactical Orders and Doctrine U.S. Fleet USF 10A, Current Tactics Battleships US Fleet USF 16, Current Doctrine Battleships US Fleet USF 17, Standard Cruising Instructions for Carrier Task Forces (Pacific Fleet Confidential Letter 1CL-43), Gunnery Instructions FTP 134, and Damage Control Procedure [S320]. None of these is online. See `gaps-07.md`.

### Night action

War Instructions opens its night action chapter with the statement that radar changed previous concepts of night action, and that by exploiting radar in its various applications, together with radar intercept receivers, night engagements are sought and accepted by small task forces even against numerically superior enemy forces; such a task force may include several heavy units, but its action is not a major action in the sense of chapter 12 [S292, para. 800]. Surprise and concentration combined through radar frustrate the enemy on contact, and superior fire power then destroys him [S292, para. 801]. Low visibility is essential to surprise by radar, and the radar advantage diminishes as visibility increases [S292, para. 802].

Illumination is controlled from the top. No illumination is used unless prescribed by the officer in tactical command or other competent authority [S292, para. 818]. Searchlights, if used, are turned on only when trained on the target and ready to open fire, and sweeping wide arcs to find a target is avoided, though once a target is picked up the lights are swept short distances each side to find other enemy vessels [S292, paras. 822, 823]. Both searchlights and star shells are visible from considerable distances [S292, para. 819]. The star shell and searchlight commands are printed in the gunnery manual: "Prepare to Illuminate Bearing ... by Searchlight and (or) Star shell" from conn to control, "Lay for Star-shell Spread to Starboard (Port)", "Illuminate", "Strike Arcs", "Break Arc", and "Open (close) Shutters" [S294, appendix C, C2 items 24 to 26, 38 to 40].

### The one clear surface action: Truk, February 1944

Task Group 58.3 during the Truk raid consisted of Bunker Hill, Monterey, Cowpens, and the battleships North Carolina, Iowa, New Jersey with Vice Admiral Spruance embarked, Massachusetts, South Dakota, and Alabama, plus two heavy cruisers [S298]. On 17 February 1944 Spruance led an around-the-atoll sweep as Task Group 50.9, consisting of New Jersey and Iowa, the heavy cruisers Minneapolis and New Orleans, and four destroyers, covered by combat air patrol from the light carrier Cowpens, to catch ships escaping Truk [S298].

The group caught the light cruiser Katori, which had already been hit by one torpedo and as many as seven bombs. Spruance wanted a surface engagement, so Mitscher waved off further air attacks, and Spruance directed Minneapolis, New Orleans, and two destroyers to engage. The destroyers Bradford and Burns fired several salvos of torpedoes, all of which missed, and Katori's own torpedo salvo also missed. Iowa then fired 46 16-inch and 124 5-inch shells at Katori, hitting her repeatedly; Katori sank with her guns still firing [S298]. The destroyer Maikaze fired torpedoes at Iowa and New Jersey; a warning from aircraft overhead let New Jersey maneuver and the torpedoes passed just ahead [S298]. The destroyer Nowaki opened distance during the melee, and New Jersey and Iowa pursued and opened fire at the extreme range of 34,000 to 39,000 yards, straddling her several times and hitting her with splinters; the last salvo, at a range of 22 miles, is believed to be the longest-range gun shot at an enemy ship [S298].

The museum history adds that New Jersey led Iowa, two cruisers, and four destroyers in the sweep, that Spruance took tactical command of the force from New Jersey, which it calls the first time a four-star admiral was on a ship in a sea action, and that New Jersey's float plane spotted the fleeing ships 25 miles away [S309]. That account is tier 3.

Two sources date the detachment differently. DANFS states that Iowa was detached from the support group on 16 February 1944 to conduct an anti-shipping sweep around Truk [S281]; the NHHC H-Gram places the around-the-atoll cruise on 17 February [S298]. Both are recorded under "Conflicts between sources".

No action report by either ship was found. The sub-facet rests on the two accounts above. See `gaps-07.md`.

## Shore bombardment

### The procedure

In an amphibious operation the fire support group furnishes naval gunfire support and screens and protects the attack force in the landing area [S292, para. 1412]. The operation plan may carry a bombardment plan and a fire-support plan among its annexes, along with the landing, movement, air-support, air-sea rescue, logistic, communication, intelligence, medical, storm, smoke, loading, salvage, major action, screening, defense, aerological, mine-sweeping, and demolition plans [S292, para. 1415]. War Instructions names these annexes but reproduces none of them.

The only fetched procedure for firing at a shore target is the Mark 37 pamphlet, which covers the secondary battery rather than the 16-inch guns. It gives three cases: a visible shore target, an obscured target displaced a known distance from a point of aim, and an obscured target with no suitable visible point of aim but a known chart position [S293, ch. 1]. Direct fire is used whenever possible, and the procedure is then essentially that for a surface target except that optical tracking in train, elevation, and range is usually necessary [S293, ch. 5]. For indirect fire the point tracked by the computer is always the desired point of fall, a chart including ship and target positions is used to fix the target relative to the ship, and the director, being free of other duties, can be used to fix the ship's position by taking ranges and bearings to known points [S293, ch. 5]. Target speed is set to zero at the computer, or, in a practice the pamphlet says some activities found desirable, to the speed of the current with target course set to the current's course plus 180 degrees, to cancel the motion of the water [S293, ch. 5]. Offset fire, indirect fire with a point of aim, is done two ways: in method 1, called the Point Oboe method by several fleet activities, the director tracks the offset reference point and the impact is shifted to the target with the range and deflection spot knobs, which limits it to targets within 10 degrees of the point of aim and requires the spots to be recomputed continuously as the relative bearing changes; in method 2, the offset method, the offsets are applied to the data fed to the generating section of the computer so that bearing and range are generated for the point of fall itself [S293, ch. 5]. The pamphlet states plainly that it does not attempt to describe the many tasks that accompany a shore bombardment, among them the preparation of charts and expected radar indications, communication problems, and the selection of projectiles and powder charges [S293, ch. 5].

### Spotting aircraft and shore fire control parties

Spots received from the shore fire control party or from aircraft are applied at the computer by means of the range and deflection spot knobs [S293, ch. 5]. For the main battery at long range the division of labour between plane and ship is the one described above: the plane spots in range and the ship in deflection, because an air spotter cannot spot accurately in deflection [S294, §21B7]. Observation plane squadrons are part of the battle line in the major action organization [S292, para. 1201]. No shore fire control party procedure and no air spot procedure specific to an Iowa-class ship was found. See `gaps-07.md`.

### Rounds expended: Muroran, 15 July 1945

Task Unit 34.8.2, Bombardment Group Baker, under Rear Admiral Oscar C. Badger, detached from the carrier task force on the night of 14 to 15 July 1945 for Muroran, Hokkaido, an approach requiring a three-hour run with land on three sides in each direction. At 0936 on 15 July the fast battleships Iowa, Missouri, and Wisconsin, the light cruisers Atlanta and Dayton, and eight destroyers opened fire on the Nihon Steel Company and the Wanishi Ironworks, Japan's second largest producer of coke and pig iron. Badger's flagship Iowa hit the Nihon furnaces on the second salvo, Missouri's gunfire produced a massive explosion, and Wisconsin's ninth salvo caused what the account calls a terrific explosion. At 1025 the force ceased fire after expending 860 16-inch shells, of which 170 were direct hits [S323].

Two other bombardments are given with figures in the same source. At Kamaishi on 14 July 1945, Task Unit 34.8.1 with the battleships South Dakota, Indiana, and Massachusetts, two heavy cruisers, and nine destroyers opened fire at 1210 and ceased at 1419 after expending 802 16-inch, 728 8-inch, and 825 5-inch shells, firing by radar at 26,000 yards because haze held visibility to 14,000 to 20,000 yards and mined water inside the 600-foot depth curve had to be avoided; the bombardment was covered by a special combat air patrol of 20 fighters from Task Group 38.1 [S323]. No Iowa-class ship was present. At Hitachi on the night of 17 to 18 July 1945, Bombardment Group Baker with Iowa, Missouri (Halsey embarked), Wisconsin, North Carolina, and Alabama, the light cruisers Atlanta and Dayton, eight destroyers, the British battleship King George V, and two British destroyers opened fire at 2314 and delivered 1,206 16-inch shells plus 267 14-inch shells from King George V [S323].

Earlier in the war Iowa took fire in return. On 18 March 1944, flying the flag of Vice Admiral Willis A. Lee, Commander Battleships Pacific, she joined the bombardment of Mili Atoll and was struck by two Japanese 4.7-inch projectiles, suffering negligible damage [S281].

These figures come from an NHHC H-Gram, a tier 2 official essay, not from the ships' action reports. No Iowa-class bombardment action report was found. See `gaps-07.md`.

## Anti-aircraft doctrine

### The ship's station in the screen

War Instructions states that the dispositions adopted for repelling air attack permit early warning of the approach of hostile aircraft, concentration of anti-aircraft fire in all range bands with the maximum concentration around heavy or valuable units, freedom of maneuver of the separate formations to bring the anti-aircraft batteries to bear, freedom of maneuver of individual ships to take avoiding action, and the launching of additional fighters from carriers [S292, para. 613]. The force to be protected is wholly surrounded by an air defense screen established at an interval and distance depending on the number of screening vessels, the size and type of the force, and the need for mutual support; this disposition is most effective against torpedo-plane, skip-bombing, and horizontal bombing attack and less effective against dive bombing, and pickets are normally disposed outside the circular screen for early warning [S292, para. 614]. Ships are stationed to give all-round coverage and to permit radar controlled fire in any sector in which an attack may develop [S292, para. 615]. A combat air patrol of fighters is either directly over the force or within visible distance in a given sector, the overhead position normally being best [S292, para. 616].

The instructions give the principle and not the diagram. No fetched source gives the station, interval, or distance a fast battleship held in a carrier task group. See `gaps-07.md`.

### Readiness, opening fire, and maneuver

Air attack may come at any time, so all ships must be prepared to repel it regardless of formation [S292, para. 720]. Because the time available is generally so short, an adequate proportion of the armament is kept manned and the personnel fully alert whenever air attack is possible or imminent [S292, para. 721]. During daylight a proportion of the close-range weapons is always kept on the bearing of greatest danger, toward the sun or toward low clouds; during twilight and on moonlit nights the more dangerous side is the one opposite the light or moon, and the major part of the close-range battery is kept bearing that way if practicable [S292, paras. 722, 723]. Each ship organizes a coordinated aircraft lookout system covering all sectors, makes full use of optical instruments, provides special measures for searching near the sun, and keeps aircraft lookout watches to not over half an hour where practicable [S292, para. 719].

The control organization must be flexible enough to permit any weapon to open fire in a surprise attack at the discretion of the senior officer or petty officer at the weapon, which requires all anti-aircraft personnel to be proficient in aircraft recognition [S292, para. 735]. Any gunfire is effective in reducing the accuracy of bombing even when the chance of hitting is small, and well disciplined fire causes attackers to release at greater altitude or range [S292, para. 733]. The general tendency, particularly with small caliber guns, is to open fire too soon, wasting ammunition and reducing the volume of fire available later while the planes are within effective range [S292, para. 734]. During an air attack a vessel must be ready for high speed and radical changes of course, and individual ships maneuver to bring the greatest volume of anti-aircraft fire to bear for short periods while keeping approximate position in the formation [S292, paras. 725, 737]. At night primary reliance is on anti-aircraft fire power and maneuver rather than on fighters [S292, para. 741]. The gunnery manual adds two commands for the close-in case: "Repel Dive Attack" sends the guns to designated sectors to open fire automatically on any plane sighted in the assigned sector, and "Drop Down" puts all hands on deck on their faces to avoid bomb fragments [S294, appendix C, C2 items 42, 43].

### The kamikaze period

War Instructions 1944 contains no instructions for suicide attack; its chapter on action against aircraft treats bombing and torpedo attack only [S292, paras. 719 to 743]. What the fetched sources give is the record of the ships rather than a doctrine.

On 11 April 1945 off Okinawa, with the Japanese renewing kamikaze attacks, DANFS records that only drastic maneuvers and heavy barrages of gunfire saved the task force, that no enemy pilot achieved a direct hit, that near misses close aboard caused some minor damage, and that combat air patrol downed 17 planes with ships' gunfire accounting for more [S284]. On 21 February 1945 off Iwo Jima, Wisconsin engaged incoming aircraft with her 5-inch/38 batteries [S284]. Across her wartime service Wisconsin shot down three enemy planes and claimed four assists [S284]. The New Jersey museum history states that the Fifth Fleet weathered the heaviest kamikaze attacks of the war, with over 355 kamikazes and 340 other planes attacking on 6 and 7 April 1945 and 185 kamikazes with a hundred or more other planes on 11 and 12 April 1945, and that unlike the carriers the Iowa-class ships were near impervious to kamikazes [S309]. That last claim is tier 3 and no primary source was fetched for it.

## Damage control

### The objective and the station

The objective of damage control is the maintenance of the maximum offensive power of the ship, which makes it an offensive function as well as a defensive provision. To that end it preserves watertight integrity, buoyancy and stability, and maneuverability, mobility, and seaworthiness; controls list and trim; effects rapid repairs; provides protection from fire and from chemical attack; and facilitates the care of the wounded [S295, §22-1]. The four fundamental elements of a damage control program are organization, education, training, and maintenance [S295, §22-2].

The damage control station is the battle station of the damage control officer and is placed in as central and well protected a location as possible. On a large ship it is manned by a group including a stability control officer, a casualty board operator and damage analyst, enough trained phone talkers, and representatives of the fuel-oil transfer, electrical, and ordnance groups, plus ratings thoroughly familiar with the ship and its systems [S295, §23-2]. The equipment usually provided there is charts and diagrams of the ship's subdivision and systems, flooding effect and liquid loading diagrams, a casualty board and a means of showing the state of closure continuously; sound-powered telephone outlets to the repair stations and main control stations; intercommunicating loudspeaker systems, with at least one general announcing (1MC) transmitter available if the damage control announcing system (4MC) fails; list, trim, and draft indicators; and a fire-alarm annunciator board showing excessive temperature in the magazine compartments [S295, §23-6]. Arrangements are made for a designated repair station to take charge if the damage control station is destroyed, with further stations designated to assume control in turn [S295, §23-4].

### Repair parties by number and station

FTP-170B's standard organization for large ships is a damage control station as headquarters plus six repair parties, with two more on carriers [S295, §23-2]:

| Party | Assignment |
|---|---|
| Repair I | Deck or topside repair party [S295, §23-2] |
| Repair II | Forward below decks repair party [S295, §23-2] |
| Repair III | After below decks repair party [S295, §23-2] |
| Repair IV | Amidships below decks repair party [S295, §23-2] |
| Repair V | Engineer repair party [S295, §23-2] |
| Repair VI | Ordnance repair party [S295, §23-2] |
| Repair VII, VIII | Gasoline and flight deck parties, carriers only [S295, §23-2] |

On a battleship Repair I covers everything above and forward and aft of the armored box, with a large number of patrols, groups, or units distributed from the superstructure down to the second deck under an experienced officer assisted by other officers, the ship's boatswain, and chief and other petty officers [S295, §23-9]. Repair II, Repair III, and Repair IV cover the forward, after, and midship areas within the armored boundaries but outside the main machinery spaces, and usually share responsibility for the magazine spaces with Repair VI; Repair V has its headquarters convenient to the main machinery spaces [S295, §23-9]. Every party has a centrally located headquarters in its own area and is subdivided into patrols, groups, or units dispersed through that area, so that no single hit removes the whole party [S295, §§23-2, 23-9]. The handbook recommends groups of four to six men under a responsible petty officer, accountable for a defined area and defined equipment, in communication with the repair station and trained to act independently [S295, §23-9].

Within a main repair station on a large combatant ship, war experience produced the following group organization [S295, §24-4]:

| Group | Men | Task |
|---|---|---|
| Fire party | 4 to 6 | Carries rescue breathing apparatus, tending lines, canisters, and carbon dioxide; precedes the other groups into a damaged area to investigate for fire and for explosive and toxic gases [S295, §24-4] |
| Sounding party | 3 to 5 | Determines the extent of flooding immediately after damage, with the fuel-oil men locating contaminated oil tanks [S295, §24-4] |
| Pump detail | 2 to 3 | Rigs portable submersible pumps and discharge hoses [S295, §24-4] |
| Lighting detail | 2 to 3 | Rigs emergency lights or battery powered flood lamps [S295, §24-4] |
| Wreckage removal detail | 3 to 6 | Provides crow bars, mauls, jacks, and chain falls to clear wreckage from a fouled mount or director [S295, §24-4] |

FTP-170B requires at least one electrician's mate with each repair party, and the handbook wants an electrician's mate with the first investigating party sent to the scene of damage [S295, §24-3]. A repair party also draws at least one and preferably two men from the fuel-oil gang, at least one storekeeper, a hospital corpsman, a yeoman capable of shorthand as phone talker and recorder, and, in the topside party, a radioman for antenna repair [S295, §§24-3]. Outlying units of four to six men each, with their own lockers of rescue breathing apparatus, spare canisters, portable extinguishers, and small tools, are stationed away from the main repair station and can function as minor repair parties in their own right, and where practicable one man is stationed as a patrol in each principal compartment of the area [S295, §24-4]. The repair party is described as the damage control officer's representative at the scene of a casualty, and the accuracy of its reports is what his analysis rests on [S295, §24-2].

### Damage control communications

The provisions on a large ship are battle telephone circuits, the damage control announcing system 4MC, ship's service telephones, the general announcing system 1MC, and messengers [S295, §28-1]. The battle circuits are sound powered, generating their own current from the voice, so they keep working after all the ship's power systems have been knocked out [S295, §28-2]. The normal damage control circuits of a large ship are [S295, §28-2]:

| 4JZ | Forward repair | The officer at Repair II with each Repair II patrol, each forward battle dressing station, and each remote valve control station forward of the engineering spaces [S295, §28-2] |
| 5JZ | After repair | The officer at Repair III with each Repair III patrol, each after battle dressing station, and each remote valve control station aft [S295, §28-2] |
| 6JZ | Amidship repair | The officer at Repair IV with each Repair IV patrol, each amidship battle dressing station, and each remote valve control station amidships [S295, §28-2] |
| 7JZ | Engineer's repair | The officer at Repair V with each remote valve operating station for steam and fuel oil, each engine room, fireroom, and auxiliary engine room, and each amidship battle dressing station [S295, §28-2] |
| 9JZ | Magazine sprinkling and ordnance repair | Repair VI forward and aft, sprinkling control forward and aft, each 5-inch powder handling room, each turret powder handling room, and the damage control station [S295, §28-2] |

### Subdivision, the closure system, and war cruising

A modern battleship has well over 600 watertight compartments, and the condition of that subdivision, its watertight integrity, is described as of the greatest importance [S295, §30-2]. The three-step closure system that protects it is set out under "Material conditions of closure" above. Compartment check-off lists, posted in each compartment, itemize every classified fitting with its name, number, location, purpose, classification, and the division responsible for operating it, and a master copy of each is kept in the damage control office [S295, §22-13].

While cruising, a watch is kept in the damage control station and in a designated repair station, with patrols through the ship to maintain the prescribed material condition and readiness to control sudden damage [S295, §23-10]. No fitting required to be closed in the condition being maintained may be opened without the commanding officer's permission through the damage control officer, and the damage control watch officer keeps a log recording the name and rank of the man requesting, the number and type of the fitting, its damage control classification, the time opened, and the time closed [S295, §23-10]. Requests pass through the damage control patrol so that the patrol knows of every authorized violation in its area, and notification of closure goes back the same way [S295, §23-10].

### Fire fighting

During battle the fire main is segregated into a number of separate and independent sections, each with its own source of water, so that damage to one section leaves the others able to furnish pressure near the damage [S295, §29-5]. Magazines, handling rooms, and ready service ammunition spaces carry perforated sprinkling piping arranged to drench bulkheads and overheads, which both reduces temperature from a nearby fire and prevents the outbreak of fire in a magazine when adjoining spaces burn [S295, §29-6]. Fires are fought primarily by repair party personnel at all times, with other personnel standing clear but ready as secondary fire-fighting groups [S295, §32-18]. Large repair parties may be divided into fire-fighting groups, and the plan of action for each area is decided in advance, because a team that is not trained to follow a predetermined plan will produce confusion [S295, §§32-14, 32-15].

The action of a fire-fighting group attacking a fire runs: the group leader investigates the location and nature of the fire; the group leader designates the equipment to be used first and it is passed out in order of priority from a check-off list at the repair station; hose men lead out hose, remove kinks, and stand by the nozzle; the plug man operates the fire plug valve; access men operate doors, hatches, and scuttles and clear the route to the fire; the foam generator operator prepares the generator while the foam supply man draws spare cans; portable carbon dioxide extinguisher men take extinguishers to the fire for electrical and other fires; and the rescue breathing apparatus men stand ready with their apparatus on, asbestos suits made ready, and tenders holding tending lines and spare canisters [S295, §32-17].

Hose is supplied in 1 1/2-inch and 2 1/2-inch sizes, double jacketed, cotton, rubber lined, in 50-foot lengths with a coupling at each end [S295, §32-7]. The gasoline-driven handy billy weighs about 100 pounds and delivers 60 gallons per minute at 100 pounds pressure with a suction lift of about 20 feet, and only one 1 1/2-inch hose line should be worked from each because of its limited discharge [S295, §32-10]. The Model P-500 portable gasoline-driven centrifugal pump delivers 500 gallons per minute at 100 pounds per square inch at a 16-foot suction lift, and is used for emergency drainage as well as fire fighting [S295, §32-11]. Electric portable submersible pumps deliver 140 gallons per minute against a 70-foot head or 180 gallons per minute against a 50-foot head [S295, §32-12].

### Counterflooding

Counterflooding is the deliberate taking of sea water into tanks or compartments opposite the damaged ones to reduce list and trim at the same time; after damage on the starboard quarter, for example, counterflooding may be undertaken forward and to port [S295, §18-7]. The reserve buoyancy it costs should be recovered as soon as possible by transferring liquids to correct list and trim and pumping the counterflooding water overboard [S295, §18-7]. All ships can counterflood peak tanks to correct trim; in ships with a torpedo protection system, counterflooding is the most rapid means of correcting list, while other ships are not designed to counterflood through sea valves and must use hose connections from the fire main, which is normally slow [S295, §18-7]. Two precautions are given: keep air escapes open so the spaces fill solidly, and close the counterflooding valves after use to prevent free communication with the sea [S295, §18-7]. On a ship with a torpedo protection system, counterflooding the voids of the protective layer opposite the flooded area removes list rapidly, which preserves speed, maneuverability, and resistance to further damage and gives a level deck for gunnery and aircraft operation; it improves the stability characteristics by removing the list; and it costs reserve buoyancy through reduced freeboard [S295, §18-7]. The Iowa-class torpedo defense system itself is described in `01-hull-and-armor.md`.

### The nearest damage evidence

`01-hull-and-armor.md` established that the Bureau of Ships war damage report series contains no report on an Iowa-class ship, and the series index confirms it [S297]. The nearest fast battleship report with a file is number 57, on South Dakota (BB-57) at Guadalcanal on 14 and 15 November 1942 [S296, S297]. South Dakota is a South Dakota-class ship. Her experience is recorded here as fleet evidence, not as a description of an Iowa.

The report states that the ship was at general quarters with Material Condition Afirm set and the fire main divided into four sections, with fire and flushing pumps 2, 3, 4, and 5 in machinery spaces B-1 to B-4 on the line [S296, §6]. There were no serious fires during or after the action; fires were small and extinguished before spreading beyond the immediate vicinity of ignition, and the most dangerous was in two life jackets found burning in a passage within the armored box adjacent to a 5-inch magazine, believed ignited by a flash through an ammunition hoist from a 1.1-inch clipping room [S296, §50]. The superstructure fires, though not serious, were difficult to extinguish: hoses had to be led up the outside of the superstructure from the main deck to the 07 level because many ladders were damaged, and the large number of personnel trapped in the superstructure complicated the work [S296, §51]. Fire-fighting water accumulated on deck, filled adjacent areas to the level of the access coamings, and as the ship heeled spilled over and flooded the areas below in turn, partially immersing lighting transformers on the main and second superstructure decks [S296, §51]. Two remedies followed: 2 1/2-inch risers were installed on each side of the superstructure from the 03 to the 010 level with a 1 1/2-inch fire plug at each level, two of them in case one was damaged, and 2-inch holes were drilled in the corners of the access coamings at deck level so that water would drain to a small area of the main deck where the door coamings are 18 inches high and could be pumped overboard by electric submersible pumps [S296, §§52, 53]. Damage to the ship did not imperil her; the loss was of fire control and interior communication power [S296, §54].

## Underway replenishment

### The fueling-at-sea procedure

In November 1944 COMINCH issued a comprehensive set of instructions for fueling at sea, intended to serve as a guide for those to whom the exercise was unfamiliar [S305, ch. 18]. At that time the broadside method was the Navy's standard means of fueling at sea, and the procedure in the instructions had one vessel, usually the larger, tow the other at 40 to 80 feet alongside, an operation described not as deadweight towing but as an exercise in position keeping aided by a towline; crews had already found they could dispense with the towline and hold station by adjusting engine speed and eye [S305, ch. 18].

Fuel oil was normally dispensed from four oiling stations on the oiler's well deck, two on each side [S305, ch. 18]. A 210-foot, 6-inch hose was normally used to transfer heavy fuel oil to any ship larger than a destroyer, assembled from 50-foot lengths of collapsible hose and 20-foot lengths of heavy duty hose, the assembly carried on two saddles suspended from the oiler's booms, with the last section taken aboard the receiving ship; for a destroyer the last 20-foot length was replaced by a lighter one [S305, ch. 18]. Five handling lines were used: a hose line passed to the receiving ship to haul in and return the hose end; an easing out line retained by the oiler; an outer bight line passed to the receiving ship, not used when fueling destroyers and smaller vessels; an inner bight line retained by the oiler; and saddle falls retained by the oiler [S305, ch. 18]. Tending the bight lines was the most critical part of the operation, with winch operators on both ships continually adjusting saddle height as the ships moved or rolled, to keep the hose out of the water without stretching it [S305, ch. 18].

The alternative to the broadside rig appeared late. A new arrangement proposed by an officer of Cimarron was tested by Kaskaskia over two weeks in December 1944, with the destroyer Hart as receiving ship, refueling some fifty times in all conditions; a 7/8-inch span wire carried wheeled trolleys supporting the hose cradles, taking weight off the saddle whips. The rig, first called the Elwood method after its originator and later the wire-span method, let the ships stay 60 to 180 feet apart, allowed greater speed through the water, made station keeping and maneuvering easier, and removed the need to fuel into the wind [S305, ch. 18]. Except for dispensing diesel fuel, which normally went by the astern method from the oiler's after station, the astern method was considered an emergency measure for use when weather prevented the broadside technique [S305, ch. 18].

A fueling rendezvous was organized in advance. On the night before, the group commander set the speed and course for safe fueling from the wind, weather, sea state, and operational requirements, and the oiler group's speed was set so that it reached the rendezvous just before dawn with the gear already rigged, so fueling could begin at first light. Just before daybreak three or four oilers formed a single line 1,500 to 2,000 yards apart, steaming into the wind at about 10 knots, with a second line of ammunition and provision ships 2,000 yards astern of them; all combatant ships made their approaches on the issuing ships so as to maintain the integrity of the replenishment line [S306, ch. 19]. It was normal to fuel a large ship and an escort at the same time, but only one large ship could be rearmed at a time [S306, ch. 19].

### The battleship as a fueling ship

The doctrine put the battleship on both sides of the hose. A Third Fleet report quoted in the replenishment history states that in order to complete the fueling of a large force expeditiously, destroyers must be fueled by the heavy ships waiting their turn to fuel from the tankers; it adds that fueling two battleships simultaneously from one tanker is feasible with favorable wind and sea but, because of the possibility of crushing the tanker, should not be resorted to except in an emergency in which the possible loss of the tanker is a secondary consideration [S305, ch. 18].

The class did both, sometimes on the same day. On 2 January 1945 Wisconsin spent the day fueling the destroyers Lewis Hancock and Hunt and taking fuel from the oiler Taluga [S284]. On 22 February 1945 off Iwo Jima she refueled Mertz and Monssen, and took on fuel from Merrimack the next day [S284]. On 1 April 1945 off Okinawa she fueled Colahan, McGowan, Mertz, and Monssen [S284]. On 8 July 1945 she fueled Mertz and Colahan before taking fuel from Sebec, and on 20 July she fueled from Lackawanna and began rearming with 16-inch ammunition from the ammunition ship Shasta, taking further ammunition from Vesuvius before the task force departed on 22 July [S284].

From the oiler's side, on 16 June 1944, D-plus-1 at Saipan, the oiler Guadalupe fueled the battleships Washington and New Jersey and the destroyers Stephen Potter and Miller with a total of 39,444 barrels of fuel oil, 743 barrels of diesel oil, and 818 gallons of aviation gasoline [S307]. Guadalupe was one of 24 oilers in the eight fueling-at-sea groups supporting that operation [S307].

Fueling in bad weather is what made the December 1944 typhoon dangerous. The museum history records that with several destroyers dangerously low on fuel and riding high, Halsey ordered refueling to continue, and that New Jersey tried to refuel the destroyer Spence until the fuel lines parted and Spence nearly collided with her [S309]. DANFS records that the typhoon caught many ships refueling with nearly dry bunkers and little ballast, and that the destroyers Hull, Monaghan, and Spence capsized and sank [S284].

The Iowa-class ships' own fueling stations, hose rigs, and pumping rates are not described in any source fetched for this facet. See `gaps-07.md`.

## Flagship duties

War Instructions treats the flagship of the officer in tactical command, with its screen, as one of the normal task groups of a major action, and notes that the ship may be assigned to another task group for the battle itself [S292, para. 1201]. In an amphibious operation the flagship provides the command facilities for the attack force commander, the commander support aircraft, and the commander expeditionary troops or landing force until the latter goes ashore [S292, para. 1412].

Iowa sailed for the Pacific on 2 January 1944 as flagship of Battleship Division 7 [S281]. On 18 March 1944 she flew the flag of Vice Admiral Willis A. Lee, Commander Battleships Pacific, during the Mili bombardment [S281]. She returned to Japanese waters in January 1946 and became flagship of the Fifth Fleet, holding that role until 25 March 1946 [S281].

New Jersey carried the two fleet commands. For the Truk raid, Vice Admiral Spruance, commander of the Fifth Fleet, personally assumed command of Task Force 50 and shifted his flag from the heavy cruiser Indianapolis to New Jersey [S298]. The museum history dates that to 4 February 1944 at Majuro and says the flag stayed in the ship until 10 April; it also states that on becoming commander of the Central Pacific Force in November 1943 Spruance had decided against a battleship flagship so that his own movements would not weaken his fleet, and that he shifted to New Jersey for Operation Hailstone [S309]. The same account has Spruance conferring aboard New Jersey with Mitscher, Lee, and Turner on 11 February 1944, and has Admiral Lee's flag in the ship during May 1944 as Pacific battleship commander [S309]. Before the Battle of the Philippine Sea, Spruance ordered New Jersey and six other fast battleships, with cruisers and destroyers, to form a battle line under Lee [S309].

The ship was modified for the next flag. The museum history states that Spruance had found New Jersey's flag quarters spacious but that they were not large enough for Halsey's much larger staff, and that the flag quarters were enlarged and updated at Pearl Harbor; New Jersey left Pearl Harbor on 24 August 1944 carrying Halsey and his staff [S309]. The fleet changed number with the commander: what was the Fifth Fleet under Spruance was the Third Fleet under Halsey [S309]. NHHC holds a photograph of Halsey, Commander Third Fleet, on the bridge of his flagship New Jersey while en route to carry out raids on the Philippines in December 1944 [S310].

What flag plot did is documented only anecdotally, and only from the tier 3 account. It places the decision to go north after Ozawa in flag plot at 2022 on 24 October 1944, with Halsey putting his finger on Ozawa's charted position; it places the signal forming Task Force 34 under Lee at 1512 the same day; it places Halsey on the flag bridge the following morning on receiving Kinkaid's message; and it has the decoded message asking the whereabouts of Task Force 34 handed to him in flag plot at 1000 on 25 October 1944 [S309]. No plan, deck plan, or organization document for the flag spaces was found. See `gaps-07.md`.

Halsey's flag moved on. At the Hitachi bombardment of 17 and 18 July 1945 he was embarked in Missouri [S323].

## Conflicts between sources

**Date of the Truk surface sweep.** DANFS states that Iowa was detached from the support group on 16 February 1944 to conduct an anti-shipping sweep around Truk [S281]. The NHHC H-Gram places Spruance's around-the-atoll cruise, and the engagements with Katori, Maikaze, and Nowaki, on 17 February 1944 [S298]. Both are tier 1 or tier 2 NHHC material. Neither is preferred here.

**Date and pamphlet number of the Mark 37 operating instructions.** The HyperWar transcription's title page prints 21 April 1949, while the promulgation letter reproduced on the same page is dated 21 April 1945 and names Ordnance Pamphlet 1919; the body of the letter and the running text name Ordnance Pamphlet 1719 [S293]. A note inside the text refers to current instructions of January 1949 [S293]. The document is therefore treated here as a late-war to early-postwar statement of procedure, not as 1943 practice.

**Material condition lettering by date.** The 1942 Bureau of Ships report on South Dakota records Material Condition Afirm set at general quarters [S296, §6].
