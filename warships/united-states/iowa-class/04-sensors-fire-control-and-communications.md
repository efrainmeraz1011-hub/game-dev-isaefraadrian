# Iowa class: sensors, fire control, and communications

Status: partial (see `gaps-04.md`)

Citations [S141] to [S159] resolve to entries in `sources-04.md`, the source list for this facet. Turret optics, turret rangefinders, and the gun mounts themselves are covered in `03-armament-and-ammunition.md`; that file carries its own sources file and its citations do not resolve here. The armor of the conning tower, the fire control tower, and the director tubes is in `01-hull-and-armor.md`.

Two kinds of source sit side by side in this file, and the difference matters.

- Ordnance pamphlets and Navy training manuals describe the equipment. They name the Mark 38 director, the Mark 8 rangekeeper, the Mark 37 director, and the Mark 8 radar, and they describe the main battery system as fitted in "newly constructed battleships" with nine 16-inch guns in three centerline turrets [S141, p. 443]. They do not name the Iowa class.
- Photo indexes and class tables tie particular sets to particular hulls on particular dates. Those are tier 3.

Where only the first kind exists, the text says so.

## Main battery fire control

### The system as the 1944 manual describes it

Naval Ordnance and Gunnery (NAVPERS 16116, May 1944) devotes chapter 18 to the main battery fire control system "used to control the 16-inch battery on newly constructed battleships," whose main battery is nine guns in three triple centerline turrets, two forward and one aft [S141, p. 443]. It lists the principal units [S141, p. 443]:

| Unit | Quantity | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Main directors Mark 38 | 2 | [S141, p. 443] |
| Rangekeepers Mark 8 (Mod 9 or Mod 11) | 2 | [S141, p. 443] |
| Stable verticals Mark 41 or Mark 43 | 2 | [S141, p. 443] |
| Rangefinders (one per director, one per turret) | 5 | [S141, p. 443] |
| Range receivers Mark 1 (with Mark 8 Mod 9 only) | 2 | [S141, p. 443] |
| Auxiliary director Mark 40 | 1 | [S141, p. 443] |
| Auxiliary computers: Mark 3 and Mark 5 trunnion-tilt corrector in the fire control tower, Mark 3 Mod 1 or Mod 2 in each turret officer's booth | 1 + 3 | [S141, p. 443] |

The manual calls this arrangement duplicate primary installations provided for control flexibility, with auxiliary arrangements available if a primary unit is knocked out [S141, p. 443]. The main directors sit high in the superstructure for visibility; the auxiliary director is at the main battery control station in the fire control tower, which is usually the main battery reference point [S141, p. 443].

### Mark 38 directors

Two per ship. NavSource's photo captions place one on Spot 1 at the foretop and one on Spot 2 abaft the after stack in Iowa [S146]. A commissioning-day photograph of Iowa on 22 February 1943 shows the after Mark 38 with Mark 8 fire control radar on top [S146].

Table: Mark 38 director as described in 1944 [S141, p. 446]

| Item | Figure |
|---|---|
| Train limits | 198 degrees right and left of centre |
| Inside working diameter | approximately 8 ft (2.4 m) |
| Inside working height | approximately 6 1/2 ft (2.0 m) |
| Rangefinder | 26 1/2-foot (8.1 m) stereoscopic, hand-operated range transmitter |
| Spotter's periscope | 12 power, line of sight 15 degrees above and below the deck plane |

The rangefinder mount is not cross-levelled, though it may carry an automatic level stabiliser [S141, p. 446]. The structure is a foundation ring carrying a roller-bearing assembly under a cup-shaped platform covered by an armored shield [S141, p. 445]. Inside are the trainer's amplidyne power drive and receiver-regulator, the trainer's telescope and handwheels with a horizontal parallax corrector, the leveller's telescope mount with roller-path tilt corrector and vertical parallax corrector, the cross-leveller's telescope mount, the battle-order indicator, a cease-firing contact maker, and a time-of-flight buzzer that sounds just before the salvo lands so the spotter can pick out his own ship's fall of shot [S141, p. 446].

The director can be trained four ways: manual, with the handwheels geared straight to the training rack; local, with the handwheels working the power drive through the receiver-regulator; automatic, with increments of director train generated in the rangekeeper driving the power drive; and slew, where a slewing switch, usually worked by the control officer, seizes the power drive [S141, pp. 446 to 447].

Unlike the Mark 37, the Mark 38's telescopes are not stabilised in cross-level, so level and cross-level are measured in planes perpendicular to the deck plane [S141, p. 447]. The stable verticals in plot generate their angles in the same planes [S141, p. 447].

Director crew, seven men [S141, p. 447]: spotter (in immediate control), trainer, pointer (leveller), cross-leveller, rangefinder operator, parallax range setter and talker, and standby rangefinder operator and talker. The spotter estimates target angle, target speed, and wind, and telephones them to plot for entry into the rangekeeper [S141, p. 447].

### Mark 8 rangekeeper and Mark 41 stable vertical

Two Mark 8 Mod 9 rangekeepers stand in the main battery plotting room [S141, p. 450]. A rangekeeper solves the surface problem set up on its dials and transmits what is needed to set sights, position guns, and train the director; it also sends battle orders, plot-ready, and fall-of-salvo signals to the directors and turret officers' booths [S141, p. 450]. Computations are based on initial velocities of 1,650 to 1,800 f.s. for target charges or 2,150 to 2,300 f.s. for service charges, set by hand knobs [S141, p. 450].

There is no automatic rate control. Observed range and bearing are compared with the generated values and rate control is done by hand, by adjusting the target course and target speed inputs [S141, p. 450].

An attached graphic plotter automatically plots advance range, generated present range, and sight deflection, marking time along each edge of the sheet at one-minute intervals; a protractor graduated in knots checks generated range rate against the range-receiver plot [S141, p. 450].

Hand inputs to the rangekeeper are target course, target speed, true wind direction and speed, initial velocity, powder charge, range spots, deflection spots, selected train, initial range, and initial bearing [S141, p. 450]. Electrical inputs are own-ship course and speed, director train, and level and cross-level; level and cross-level also arrive mechanically from the stable vertical [S141, p. 450].

The Mark 41 stable vertical carries a gyroscopic element very like the Mark 6 stable element of the Mark 37 system, but its level and cross-level gimbals measure in planes perpendicular to the deck plane rather than in vertical planes [S141, p. 449]. It feeds continuous level and cross-level mechanically to the deck-tilt computer in the rangekeeper and electrically to rangekeepers and directors, and it provides the means of firing the battery automatically or by hand at a selected value of level or cross-level [S141, pp. 449 to 450].

A photograph of main battery plotting-room equipment in the same manual labels, left to right, the main battery control switchboard, range receiver Mark 1, the Mark 8 Mod 9 rangekeeper in its computer and pedestal sections with the graphic plotter above, the firing keys, the battle-order and ready-light indicators, and the Mark 41 stable vertical with its target-bearing and cross-level follow-up handles [S141, p. 445].

### Range receiver

The range receiver sits near the rangekeeper and takes the ranges from all five main battery rangefinders, the two in the directors and the three in the turrets, one at a time [S141, p. 451]. An operator at a rangefinder presses a signal button, a selector switch connects his circuit, and the recorder prints a number identifying his rangefinder at a position on the plotting sheet corresponding to the range. All five can be recorded in under ten seconds [S141, p. 451]. The range-receiver operator draws the mean range line, reads range rate off its slope, and passes mean range and range rate to the rangekeeper operator [S141, p. 451]. In the Mark 8 Mod 11 the range receiver is built into the graphic plotter [S141, pp. 443, 451].

### Auxiliary director Mark 40

The Mark 40 is an auxiliary installation in the fire control tower [S141, p. 451]. NavSource, describing this director on the North Carolina, South Dakota, and Iowa classes, says there was one aboard, in the fire control tower, serving as a controlling station in both primary and auxiliary control, designated main battery director three, and that it was first equipped with Mark 3 radar and later with Mark 27 [S146].

It comprises a combined spotting glass and trainer's periscope, two periscopes and mounts for leveller and cross-leveller, a Mark 3 computer and Mark 5 trunnion-tilt corrector, a target designator, a target-bearing transmitter, and level and cross-level transmitters [S141, p. 451]. The spotting glass has a 15-foot (4.6 m) base length; it and the periscopes sit on top of the fire control tower and train together through about 300 degrees on the trainer's handwheel drive. There are no power drives [S141, p. 451]. The Mark 3 computer and Mark 5 corrector take director train mechanically from the Mark 40, solve for sight angle and sight deflection, correct for trunnion tilt, and send gun elevation and train orders to the turrets through the switchboard in turret 2 [S141, p. 451].

Main battery control is manned by the gunnery officer and ten men: trainer, spotter, leveller, cross-leveller, range talker, and five computer operators [S141, pp. 451 to 452].

### Fire control radar on the main battery directors

Ordnance Pamphlet 658 of January 1943 describes the Mark 8 as a main battery fire control equipment for use with Mark 34 and Mark 38 directors [S142, p. 1]. A narrow beam, 6 degrees vertically and 2 degrees horizontally, is radiated from a polyrod antenna; the echo appears as a type A or type B scan and the operator reads range and bearing off the controls and passes them to plot [S142, p. 1].

The set's units sit in and under the director. The antenna assembly is on top of the director; the power control unit, the control indicator with its 5-inch cathode ray screen, the train indicator with a 3-inch screen, the range unit with its Veeder counter and range transmitter, the scan position indicators, and the scanning switch panel are inside; the transmitter and receiver are under the director [S142, pp. 2 to 7].

Dated fits on the four ships, from photo captions and a class table, are in the search radar table below. In summary: the first two ships commissioned with Mark 8 on both Mark 38 directors [S150], Mark 8 Mod 1 appears on Iowa's Spot 1 and Spot 2 in August 1945 captions [S146], Mark 8 Mod 3 appears on Iowa's Spot 1 in a July 1945 caption [S146], and Mark 13 replaced Mark 8 on Wisconsin in May 1945 and on New Jersey in June 1945 [S150]. NavSource notes a general arrangement drawing of the Mark 13 Mod 0 antenna fitted atop main battery director Mark 38 Mod 0 and Mods 1 to 5 [S146].

### Turret local control and turret rangefinders

Each turret carries a rangefinder, sight-setting indicators, telescope sights, an auxiliary Mark 3 computer, and the associated indicating and transmitting circuits, grouped at three stations: the turret officer's booth, the sight stations, and the gun-elevating and turret-training stations on the machinery floor [S141, p. 456].

The 1944 manual gives the turret rangefinder a base length of 43 feet (13.1 m) and a range scale from 4,500 yards to about 70,000 yards, with turret 1 coincidence and turrets 2 and 3 stereoscopic; both types have a range transmitter feeding the range receiver in plot [S141, p. 456]. NavWeaps gives the Iowa class 46-foot (14 m), 25-power rangefinders, stereoscopic Mark 52 in turrets II and III and coincidence Mark 53 in turret I [S151]. The two figures are listed under conflicts. `03-armament-and-ammunition.md` treats the turret rangefinders in detail.

The turret officer's booth also holds a multiple turret-train indicator, a battle-order indicator, and a fire control switchboard [S141, p. 456]. The battle-order indicator shows range, deflection, and one of six orders sent from a rangekeeper in plot: fire, cease, auto, indicating, local, and circuit broken [S141, p. 456].

Each turret has duplicate sight stations in flame-proof compartments right and left of the wing guns, each with a sight pointer's telescope, a sight trainer's telescope, and a sight-setting indicator; like elements are interconnected so either station can work while the other stands by [S141, p. 456]. Sight scales read 2,000 minutes for zero sight angle and 500 mils for zero deflection [S141, p. 457].

Gun-laying and sighting personnel per turret: three gun layers, one gun-train operator, two sight trainers, two sight pointers, and two sight setters [S141, p. 456].

### How the solution reaches the turrets

The directors measure target bearing, range, level angle, and cross-level angle; target angle and target speed are estimated by the spotter. These go to the plotting room, where the rangekeepers compute sight angle, sight deflection, and gun elevation and train orders. Those are transmitted electrically to the turrets, where sights are set by matching pointers and the guns are positioned automatically or by matching pointers [S141, p. 443].

Director train is corrected for horizontal parallax against the main battery reference point before it goes to the rangekeepers; gun train order computed for that reference point is corrected again at each turret for the turret's own horizontal displacement [S141, p. 447]. The turret's parallax mechanism takes parallax range (1/cR) from the rangekeeper, introduced by matching pointers, and the horizontal displacement is set once at installation [S141, p. 457].

Gun elevation order is level angle added to sight angle corrected for trunnion tilt; gun train order is director train combined with computed sight deflection corrected for trunnion tilt [S141, p. 450]. At the gun layer's station the elevation indicator corrects for roller-path inclination, settable from 0 to 1 degree with a second scale for the bearing of the high point, and for the difference in equivalent service rounds fired, which compensates the gap between the number used to set initial velocity on the rangekeeper and the number the guns have actually fired [S141, p. 457].

Four control methods are available [S141, pp. 457 to 458]:

| Method | What controls | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Forward or after Mark 38 director through the plotting-room rangekeepers to the turret indicators | [S141, p. 457] |
| Auxiliary | Mark 40 director in the fire control tower, working either the plotting-room rangekeepers or the fire control tower computer; or the control equipment in turret 2, whose gun elevation order can be switched to the other two turrets | [S141, pp. 457 to 458] |
| Local | The turret alone, on its own sights, rangefinder, and Mark 3 computer | [S141, p. 458] |
| Turret sights as local director | The sight pointer's sight transmits gun elevation order to the gun layer's indicator; the transmitter in turret 2 can be switched to the indicators in the other two turrets | [S141, p. 457] |

Firing methods for salvo fire are selected level, selected cross-level, and selected train. In the first two a value is set on the stable element or at the leveller's or cross-leveller's station and the guns fire when the actual angle matches; in the third a value of gun train order is locked on the rangekeeper's selected-train knob and the battery is fired from a key at the rangekeeper when the computed value reaches it [S141, pp. 453 to 454]. With continuous aim the rangekeeper transmits gun orders constantly and the battery is usually fired from the director or the stable element [S141, p. 454].

## Secondary battery fire control

### Mark 37 directors

The 1944 manual states that battleships carry four secondary battery directors in a diamond-shaped pattern around the superstructure, a quadruple installation of four Mark 37 directors, four Mark 6 stable elements, and four Mark 1 computers [S141, p. 387]. Photographs bear this out for the class: a November 1943 caption on Iowa's conning tower section names "one of the four 5in/38cal gun directors" [S146].

A complete installation is one Mark 37 director, one Mark 6 stable element, and one Mark 1 computer [S141, p. 387]. The stable elements and computers live below decks in protected plotting rooms; a stable element and a computer exchange data through mechanical shafting, so the two stand adjacent and always work as a pair [S141, p. 387].

Table: Mark 37 director as described in 1944 [S141, pp. 392 to 393]

| Item | Figure |
|---|---|
| Modifications in existence | over 50 |
| Crew | usually six or seven men |
| Shield thickness on battleships | 1 1/2 in (38 mm), splinter-proof |
| Telescopes | three movable-prism, some modification of the Mark 60 |
| Rangefinder | 15-foot (4.6 m) stereoscopic, some modification of the Mark 42 |
| Shield openings | five or six observation hatches in the top, three telescope ports in the front plate, a vertical slot each side for the rangefinder |

The director also carries a slewing sight, change-of-range receiver, cross-level gear with power drive only, training and elevating gear with hand and power drive, train and elevation indicators, a battle-order or spot transmitter, a range-spot transmitter, a searchlight trunnion-tilt corrector, a searchlight control transmitter, a star-shell spot transmitter, a target-angle or target-course repeater, and, in multiple installations, a roller-path tilt corrector and a horizontal-parallax mechanism [S141, pp. 393 to 394]. Crew are shown around the director as control officer, illumination control officer, talker, pointer, trainer, rangefinder operator, and range talker [S141, p. 394].

Switching arrangements on a quadruple installation let any director feed any computer and stable element group, and let a selected group control all or part of the battery, so a ship can engage more than one target at once and hold standby groups [S141, p. 387].

Two crossovers with the main battery are documented. On ships where the 5-inch/38 guns are the secondary battery, the Mark 37 can supply director train and director elevation to the main battery computers; and the secondary battery director, computer, and stable element groups can control main battery fire against air targets when selected fuze settings are used [S141, p. 387]. The Mark 1 computer's stated functions include pointing and training the main battery guns and setting their sights for AA fire at a common target using a selected fuze setting [S141, p. 416].

### Secondary plotting rooms

The manual gives battleships two arrangements and does not choose between them: one plotting room holding four computer and stable element groups, or two separate plotting rooms each holding two [S141, p. 387]. No source fetched for this facet states which arrangement the Iowa class used, or where the rooms were. See `gaps-04.md`. A 1983 photograph aboard New Jersey is captioned "the forward secondary battery plot room," which implies at least two, but it describes the ship after her 1980s modernization [S147].

### Secondary battery fire control radar

navypedia gives the as-commissioned fit as four Mark 4 radars on Iowa and New Jersey and four Mark 12 and Mark 22 combinations on Missouri and Wisconsin [S150]. The Radar Operator's Manual treats the Mark 3 and Mark 4 together under the equipment letters FC and FD [S143, part 4]. Its accuracy table, taken from BuOrd Pamphlet 657, gives the following for the three antennas used [S143, part 4]:

| Characteristic | FC long antenna (3 ft x 12 ft) | FC short antenna (6 ft x 6 ft) | FC antenna (6 ft x 7 ft) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nominal maximum range | 100,000 yd | 100,000 yd | 100,000 yd |
| Minimum range | about 1,000 yd | about 1,000 yd | about 1,000 yd |
| Horizontal beam width, lobing off | 4.6 degrees | 9.0 degrees | 9.0 degrees |
| Vertical beam width, lobing off | 30.0 degrees | 11.0 degrees | 19.0 degrees |
| Range accuracy | plus or minus 40 yd | plus or minus 40 yd | plus or minus 40 yd |
| Bearing accuracy | plus or minus 2 mils | plus or minus 4 mils | plus or minus 4 mils |
| Range resolution | plus or minus 400 yd | plus or minus 400 yd | plus or minus 400 yd |
| Bearing resolution | plus or minus 5 degrees | plus or minus 10 degrees | plus or minus 10 degrees |

Minimum range on ship targets is 800 to 1,000 yards, and on aircraft 1,100 to 1,700 yards [S143, part 4].

The Mark 12 and Mark 22 pair replaced the Mark 4 during 1945. Iowa carried Mark 4/22 on her Mark 37 directors by 8 July 1945 and still in August 1945 [S146]; navypedia records the change to four Mark 12/22 on Iowa in March 1945 and on New Jersey in June 1945 [S150]. Ordnance Pamphlet 1719 of 1949, the operating instructions for the Mark 37 system, lists separate pamphlets for radar equipments Mark 12, Mark 22, and Mark 25, and describes the system as working with Mark 12 alone against visible air targets and with Mark 12 and Mark 22 together against obscured ones [S145]. Mark 25 replaced the Mark 12/22 combination after the war, first on New Jersey's No. 1 director in the early 1950s while Nos. 2 and 3 kept the older sets [S147].

## Anti-aircraft directors

### Mark 51 and the Mark 14 sight

The Mark 51 is a light one-man director for the 40 mm and 1.1-inch guns, placed a short distance from the guns it controls so that it is free of gun vibration and smoke, and close enough to make horizontal and vertical parallax corrections unnecessary [S141, pp. 462, 470]. It has three parts: pedestal, head, and a modification of the Mark 14 gun sight [S141, p. 470]. Train transmitters and, on some modifications, an oil-filled impeller train damper sit in the pedestal; train is limited by mechanical stops to 375 degrees either side of centre [S141, p. 470]. The right handle carries the firing key [S141, p. 470]. Crew is two: a pointer and a range setter [S141, p. 471].

The Mark 14 sight computes lead angle and superelevation with two gyroscopic mechanisms driven by the motion of the sight as the operator tracks; the only manual operations are range setting, smooth tracking, and introducing spots [S141, p. 462]. On the 20 mm gun the Mark 14 is used directly, with a gunner and a range setter [S141, pp. 462, 471]. NavWeaps, in an essay by Gene Slover, adds that range was set at a fixed 5,000 yards on the early Mark 51, that the operator caged the gyro with a trigger on the left handlebar while searching and released it when tracking smoothly, and that fire was corrected by watching the tracers [S153].

On the class, the covered objects in the gun tubs of Iowa shortly before her February 1943 commissioning are identified as Mark 51 directors, with Director Mark 51 Mod 2 carrying Gun Sight Mark 14 Mod 2 [S146]. Mark 51 Mod and Mark 51 Mod 3 heads are both shown in equipment photographs on the same index [S146]. A 1945 caption on New Jersey notes the folded-down splinter shield for the Mark 51 director controlling the 40 mm mount on top of No. 3 turret [S147]. NavWeaps states that a Mark 51 director crew of a pointer and a range setter normally accompanied each twin or quad mount [S152], and that half of all Japanese aircraft shot down between 1 October 1944 and 1 February 1945 were credited to the Bofors and Mark 51 combination [S152].

### Mark 49

New Jersey was fitted with the Mark 49 director. Photographs from the records of Puget Sound Naval Shipyard taken at the Philadelphia Navy Yard on 29 October 1943 show the director from the rear with the operator's seat and controls, and a close-up of the enclosed operator compartment with a Mark 19 radar antenna; the caption states that both Mark 11 and Mark 19 radars were used, that early units lacked the radar, and that Navy documentation in late 1943 found the optical-only units no better than the Mark 51 and far more maintenance-hungry, their only advantage being at night and in low visibility with the radar fitted [S147]. NavWeaps says the Mark 49 was not very successful and was replaced by the Mark 51 and later the Mark 57 [S152].

### Mark 57

The Mark 57 is the Mark 51 with radar added and range fed in rather than fixed, which NavWeaps calls a blind-fire capability for the Bofors mounts, though the same essay notes the operator still had to see the target to track it [S152] [S153].

Dated fits: New Jersey had Mark 57 radar directors for her 40 mm guns by the time she was inclined at Puget Sound on 24 June 1945 [S147]. An August 1945 caption on Iowa shows two Mark 57 AA directors with their Mark 37 antennas flanking the No. 1 Mark 37 secondary battery director [S146].

### Mark 63

No source fetched for this facet places a Mark 63 director on any ship of the class. NavWeaps describes the Mark 63, along with the Mark 56, as post-war radar-equipped systems for the 40 mm gun, raising its maximum effective range from about 1,500 yards with Mark 51 control to about 2,500 yards, and notes that quad mount modifications marked with an asterisk carried a radar antenna and were used with the Mark 63 director [S152]. The one Mark 63 named on a NavSource Iowa-class page belongs to a Fletcher class destroyer photographed alongside New Jersey, not to the battleship [S147]. See `gaps-04.md`.

### 20 mm gunsights

The Mark 14 sight is the 20 mm gun's director, with a gunner and a range setter [S141, pp. 462, 471]. A photograph of a 20 mm crew on Iowa's forecastle during her 1943 shakedown shows the gunner using a Mark 14 lead-computing gun sight on the gun at right [S146]. The manual notes that ring sights remain for local control when the Mark 14 is out [S141, pp. 461, 470].

Minimum range on the A scope is 1,500 yards; PPI minimum is 2 1/2 miles on the 20-mile range and 6 miles on the 75-mile range [S143, part 4]. Resolution is 10 degrees in bearing and 500 yards in range [S143, part 4].

NavSource's caption for Iowa in July 1945 gives the SK antenna as a 17-foot square array detecting aircraft to about 100 nautical miles with a height capability at that range of 10,000 feet, and describes the SR as the first entirely new air search set since CXAM, with an antenna about 15 ft by 6 ft, similar in size to the SC-2 [S146]. It describes the SU as a higher-resolution X-band surface search set with a small dish in a radome, good for about 20 nautical miles on a battleship [S146].

## Radio

### Radio spaces

The Battleship New Jersey radio room restoration group states that before the 1980s modernization the BB-62 radio room was on the third deck, just aft of the No. 2 barbette, near sick bay, and that the present radio room on the main deck occupies what had been crew berthing, with the inner space now called FACCON 1 having been chief petty officer berthing and FACCON 2, now the ham shack, a passageway and part of the chaplain's office [S155]. The same museum group's member reference page lists arrangement drawings for the ship's Combat Engagement Center, Combat Information Center, Radio Central, and transmitter room, and a list of communications spaces, all describing the ship as preserved [S156].

No source fetched for this facet gives the wartime numbering of the radio rooms, the division of transmitter and receiver rooms, or the compartment numbers of radio central. See `gaps-04.md`.

A wartime photograph of the radio room aboard New Jersey exists and is dated. Naval History and Heritage Command photograph 80-G-469927, from the Admiral Carney collection, is captioned "Radio room aboard USS NEW JERSEY (BB-62), December 1944, en route to Philippine Islands. Captain L. Dow is at the telephone" [S154].

### Equipment types

One set is named for the class by a fetched source: the TBS. NavSource's caption on Iowa in July 1945 places a TBS antenna on the starboard yard [S146], and a caption on New Jersey in the early 1950s notes two extra TBS antennas added on the forward air defence platform [S147]. For the class in wartime, no fetched source names TBL, TBK, TCS, RAK, or RAL. Those sets are therefore described here only as the Navy's own 1946 training manual describes them, without any claim that the Iowa class carried them. See `gaps-04.md`.

Table: Navy radio sets of the period as NAVPERS 10172 (1946) describes them [S144, ch. 22 to 23]

| Set | Function | Figures |
|---|---|---|
| TBS | Short-range transmitter and receiver for ship-to-ship work in task forces and convoys; voice or M.C.W.; crystal-controlled, one antenna shared by transmitter and receiver through a press-to-talk relay | 50 watts; 60 to 80 mc band; receiver i.f. 5.3 mc; nominal range about the horizon |
| TBK | C.W. transmitter (the companion TBM adds a modulator for voice and M.C.W.) | 2,000 to 18,100 kc; about 500 watts at the low end falling to 300 watts at 18,100 kc |
| TBL | Two transmitters in one cabinet, an intermediate-frequency section and a high-frequency section sharing tubes through a transfer switch; C.W., M.C.W., or voice | 175 to 600 kc and 2,000 to 18,100 kc; 200 watts C.W., 100 watts M.C.W., 50 watts voice |
| RAK and RAL | Companion receivers, usually installed in pairs, with a low-pass audio filter switchable between 450 to 770 and 770 to 1,300 cycles | RAK 15 to 600 kc in 6 bands; RAL 0.3 to 23 mc in 9 bands |

The manual notes that the original RAK and RAL were built for direct current and that all later modifications run on 110/120 volt 60 cycle single-phase alternating current with provision for emergency direct current, and that modifications 6, 7, and 8 carry extra shielding against high-frequency radar interference [S144, ch. 23]. It recommends a separate antenna for each of the two receivers, and a loose input coupling when one antenna has to serve both [S144, ch. 23].

### Flag communications

New Jersey became a fleet flagship on 4 February 1944, when Admiral Raymond A. Spruance, commanding the Fifth Fleet, broke his flag from her main at Majuro, and again on 24 August 1944, when Admiral Halsey broke his flag in her as Commander, Third Fleet [S158]. The December 1944 radio room photograph was taken while she was Halsey's flagship and shows the fleet communications officer at the telephone [S154].

Two pieces of the ship were built for the flag. NavSource, drawing on Friedman, records that Iowa's conning tower had three levels and that the lowest was for the fleet commander [S146]. A pre-commissioning caption on the same page notes the antenna trunks on the flag bridge level forward of the after stack [S146].

No source fetched for this facet describes the flag radio spaces, the flag plot, or the extra circuits worked when the ship carried a fleet staff. See `gaps-04.md`.

The Mark III IFF system that the ski-pole and BK/BL designations belong to is described in the 1945 Radar Operator's Manual. It has two units. The BL, or BN, is a shipborne or land-based recognition radar mounted alongside the search radar, with an antenna, duplexer, transmitter, and receiver but no modulation generator or indicator of its own, using the search radar's timing system and indicator; its interrogator section challenges and its responsor section intercepts the answers [S143, part 2]. The transpondor that answers is the BK in shipboard installations and the ABK in aircraft, a compact unit weighing about 30 pounds, entirely automatic once switched on [S143, part 2]. A steering-wheel dipole antenna is generally used with the BK aboard ship and a single dipole with the ABK in aircraft [S143, part 2].

The BK-7 on Iowa's yardarms and the BL-5 panel above her SK antenna in 1945 photographs match that pairing [S146].

## Combat information center

No Iowa-specific source found; see `gaps-04.md`.

Nothing fetched for this facet states where the combat information center was in any ship of the class, when it was fitted, what it held, or who manned it. What follows is what the two service publications say about the CIC in general, and it is not offered as a description of these ships.

The 1944 gunnery manual defines the CIC as an agency for the collection, evaluation, and distribution of combat information, existing to assist command in planning a course of action and to assist command and fire control in executing it, and says explicitly that it is neither strange and complex nor merely another plotting room under a new name [S141, p. 501]. It divides the CIC into evaluation and control sections and lists what it can do for fire control: designate or suggest targets, coach gun control onto targets, provide an initial solution for the computing mechanisms in plot, control or warn the automatic weapons, and spot fall of shot when a gun control station asks [S141, pp. 501 to 502].

The 1945 Radar Operator's Manual repeats that definition and lists the position information that feeds the CIC and is displayed on the dead reckoning tracer, summary, and air plots: visual ranges and bearings, optical ranges and bearings, radar ranges and bearings, sound ranges and bearings, direction finder bearings, radar detection receiver bearings, fathometer depths, and SMSD indications [S143, part 5]. It adds that the CIC is broadly alike on all combatant ships but differs in size, in the number of radar sets, and in personnel allowance by ship type, and it prints typical layouts for battleships, carriers, light cruisers, and destroyers as figures [S143, part 5].

## Navigation and identification

### Identification, friend or foe

The Mark III system, with the shipborne BL or BN interrogator-responsor and the BK transpondor, is described under antennas above [S143, part 2]. Photographs identify a BK-7 on Iowa's yardarms and a BL-5 panel on top of her SK antenna in 1945, and ski-pole IFF antennas flanking her relocated SG [S146].

### Radio direction finder

A DBA radio direction finder stands on a topmast behind Iowa's SK antenna in a photograph of 8 July 1945, flanked port and starboard by two fighting lights [S146]. Postwar, DBM radar direction finders appear on the after funnel and on an enlarged mast attached to No. 2 stack [S146].

### Gyrocompass

The SG radar takes true bearing from the ship's gyrocompass system; when the gyro repeater system fails the operator throws a synchro switch to emergency and reads relative bearings only [S143, part 4]. The SC and SK sets have the same relative-true bearing switch, true putting the antenna under the ship's gyro system [S143, part 4]. The SG troubleshooting instructions tell the operator that if synchro excitation to the antenna control motor fails he should first shift the gyro-control panel from the forward gyro supply to the after gyro supply or the reverse, which establishes that a ship carrying SG had at least two gyro supplies [S143, part 4]. No source fetched for this facet names the gyrocompass model fitted in the Iowa class or says how many were carried. See `gaps-04.md`.

### Fathometer and pit log

Fathometer depths appear in the Radar Operator's Manual list of position information reaching the CIC [S143, part 5]. No source fetched for this facet names a fathometer or a pit log in the Iowa class. See `gaps-04.md`.

## Optical equipment and searchlights

### Conning tower and fire control tower

NavSource, drawing its caption text from Friedman, describes Iowa as inclined at Bayonne on 28 March 1943 with three conning tower levels, the lowest for the fleet commander, and says that as in other American battleships of the period the upper level was a fire control station with periscopes and radars protruding from it [S146]. Above it was the large platform of the primary conn; the mast structure held a small chart room supplementing the chart house at the navigating bridge level below; surface lookouts occupied the next level, with slits; and the upper platform was the forward air defence station, carrying two sky lookout positions and two target designators [S146]. A November 1943 caption notes the armor thickness visible at the conning tower hatch and the direct vision slots [S146].

The spotting glass and the two periscopes of the Mark 40 director sat on top of the fire control tower, the glass with a 15-foot base length, all trained together through about 300 degrees [S141, p. 451]. A 1945 photograph of New Jersey's conning tower shows one large periscope for the captain and two smaller ones, with a Mark 27 radar abaft them [S147].

### Director optics

Mark 38: trainer's, leveller's, and cross-leveller's telescopes, a 12-power spotter's periscope with 15 degrees of look above and below the deck plane, and the 26 1/2-foot stereoscopic rangefinder [S141, p. 446]. An exterior view labels the rangefinder windows at both arms, the trainer's telescope port, the cross-leveller's shutter, the pointer's telescope port, the hatch, and the periscope [S141, p. 446].

Mark 37: three movable-prism telescopes of the Mark 60 family behind three ports in the front plate, and a 15-foot Mark 42 stereoscopic rangefinder protruding through a vertical slot on each side [S141, pp. 393 to 394].

Turret optics are in `03-armament-and-ammunition.md`.

### Searchlights

Photograph captions give two sizes and two positions.

| Light | Position | Ship and date | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 36-inch | one port and one starboard of the stack | Iowa, shortly before commissioning, early 1943 | [S146] |
| 24-inch | after side of the forward air defence platform | Iowa, 28 March 1943 | [S146] |
| 36-inch | abaft the Mark 27 radar atop the conning tower | Missouri, 23 July 1944 | [S148] |
| 36-inch | worked by two crewmen during shakedown | Missouri, about August 1944 | [S148] |

Searchlight control was centred in the Mark 37 director [S141, p. 401]. Searchlight mounts are trained and elevated like guns; in surface fire the orders are essentially the train and elevation of the line of sight plus parallax corrections introduced at each mount, and in anti-aircraft fire they must also correct for tilt of the searchlight trunnions because the director's line of sight is stabilised in cross-level [S141, p. 401]. Later installations split the job between a searchlight control transmitter, on the shield behind the illumination officer, and a searchlight trunnion-tilt corrector under the control officer's telescope; the illumination officer adds arbitrary train and elevation offsets through two knobs, and a lever works an annunciator that sends the orders off dose, on dose, and on open to visual indicators at the mounts [S141, p. 401]. In still later directors the trunnion-tilt corrector was left out and the searchlights were no longer used against air targets [S141, p. 401].

The same director controls star shell. A star-shell computer attached to the main computer in plot works out elevation, train, and fuze-setting orders for the guns firing star shell, and a star-shell spot transmitter on the director shield to the left of the illumination officer sends range, elevation, and deflection spots to it [S141, pp. 401, 416].

## Conflicts between sources

1. **Turret rangefinder base length.** Naval Ordnance and Gunnery (1944) gives 43 feet for the turret rangefinder in a new-construction battleship, with a scale from 4,500 to about 70,000 yards [S141, p. 456]. NavWeaps gives the Iowa class 46-foot, 25-power instruments, Mark 52 stereoscopic in turrets II and III and Mark 53 coincidence in turret I [S151]. The manual is not class-specific and the NavWeaps page is; both figures are recorded.
2. **Number of secondary plotting rooms.** The 1944 manual offers two alternatives for battleships, one plotting room with four computer and stable element groups or two rooms with two each, and does not say which applies to any class [S141, p. 387]. A 1983 caption names a "forward secondary battery plot room" in New Jersey, which implies two, but describes the ship after her 1980s modernization [S147].
3. **Wisconsin's main battery radar in 1945.** navypedia records two Mark 8 removed and two Mark 13 added in May 1945 [S150]. No photograph caption on the NavSource Wisconsin index confirms or contradicts the date; its 21 March 1952 caption refers only to "her Mk.38 main battery director and various radar antennas" [S149].
4. **Iowa's air search set after the March 1945 refit.** navypedia lists both SC-2 and SK-2 added in March 1945 [S150]. NavSource states that Iowa received SK-2 postwar, noting it in a photograph of about mid-1946, and a caption on a late-1948 photograph identifies the installation as an SC-2 [S146].
