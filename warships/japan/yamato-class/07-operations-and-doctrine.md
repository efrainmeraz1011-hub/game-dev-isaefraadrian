# Yamato class: operations and doctrine

Status: partial (see gaps.md)

Two tier 1 reports carry this facet. US Naval Technical Mission report S-06-2 records how Yamato and Musashi were actually fought and how their damage control worked or failed, from interrogation of survivors including both ships' executive officers and Musashi's chief engineer. Report S-84(N), "Japanese Damage Control", written 4 February 1946 by Commander R. H. Hedgecock, describes the Imperial Japanese Navy's damage control system as an institution. S-84(N) names no Yamato-class ship in its body, so its detail is Navy-wide practice, not ship-specific; it does record that Captain J. Nomura, Yamato's last executive officer, was among the officers interviewed [S23, p. 3].

## Readiness conditions and closure

The mission recorded two named conditions aboard Yamato on her last sortie [S12, p. 11]:

- **Material readiness condition.** Set at 1800 on 6 April 1945 with one third of the crew at battle stations and the remainder sleeping in the vicinity of their battle stations. It was assumed because US submarines were reported off the Bungo Strait.
- **Complete closure.** All doors, hatches and ventilation closures tightly shut, including the escape scuttles in the lower portion of the vertical watertight doors, which the mission says the Japanese employed on all warships. Five to seven minutes were required to set complete closure from the cruising condition.

Yamato went to General Quarters on 7 April after an uncertain radar contact at about 1000 produced the order "Prepare to go to General Quarters", passed to all ships of the group [S12, p. 11]. Musashi went to General Quarters at 0600 on 24 October 1944, with a condition of complete closure reported set and speed at 20 knots [S12, p. 17].

Musashi's regular routine included two drills a day on setting a proper and complete condition of closure, which the mission thought made her crew exceptionally well trained by Japanese standards [S12, p. 17].

S-84(N) does not name or describe the Navy's watertight closure conditions and does not mention escape scuttles [S23].

## Gunnery procedure in action

The clearest surface-action sequence in the record is the Battle off Samar, 25 October 1944, as combinedfleet reconstructs it [S1]:

| Time | Event |
|---|---|
| 0523 | Yamato's Type 13 radar picks up enemy aircraft |
| 0544 | Enemy carriers sighted hull down, bearing 60 to port, range 23 miles, misidentified as six fleet carriers with three cruisers and two destroyers |
| 0545 | Yamato opens fire on aircraft |
| 0558 | Both forward turrets open fire at 20 miles. Of the six forward rifles only two were initially loaded with armour-piercing shell, the rest with Type 3. The F1M2 spotter confirms the first salvo as a hit. Three six-gun salvos are fired on the same target, then fire shifts to the next carrier, which is immediately concealed by destroyer smoke |
| 0606 | Yamato continues east firing her 155 mm secondary guns |
| 0651 | A charging cruiser emerges from the smoke; Yamato engages at more than 10 miles and hits with the first salvo |
| 0654 | Heermann fires three torpedoes at Haruna that run on toward Yamato. Yamato turns away to port and steams north for 10 miles until the torpedoes run out of fuel, which takes her and the force commander out of the battle |

Against aircraft the main battery fired Type 3 sankaidan. Yamato opened with main guns and Type 3 at 1026 on 24 October 1944 [S1], fired Type 3 alongside Nagato at about thirty B-24s on 26 October 1944 [S1], fired at the shadowing PBM Mariners at 1017 on 7 April 1945 [S24], and opened with her two forward turrets at 1234 that day against the first strike [S24]. By the second wave the shells were set to burst one second from the ship, about 3,000 yards, and US aircraft flew through them [S24]. US Navy pilots considered the round more of a visual spectacular than an effective anti-aircraft weapon [S5].

The mission was told that blast on the bridge was very severe and that salvo spreads at maximum range were about 500 to 600 yards [S5, quoting S11].

## Damage control organisation

### Above the ship

S-84(N) describes three bodies in the Navy Ministry [S23, p. 7]:

- **Technical Council**, drawn from the Navy Ministry, the Navy Technical Department and instructors from the damage control schools, for research, investigation and improvement of practices and equipment.
- **Battle Report Investigating Committee**, for quick application of lessons from battle reports, with sub-committees headed by the superintendents of the damage control schools.
- **Bureau of Naval Education**, for the education of naval personnel and to foster the use of approved practices aboard ship.

Three schools taught damage control: the Naval Navigation School (fire fighting, prevention of flooding, counter-ballasting, prevention of poisonous gases, handling of the wounded), the Naval Workshop and Repair School (repair of damage, water pumping) and the Naval Engineering School (repair of machinery, electrical wiring and equipment) [S23, p. 7]. Officer courses at all three ran one year, shortened to six months in wartime; enlisted higher courses ran nine months and ordinary courses six to nine months, each shortened in wartime [S23, p. 7]. All officers were educated to become commanding officers of damage control, and a student of one school had to attend each of the other two for about a month, two weeks in wartime [S23, p. 8].

Once a year the Navy Ministry observed the practice of damage control in the fleet, using observers from the Bureau of Education and the schools [S23, p. 8]. Every fleet and squadron had a damage control staff, held as additional duty to regular navigation or engineering duty: one captain or commander, three to four lieutenants and ten to fifteen petty officers for leading exercises, plus one technical captain or commander to inspect and advise on permanently installed equipment. A ship's captain within a squadron was appointed Leader of Damage Control, responsible for improving practice [S23, p. 8].

### Aboard a battleship

S-84(N) prints an organisation chart headed "example of BB or large CV" [S23, pp. 9 to 11]:

- The captain, under him the chief commanding officer for damage control, described as the vice-captain.
- Five parallel positions under that officer: Commander of Defense, Chief Gunnery Officer, Chief Torpedo Officer, Commander of Machinery, Chief Communication Officer.
- The Defense Section comprised four sections: Emergency, Flooding, Electrical and Auxiliary Machinery, each with its own commander and sub-section commanders.
- The Emergency Section had a First and a Second Emergency Section Commander. Under the First were the First, Second and Third Emergency Sub-Section Commanders controlling the First through Sixth Emergency Groups; under the Second were the Fourth, Fifth and Sixth Sub-Section Commanders controlling the Seventh through Twelfth Emergency Groups. An emergency group consisted of 16 to 24 men, drawn from seamen, industry-men, engineer-men and paymaster-men.
- The Flooding and Pumping Section had a commander over Forward, Midpart and After Sections, each with control members; control members numbered about 20.
- The Electrical Section had a commander over forward and after section commanders, with about 15 to 20 members.
- The Auxiliary Machinery Section had the same shape, with about 15 members.

The report uses the term "wreckage party", not "repair party". Where an emergency occurred near personnel at their battle stations they generally handled it themselves; the wreckage party existed primarily to handle emergencies in compartments where no personnel had battle stations, although some damage might require it to assist personnel at their stations [S23, p. 11].

S-84(N) does not state the number or shipboard location of damage control stations, beyond referring to major and secondary damage control stations and to flooding and pumping stations [S23, pp. 13, 83 note]. Shinano had a second damage control station on the deck below the armoured deck to starboard between frames 104 and 112 [S12, p. 26].

### Drills

Each section of the wreckage party was instructed individually, then the whole party acted on a simulated emergency, then the entire ship's company was exercised in simulated emergencies once or twice a week. The emergency was planned in advance and a selected committee led the training throughout the ship. Trained committees from one ship were sent to other ships in the squadron to conduct and observe simulated emergencies [S23, pp. 11 to 12].

Almost all instruction for an emergency began by stating that the fundamental doctrine was to fight the ship, and then dealt with the particular emergency [S23, p. 11].

Fire fighting was taught at Yokosuka Naval Station on four old ships. It consisted of extinguishing a gasoline fire with foam, then extinguishing a similar fire by shutting the compartment and letting the fire burn itself out while cooling the outside with water, with oral instruction on how to attack each kind of fire [S23, p. 11].

## Damage control equipment

From S-84(N)'s list of standard equipment [S23, pp. 12 to 13]:

| Category | Equipment |
|---|---|
| Fire main | Capable of being sectionalised into 3 to 5 groups during battle |
| Pumps | Portable hand pumps, 4 to 6 sets, each 12 tons per hour; portable gas-driven pumps of 12, 50, 150 and 250 tons per hour |
| Fixed firefighting | Sprinkling apparatus in magazines and carrier hangars |
| Portable firefighting | CO2 gas discharging apparatus carried to the fire; portable foam equipment worked from portable pumps or fire-main outlets |
| Personnel protection | Smoke defence masks; fire-proof robes for wreckage parties; Type 93 or 97 gas mask for every person aboard; gas-proof robes for topside personnel |
| Flooding detection | Sounding tubes for oil tanks; air vent lines and valves in the flooding and pumping compartment. The only way to tell whether water was entering a counter-ballast void was to listen for air escaping through the valve or to remove the tank top |
| Flooding control | Flooding valves worked by hydraulic oil or by handwheel; flooding prevention boxes of various kinds and sizes, made of wood, to be put over damage holes; shores for bulkheads and for the boxes |
| Drainage | Drain pumps, either firemain pumps with suction open to the compartment, or portable pumps |
| Gas defence | Each compartment made gas-tight; air purifying apparatus for important compartments; gas detector; gas tent for training; "smell" training substances; gas first aid boxes |
| Repair stores | Supplies of wood and steel; emergency tackles and torches for removing damaged topside equipment |

Control instruments at a damage control station were the emergency command board, which was a flooding diagram, and a flooding and pumping control board showing pipe lines, valves and pumps with pegs to indicate the condition of any tank or void. Both were to be provided at major and secondary damage control stations, and the flooding and pumping board also at flooding and pumping stations. Other instruments were a draft meter receiver, clinometer, helm angle report receiver, speed telegraph receiver, flooding and pumping telegraph receiver, air pipe transmitter, telephones, loudspeaker, voice tube and a GM indicator [S23, p. 13].

## Counterflooding in practice

Yamato's and Musashi's outboard voids and machinery spaces had remotely operated ten-inch sea valves, and the outboard voids had air escapes used to indicate flooding [S12, p. 9].

Musashi held special counterflooding drills in which an actual list of 1 degree had to be removed. These found that four or five outboard voids were needed to remove a 1-degree list, about one half to one third of the list Yamato took from a single torpedo in December 1943 [S12, p. 17].

Yamato's counterflooding on 7 April 1945 ran as follows: the 5 to 6 degree list from the first attack was brought to 1 degree by flooding starboard outboard voids; the 15 to 16 degree list from the second was brought to 5 degrees, using up all the starboard voids; after the third attack the executive officer ordered starboard outboard firerooms Nos. 3 and 11 and the starboard hydraulic machinery room flooded, then the starboard outboard engine room, leaving a 22 to 23 degree list [S12, pp. 12 to 14]. The NHHC essay records that there was insufficient time to warn the engineering spaces and that several hundred crewmen were drowned as a result [S24].

The mission's verdict: counterflooding capacity was limited to little more than righting the ship after three torpedoes on one side; counterflooding the outboard engineering spaces was an extreme measure; at a 16 degree list the outboard voids could be filled only to about 55 per cent from the sea; there was no damage control pumping system of adequate capacity; and liquid-filled outboard layers would have been preferable [S12, p. 16].

Musashi's voids had steam eductors rated at 200 tons an hour but no attempt was made to pump, and when flooding reached the storerooms there were no sea valves there and few fire pumps available [S12, pp. 19 to 20].

## The mission's overall assessment

S-06-2 graded the four ships it examined: damage control performance was reasonably good aboard Musashi, mediocre aboard Yamato, and conspicuously poor aboard Taiho and Shinano. Faith in counterflooding outboard voids was misplaced, and counterflooding was inadequate aboard both Yamato and Shinano. That Musashi took four hours to sink shows that hits distributed about equally on both sides were less lethal [S12, p. 2].

S-84(N) is blunter about the institution: compared to American and British standards, Japanese damage control organisation, training and equipment were inferior; definite improvement was made during the war in all three phases, but good results were never obtained. The report calls itself a negative report, in that nothing was uncovered that could be used to improve US Navy damage control procedure or training [S23, pp. 1, 5].

The Japanese committee that investigated Shinano's loss found that counterflooding was ordered too late, that the torpedoes ran shallow at less than 3 metres, that the joint in the side protection was weak, that the ship was unfinished and that the crew was undertrained [S12, p. 28]. The Japanese record in S-06-3 adds that the crew was overconfident about the hull's resistance to damage, that the belief was prevalent that four torpedoes could not sink her, and that when the crisis came proper and timely measures were not taken [S13, p. 21].

## Fleet doctrine and the Ten-ichi-go order

The order received by the Second Fleet at 1359 on 5 April 1945 read: "The Surface Special Attack Unit is ordered to proceed via Bungo Suido Channel at dawn on Y-1 day to reach the prescribed holding position for a high-speed run-in to the area west of Okinawa at dawn on Y-day. Your mission is to attack the enemy fleet and supply train and destroy them. Y-day is April 8th." [S1]

Under the plan, Yamato, Yahagi and eight destroyers formed a Surface Special Attack Force, the term "special attack" being understood to mean suicide. They were to sortie on 6 April, transit to Okinawa on 7 April with only a few hours of minimal air cover, arrive in the US transport area off south-western Okinawa on 8 April, sink as many transports as possible, beach themselves and keep firing while ammunition lasted, then land the crews to fight ashore alongside the army [S24].

The screen used circling tactics. When Yamato went to 24 knots after 1200 on 7 April the screen commenced what the mission calls the usual Japanese circling tactics [S12, p. 11]. The force zigzagged at 22 knots through the Bungo Suido and assumed an anti-submarine formation on a submarine report [S1].

## Refuelling

The record shows replenishment from tankers, from other capital ships and from escorts rather than any specific underway refuelling rig. Yamato and Mutsu each transferred 4,500 tons of fuel oil to refuel other warships [S1]. Tanker Nichiei Maru refuelled Yamato at Kure on 2 July 1944 [S1]. Tanker Mitsushima Maru delivered about 1,000 tons to Yamato at the Tokuyama Navy Fuel Depot [S1]. Yamato received fuel from the destroyers Hanazuki and Asashimo at Kure before the last sortie, and took on 3,400 tons at the Tokuyama Oil Depot on 6 April 1945 [S1]. Musashi burned about 1,000 tons of fuel oil on passage from Lingga and transferred another 800 tons to accompanying destroyers [S12, p. 17].

## Conflicts between sources

- Musashi's damage control performance. S-06-2 rates it reasonably good [S12, p. 2] and calls her crew exceptionally well trained by Japanese standards [S12, p. 17], while S-84(N) rates the Navy's damage control as a whole inferior to US and British standards with good results never obtained [S23, p. 1]. The two statements are about different scopes and are recorded here as printed.
