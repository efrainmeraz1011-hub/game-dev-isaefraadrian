# Littorio class: hull and armor

Status: covered, with gaps listed at the end

## Hull form and dimensions

Littorio and Vittorio Veneto were 224.05 m between perpendiculars and 237.76 m (780 ft 1 in) overall; Roma and Impero were 240.68 m (789 ft 8 in) overall. All four had a beam of 32.82 m (107 ft 8 in) and a draft of 9.6 m (31 ft 6 in). The ships were built with bulbous bows to gain speed; the bows caused serious vibration and had to be modified [S29, pp. 289-290].

The Marina Militare data sheets give 237.8 m overall and 224.5 m between perpendiculars for Littorio and Vittorio Veneto, 240.7 m overall and 227.5 m between perpendiculars for Roma and Impero, a beam of 32.9 m and a mean draft of 10.5 m at full load [S7; S8; S9; S10].

The most visible difference between the second pair and the first was the bow line: Roma and Impero had a higher forecastle and so a more pronounced sheer. The stern was of cruiser form, slightly more rounded in Roma and Impero. All four carried three rudders, one main rudder on the centreline and two auxiliary rudders set between the inner and outer shafts of the four screws [S19]. Roma's bow was further modified during building, on the experience of Vittorio Veneto, to give a finer waterline ending [S28, pp. 171-172].

US naval intelligence in February 1943 recorded the class as 775 ft 7 in overall and 762 ft 4 in on the waterline [S17].

## Armour scheme

The main belt was designed and tested to keep out 381 mm armour-piercing shells at ranges down to 16,000 m, taken as the inner edge of the useful battle range [S25, p. 35].

The belt was a composite. Outboard was a 70 mm homogeneous plate whose job was to strip the cap off an incoming armour-piercing shell. Behind it, 250 mm inboard, stood the 280 mm cemented main belt. The 250 mm gap was filled with a cement foam called Cellulite, which kept water out of the gap and helped the decapping action; Italian assessment was that the 250 mm of Cellulite did the work of a 600 mm void. The main belt was mounted on 150 mm of oak and a 15 mm steel backing plate, and the whole belt structure was inclined at 11 to 15 degrees depending on the part of the hull. A 36 mm homogeneous plate stood 1.4 m inboard of the belt, and 4 m further in a 24 mm plate sloped 26 degrees the other way [S25, p. 35].

The belt was 3.8 m high between the end barbettes, with 1.5 m of it below water at full load, and was inclined 11 degrees outward at the top; total thickness through the whole arrangement is given as 409 mm [S22].

The citadel was closed by transverse bulkheads of 100 to 210 mm forward and 70 to 280 mm aft [S29, p. 290]. The Italian account gives 210 mm forward and 290 mm aft, sited forward of the No. 1 turret magazine and abaft the No. 3 turret magazine [S19]. The hull above the citadel was an armoured casemate with 70 mm plating; the bow carried a 130 mm belt running 35 m ahead of the main belt and ending in a 60 mm transverse bulkhead; shafts, after diesel generator groups and steering gear had 100 mm plating and a separate 200 mm bulkhead abaft the citadel [S25, p. 34; S29, p. 290]. The Italian account gives the belt reducing to 60 mm forward and 100 mm aft [S19].

Below the third deck neither the main nor the secondary barbettes were armoured [S25, p. 61; S26, pp. 420-421].

The armoured tube inside the conning tower ran from the damage-control central to the admiral's bridge. It carried men between levels of the conning tower and the cable runs that carried every order and every report about weapons, machinery and safety systems [S19].

Where the boiler uptakes pierced the horizontal armour, the armour was replaced by gratings of plates set edge-on side by side, so that the funnel openings retained some protection against bombs [S19].

## Armour trials

The armour scheme was proof-fired in May 1935 at the Cottrau range at La Spezia. It held against 406 mm armour-piercing projectiles fired from 24,000 m, against 1,280 kg aircraft bombs of limited penetrating power but large explosive charge, and against 835 kg armour-piercing bombs, both bomb types at an impact velocity of 250 m/sec, which was the maximum natural terminal velocity of the day since rocket-boosted bombs did not yet exist [S19].

## Pugliese underwater protection system

All four ships used the underwater protection system designed by General Umberto Pugliese of the Genio Navale, who is also named on the official ship sheets as the designer of the class [S7; S8; S9; S10].

A 40 mm torpedo bulkhead ran inboard from the foot of the main belt and then curved down to meet the bottom of the hull. The void so formed held an empty drum 3,800 mm across with 6 mm walls, and the rest of the void was filled with liquid. The drum ran the length of the system and was meant to collapse under the explosion, taking up the energy; the torpedo bulkhead then kept splinters and blast out of the ship's vitals. The system was designed against warheads of up to 350 kg [S29, p. 290]. The system was 7.6 m deep [S22].

The Italian description agrees on the geometry and adds the working detail. The cylinder had a maximum diameter of 3.80 m and lay below the waterline along the ship's sides over the length of the armoured citadel. Diaphragms tied it to the outer hull and to the inner hull, the latter being splinter protection 28 to 40 mm thick. The cylinder was watertight and held only air, while the space between outer and inner hull was filled with fresh water, for drinking, for boiler feed or for washing, or with fuel oil; as those liquids were consumed they were replaced with sea water. For the system to work the space around the cylinder had to stay full and the inside of the cylinder had to stay empty. The pressure of a torpedo burst was passed in every direction by the liquid and so was applied over the whole surface of the cylinder until it ruptured; the walls of the cylinder, weaker than everything around them, collapsed and absorbed most of the blast energy, protecting the vertical bulkhead of the inner hull [S19].
### How it performed

Roma was never hit by a torpedo. Vittorio Veneto and Littorio were torpedoed several times, and when the hit fell in the stretch covered by the armoured citadel the Pugliese structure worked well [S19].

The contrary reading is that the system did not perform as expected [S29, p. 290], for two reasons: the riveted joint connecting the inner torpedo bulkhead to the bottom of the hull was not strong enough to take the shear of a contact explosion and failed even on non-contact bursts, which stopped the drum collapsing as designed and let flooding spread; and the fineness of the hull prevented the full 3,800 mm from being carried through the whole citadel, the drum narrowing to 2,280 mm abreast the main battery, with the absorbing capacity falling as the diameter fell [S26, pp. 422-423].

That reading is disputed. A summary of the system repeats the riveted-joint failure and adds that the curved structure was hard to build and to repair [S23]. In the comments on the same page a reader states that the recent Bagnasco and de Toro work finds no riveted part in the system, only welding, and no evidence that the Pugliese system was harder or slower to repair than a layered system; the author answers that he finds riveting recorded on p. 57 of that book and no side-by-side study either way [S23].

At Taranto on the night of 11 to 12 November 1940 Littorio took three torpedoes, two forward and one aft. The after hit destroyed the rudder and the shock damaged the steering gear. The two forward hits flooded heavily and the ship settled by the bows with her decks awash as far as the main battery turrets. She could not be docked until 11 December because a fourth torpedo, unexploded, was found under her keel, and lifting it was slow work since any change in the magnetic field around it might set off its magnetic pistol. Repairs ran to 11 March 1941 [S28, p. 175].

In the Italian account of the same night, Littorio was torpedoed on the bow to starboard and aft to port in the first wave, and hit a third time in the second wave [S15].

Vittorio Veneto took one aerial torpedo at Cape Matapan on 28 March 1941 and flooded with about 4,000 t of water; she was under way again after ten minutes and reached Taranto on 29 March [S28, p. 174].

In June 1942 a torpedo from a Wellington struck Littorio's starboard bow; about 1,500 long tons of water entered and the crew counter-flooded 350 long tons to correct the list [S26, p. 402].

## Conflicts between sources

Belt description: a single 350 mm plate inclined inboard toward the bottom [S19], against the two-plate arrangement of a 70 mm decapping plate and a 280 mm main plate separated by 250 mm of Cellulite [S25, p. 35]. The Marina Militare sheets quote a single vertical figure of 350 mm [S7; S8; S9; S10], which is the sum of the two plates.

Splinter bulkhead behind the belt: 36 mm at 1.4 m with a second plate of 24 mm sloped 26 degrees the other way at 4 m [S25, p. 35], against a 36 mm splinter bulkhead at 1.4 m and a second of 25 mm inclined 27 degrees inward [S22].

Transverse bulkheads closing the citadel: 100 to 210 mm forward and 70 to 280 mm aft [S29, p. 290], against 210 mm forward and 290 mm aft [S19], against 200 mm forward and 280 mm aft reducing to 70 mm below the main deck [S22].

Turret face: 380 mm [S25, p. 61], against 350 mm [S22; S19].

Pugliese joint: riveted and too weak [S26, pp. 422-423; S23], against welded throughout with the riveting claim treated as a myth [S23, reader comment].

Pugliese performance: worked well when the hit fell within the citadel [S19], against did not perform as effectively as expected [S29, p. 290].

Deck armour over the magazines: 150 mm on 12 mm plating [S25, p. 34], against 162 mm quoted as the main deck figure [S29, p. 289], against a Marina Militare summary of 207 mm horizontal [S7].
