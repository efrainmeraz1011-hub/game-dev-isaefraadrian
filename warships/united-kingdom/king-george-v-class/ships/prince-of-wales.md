# HMS Prince of Wales (pennant 53)

The only ship of the class lost, and the only one whose wreck has been surveyed, which makes her the best documented member for hull damage and for how the class behaved under damage. The timeline is in facet 08.

## Completion and working up

Built by Cammell Laird at Birkenhead, laid down 1 January 1937, launched 3 May 1939 [S23]. She commissioned on 19 January 1941 incomplete and with half her crew, and completed on 31 March 1941 [S2], [S23]. She was at Rosyth from January to March 1941 for fitting out, receiving Type 281, Type 284 and four Type 285 radars [S2]; Type 282, the close-range anti-aircraft control set, went to sea for the first time in her [S12].

Her state of readiness when she sailed against Bismarck is stated in the Commander-in-Chief's despatch. She joined the fleet on 25 March 1941 with many of the contractor's workmen still on board; it was not until 27 April that the last of her turrets could be accepted from the contractor and practice drills with the whole main armament could start; her captain reported on 17 May that he considered the ship fit to operate, but neither he nor the Commander-in-Chief read that as meaning fully worked up [S15]. Her own chronology dates the acceptance of the last turret from Vickers-Armstrong to 27 March 1941 [S2]. Tovey's judgement was blunt: "I did not and do not consider that in her then state of efficiency the Prince of Wales was a match for the Bismarck" [S15].

## The Denmark Strait, 24 May 1941

The turret and hoist defects are set out gun by gun in facet 03, from her own gunnery appendix [S14]. In summary: she fired 55 of a possible 74 guns in 18 salvoes in the first action, a 25.65 per cent loss of output; 41 of 48 in 12 salvoes in the second; and five rounds in the third [S14]. She opened fire at a true range of 25,000 yards and ceased at 14,500 yards [S14].

Neither her radar nor her rangefinders gave her a range before opening fire. No results were obtained from either the Type 281 or the Type 284; Signal School officers later concluded that the Type 281 had suffered interference and the Type 284 was defective [S14]. The rangefinders could not develop a satisfactory range plot, the fore director control tower's 15-foot instrument being the only one with a reasonable chance against the high closing rate, and the 'A' and 'B' turret rangefinders could see the enemy's superstructure only briefly [S14]. Two down ladders were needed to find the target [S14].

She was hit by four 15-inch and three smaller, probably 8-inch, shells. Her compass platform was damaged and most of the people on it killed or wounded; both forward high-angle directors and the starboard after one were put out of action; one four-gunned turret jammed; and she was holed underwater aft, taking in 400 tons of water and unable to exceed 27 knots [S15]. She turned away under smoke at 0613 and had cleared most of the debris from the bridge and resumed conning from the compass platform by 0720, with two guns of 'Y' turret back in action [S15].

The 5.25-inch battery had come into action at 18,600 yards when a 15-inch shell passed through the superstructure supporting the high-angle directors, jamming the fore director temporarily in training; the control officer ordered all mountings into after control, but a 15-inch shell then burst on the boat deck and seriously upset the after starboard high-angle director, whose crew had already been badly blasted by 'Y' turret firing on a forward bearing. By the time control was regained the target was lost behind smoke astern. A careful inquiry into why the fore director jammed reached no satisfactory explanation; the director was free after the action, and the report considers that the locking bolt may have jumped down and the control officer been too hasty, or that the shaking may have made the director bind temporarily [S14].

When the cruisers engaged from astern at 1840 that evening, Tovey records that "the unreliability of the Prince of Wales' armament was demonstrated once more, as two guns again went out of action" [S15].

## Atlantic Charter

In August 1941 she carried the Prime Minister to Placentia Bay and was photographed off Argentia, Newfoundland, from USS Augusta [S51].

## Force Z, 10 December 1941

Sunk off Kuantan by Japanese aircraft, sinking at 1320 after capsizing to port, with 327 lost of 1,612 aboard [S2], [S24], [S25]. She reported 3,390 tons of oil on board the previous day, under ADM 199/1149 [S26].

### The fatal hit

The first torpedo struck at about 1144, a few metres abaft the point where the port outer stern tube left the hull, at about Frame 280, tearing a ragged hole roughly four metres high and six metres wide just abaft the stern tube [S24], [S25]. At that moment the ship was doing 25 knots with the port outer shaft turning at 204 revolutions [S24]. The explosion weakened both arms of the outer port shaft strut and damaged the shaft and stern tube; the propeller kept turning out of centre, wobbling and putting further stress on the strut [S24].

The engineer officer of 'B' Engine Room did not know how bad the damage was. He tripped the emergency bulkhead stop valve, then admitted 100 lb of steam astern to stop the turbines, then reduced and cut off astern steam; the shaft trailed in, so he believed shaft and propeller were still functioning and worked the steam back up to 158 revolutions ahead. Going aft to inspect, he found the bulkhead stuffing gland at Frame 184 wrecked and water pouring through, and realised the whole line of shafting was running eccentrically under power and destroying watertight integrity along its length [S24]. The power control room recorded no revolutions on the port outer shaft at 1151 [S24].

A stoker testified to the Bucknill Committee that he saw the shaft break in 'Y' Action Machinery Room. Senior naval architects and marine engineers found it hard to believe that a 17.5-inch line shaft would actually break, and his testimony was challenged [S24]. The 2007 survey and the 2008 and 2009 penetrations of the shaft tunnel proved him right: the port shafting was separated at numerous flanges, the transverse bulkheads along the shaft alley had large elongations where the shaft passed through, and eventually the strut and propeller broke away with about 17 metres of shafting aft of the last flange joint, ripping the stern tube out of the hull. Debris from that failure entered the inner port propeller race and damaged its blades [S24]. The analysis attributes the parting of five sections of shafting to the shaft rotating out of centre, the bolted connections having been designed for torque loading rather than a combination of tensile and torque loads [S24].

### The consequences

Of eight generators, six 330 kW direct-current turbo-dynamos and two 330 kW diesel dynamos, only three turbo-dynamos were working when the second torpedo attack began at 1219 [S24]. The power loss took internal communications, ventilation, steering gear and pumps, and the training and elevation of the 5.25-inch mountings and the pom-poms [S24]. The list reached 11.5 degrees to port with a trim by the stern of 5 feet (1.5 m), reduced to 9 degrees by counter-flooding by 1210, in part caused by the after port 5.25-inch magazine crews flooding their own magazines in the belief that the noise of the shaft failing was another torpedo [S24]. The forward starboard 5.25-inch mountings could not depress low enough to engage the torpedo aircraft; the after group had no power at all; only S1 and S2 were manageable, and even they were compromised by the list; the crews could not train the other mountings by hand with chains, and the pom-pom crews had trouble getting their mountings into manual operation [S24]. She fired at the high-level bombing attack that began at 1241 with S1 and S2; the three 14-inch turrets were never used in any of the attacks [S24]. She hoisted the "Out of Control" signal at 1210, and with only the starboard engines turning and the steering unresponsive she could make 15 knots at best and was virtually unmanageable [S24].

A gunner's mate in P3 described the port torpedo explosion as "tons of plate glass shattering on a pavement", and the mounting lost all power while training aft [S24].

### The second attack

Three torpedoes struck the starboard side in quick succession, with warheads of 205 kilograms, larger than the first [S24]. One struck at the bow almost directly beneath the forward starboard anchor, tearing a hole about seven metres across right through the ship and out to port, leaving about two metres of split plating to the bow and a fracture through the stem post, flooding the two peak tanks immediately [S24]. One struck near Frame 109 abreast 'B' turret, where the inner side protection voids had already been counter-flooded and the captain had reluctantly flooded the outer voids as well; with water in the voids the system could not work as designed and much of the explosion vented into the ship, which may have caused the collapse and breakage of gear in the cordite and shell-handling rooms of 'B' turret and in the 14-inch transmitting station. A degaussing generator appears to have been hurled through the middle deck, leaving an irregular hole about 1.25 metres across at Frame 109 under a washroom [S24].

## The inquiries and the surveys

The Second Bucknill Committee met in February 1942 to determine the causes of the loss; its reports are held at Kew as ADM 116/4521 and ADM 116/4554 [S28], [S29], [S24].

Expedition Job 74 dived the wreck in May 2007 and ran a video survey of the whole hull; its findings on the number and position of the torpedo holes, and on the dished and split plating where hits had been reported but no holes exist, are in facet 01 [S25]. The 2019 Ministry of Defence survey found the wreck upside down in 68 m of water, oriented 016/196 degrees with a list to port of between 10 and 15 degrees, and heavily damaged by illegal salvage: the midships bottom hollowed out to the level of the seabed with the engine rooms as the focus, the propellers removed, the fuel tanks in most of the ship compromised, 'B' turret's magazine likely entirely removed, and intact shells and cordite rods scattered across the wreck site [S26]. No human remains were encountered during that survey [S26].

## Design changes made because of her

After the Bismarck battles her 14-inch mountings and those of King George V had modifications to the ammunition supply safety interlock system, improved watertightness of the mantlet plates and enlarged and additional shell-room drains, and their 5.25-inch mountings had their mantlet plate watertightness improved; the changes were worked into the rest of the class during building [S6], [S8]. After her loss, the surviving ships had duplicated power leads to the 5.25-inch mountings and extra protection around the cable entries [S8]. Vanguard, then building, was given Bofors in place of the pom-poms, dual steam-line connections to the steering gear for emergency steering, and increased separation between the inner and outer shafts [S24].

## Conflicts between sources

- Date the last turret was accepted from the contractor. 27 March 1941 [S2]; 27 April 1941 [S15].
- Number of lost. 327 of 1,612 aboard [S2], [S24], [S25]; navypedia gives 513 men lost [S23].
- Number of torpedo hits. Her own survivors and British accounts recorded at least six, four to starboard and two to port; the 2007 survey confirmed four penetrating holes, three to starboard and one to port; Japanese aircrews claimed seven of fourteen launched [S25]. Navypedia gives five in one sentence and six in another on the same page [S23].
- Number of shells she hit Bismarck with on 24 May. The tier 3 monograph says two hits from a straddle with her third or fourth salvo [S69]; navypedia says three hits [S23]; Tovey states that he had been unable to observe for certain any hits on Bismarck [S15].
