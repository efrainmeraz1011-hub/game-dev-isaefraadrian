# HMS King George V (pennant 41)

Name ship of the class and the only one for which a substantial run of original drawings and models was located. The timeline is in facet 08; this file holds what is specific to her.

## Name

By tradition the first capital ship of a new reign takes the monarch's name, so she was to have been HMS King George VI. The King asked instead that she be named in honour of his late father, after whom an earlier class of battleships had been named in 1911; a later ship of this class was named Duke of York in honour of the reigning King instead [S18].

## Build and completion

Laid down at the Walker Navy Yard, Newcastle-upon-Tyne, by Vickers-Armstrong on 1 January 1937, launched by the King on 21 February 1939, commissioned with pendant number 41 on 1 October 1940 [S18]. In October 1940 she was at Rosyth for the fitting of her outer propellers, strengthening of the rudder, and completion of her armament and radar [S1]. Her first-of-class and acceptance trials at Scapa on 2 to 4 December 1940 included gun trials that exposed defects in the loading mechanisms, before any of the class had fired in action [S1].

## Dimensions and weights as built

Her figures are the ones used for the class throughout facet 01: standard 38,031 tons and deep load 42,237 tons in 1940, rising to 39,100 and 44,460 tons by 1945 [S18], with a weight breakdown of hull 13,830 tons, outfit 1,619 tons, machinery 2,768 tons, armament 7,401 tons and armour 12,413 tons [S18]. The tier 3 monograph gives her shaft power as 111,700 shp (83.3 MW) for 28 knots and her complement as 1,409 [S69].

## Gunnery in the Bismarck action

She fired 339 shells in the final action of 27 May 1941, against 380 from Rodney [S6]. She began well, at 1.7 salvoes per minute under radar control, having opened fire at 0850; from 0920 things went wrong. 'A' turret was out of action for 30 minutes after about 23 rounds per gun, through a jam between the fixed and revolving structure in the shell room, and 'Y' turret was out for 7 minutes through drill errors. Both guns in 'B', guns 2 and 4 in 'A' and gun 2 in 'Y' were jammed and remained so for the rest of the action, five guns of ten; gun 3 of 'A' misfired twice and was out for 30 minutes before its breech could safely be opened [S6].

Fuel, not gunnery, ended the action for her. By the evening of 26 May she had 1,200 tons remaining, 32 per cent, and her speed was reduced to 22 knots at 1705 to economise; the Commander-in-Chief broke off the gun action on 27 May while Bismarck was still afloat because the shortage had become acute and he had to keep a reserve to return at speed through waters where U-boats were concentrating and heavy air attack was expected [S15]. He learned of her sinking at 1100, though it had been seen from King George V's after director control tower [S15].

One communications limitation is recorded in the same despatch. In King George V, wireless transmission on power on certain wavelengths interfered with reception on radio direction finding, which is why the Admiralty could not be kept fully informed during the action [S15].

## The Punjabi collision

On 1 May 1942 she collided in fog with the destroyer Punjabi off Iceland at 25 knots. Punjabi was cut in half and sank, her depth charges exploding; 49 were lost. King George V was left with a gash of about 40 ft in the bow and was repaired at Gladstone Dock, Liverpool, from May to June 1942 [S1], [S30]. An official photograph shows the hole in her bows, taken from the destroyer Wheatland at Seidesfjord, Iceland [S44].

## Remote power control and the 1945 bombardments

Most references state that no ship of the class ever received remote power control for the main armament, but one source states that King George V had received RP 40 before joining the British Pacific Fleet in 1945 [S6]. Her gunnery in the Japanese mainland bombardments was still troubled. On her first firing mission against the Hitachi works she was to fire 10 rounds of HE per gun and missed 15 through trouble with flashtight doors and interlocks; at Hamamatsu on 29 July 1945 she was to fire 300 rounds with reduced charges and fired 265 because of misfires in the lock firing mechanisms [S6]. The museum guide gives the Hamamatsu shoot as opening at 2319 at a range of 20,075 yards and ceasing at 2356, with 265 rounds fired for seven hits, the last time she fired her main armament in anger [S18].

She was photographed at sea off Japan on 16 August 1945 in company with United States Third Fleet ships, with USS Missouri beyond her, from the carrier Bon Homme Richard [S52].

## Radar fits

Type 271 was added in 1941; late in 1941 it came out and Type 273 and five Type 282 sets went in; four Type 285 sets followed in mid-1942. In July 1944 Type 273 and Type 279 came out and Type 277, Type 279B, two Type 282, Type 285 and Type 293 went in, along with the removal of the catapult and aircraft [S23].

## Conflicts between sources

- Her commissioning and completion dates are recorded in facet 08. The museum guide's statistics block gives completion as 1 October 1940 while its own timeline has her joining the Home Fleet on 11 December 1940 [S18].
- Whether she carried remote power control in 1945. Most references say no ship of the class ever did; one says she had RP 40 [S6].
