# Shinano

Third Yamato-class hull, laid down as Hull No. 110 and completed as an aircraft carrier. The US Naval Technical Mission described her as "built on a YAMATO Class hull" [S12, p. 25].

The Technical Mission recorded the commissioning date as 18 November [S12, p. 26]; combinedfleet gives 19 November [S3].

## Conversion

The original schedule called for launch in October 1943, guns in April 1944 and commissioning in March 1945. Work slowed from 12 August 1941 and was halted in November 1941 with the hull built up to near the Lower Deck. On 30 June 1942 she was reassigned to the carrier programme. A specification of 16 July 1942 recorded the turbines and nine of twelve boilers installed, the magazine floors of Nos. 1 and 3 turrets complete, and bulkheads up to the Middle Deck [S3]. navypedia says work was suspended in December 1941 at 50 per cent completion [S28].

The dock was flooded incorrectly during the launch on 5 October 1944 and the hydrophone compartment was damaged; the repairs used material from her cancelled sister, Hull No. 111 [S3].

Where the battleships carried a 16.1-inch belt and an 8.1-inch second deck, Shinano's belt was 6.4 inches and her second deck armour 4 inches, with the 3-inch armoured flight deck above [S12, p. 25].

## Incomplete on sailing

Trials were run on 11 November 1944 at 27.5 knots with a 12-degree roll [S3]. She sailed on 28 November 1944 at 1330 with six boilers fired of the eight completed out of twelve, giving a maximum of 24 knots [S3].

The Technical Mission listed two major incomplete items when she sailed: air tests had not been carried out, and the holes cut for cables, pipes and ventilation had not been sealed. The firemain and drainage systems were not in complete operation because the pumps had not been delivered. Complement was about 1,900, of whom about 75 per cent had sea experience [S12, pp. 25 to 26].

## Loss, 29 November 1944

She was making 18 to 20 knots with six firerooms steaming. Watertight doors on and above the armoured deck were open and hatches below were open. A second damage control station sat on the deck below the armoured deck to starboard between frames 104 and 112 [S12, p. 26].

At about 0320 four torpedoes hit to starboard in a single salvo and she took a 9 to 10 degree list. The starboard air compressor room between frames 89 and 103 on the orlop deck flooded, as did the anti-aircraft magazines. No. 3 fireroom flooded instantly with no survivors; No. 1 flooded slowly through leaks around the H beams and No. 7 slowly. Bulkhead 120 separated the firerooms. The starboard outboard engine room flooded through the shaft stuffing gland at bulkhead 160. The fourth torpedo struck aft of the torpedo defence system in way of the after gasoline tanks, which were empty, and the refrigerated storerooms between frames 188 and 201 flooded. Flooding the port outboard voids checked the list at 11 to 12 degrees [S12, pp. 26 to 28].

There were no drainage facilities in operation. Gasoline-driven handy billies were aboard but nobody knew how to work them, and there were no portable submersible pumps. Civilian technical personnel aboard refused orders. At about 0500 the chief engineer flooded three port outboard firerooms. At about 0600 all power was lost: the boiler feed water was exhausted and the crews did not know how to steam on salt water. The Emperor's photograph was transferred at about 0700 and she capsized to starboard before 1100. About 75 per cent of the crew were saved [S12, pp. 26 to 28].

combinedfleet gives 1,435 lost and 1,080 survivors, of whom 55 officers, 993 petty officers and men, and 32 civilians [S3].

The Japanese investigating committee found that counterflooding was ordered too late, that the torpedoes ran shallow at less than 3 metres, that the joint in the side protection was weak, that the ship was unfinished and that the crew was undertrained. The mission concluded that Japanese damage control technique and equipment were far below US and British standards [S12, p. 28].

## Torpedo defence lesson

Four torpedoes struck in way of the torpedo defence system and each produced definite, immediate flooding of inboard spaces. The mission treated this as substantiating its conclusions about the Yamato-class system, and as important in assessing the number of torpedoes that struck Yamato and Musashi [S12, p. 15].
