# King George V class: propulsion and power

Status: partial (see gaps.md)

## Arrangement

The machinery is arranged in four self-contained units, so that damage to one need not stop the others. Each of the four engine rooms drives one shaft through turbines of 27,500 shaft horsepower (20,500 kW), and each has its own boiler room with two oil-fired boilers feeding it and an associated action machinery room holding a steam-driven turbo-generator [S24]. A tier 3 account describes the same layout as four turbine rooms and four boiler rooms, eight machinery compartments in all, alternating in pairs [S70]. The builder's general arrangement profile of King George V labels the pairs on the drawing: 'A' Boiler Room to starboard and 'B' Boiler Room to port, with 'A' and 'B' Action Machinery Rooms below them and reserve feed water tanks port and starboard, between watertight bulkheads carrying frame numbers in the 140s to 160s [S21].

Power from each set passes through a single reduction gear and a thrust block into a segmented line shaft, supported on bearings, with a watertight gland at every transverse watertight bulkhead; a shaft strut carries the exposed shafting and propeller on all four shafts [S24]. The line shaft is 17.5 inches (44.5 cm) in diameter [S24].

A separate power control room, in 'X' engine room, carries duplicate gauges and switches for monitoring and controlling all four engine rooms; in Prince of Wales it was commanded by the chief engineering officer while the senior engineer was in 'X' engine room itself in overall control of the four [S24]. Orders reached the engine rooms from the bridge and were passed on to the boiler rooms by voice; two IWM photographs taken in Howe in August 1942 show an engine room artificer opening the throttle valve in response to an order from the bridge, and the engineer officer in the engine room relaying an order to the boiler room [S45], [S46].

## Boilers

Eight Admiralty three-drum small-tube boilers with superheaters, at a maximum working pressure of 400 lb per square inch [S18], [S23]. A tier 3 account gives the steam temperature as 371.5 degrees C [S70].

The same tier 3 account records a wartime fall in boiler efficiency that had nothing to do with the boilers. It gives 0.748 lb per shaft horsepower for the type on Warspite's trials after her 1937 reconstruction, and states that from 1942, when the Royal Navy was forced onto fuel oils of higher viscosity and greater seawater content, efficiency fell to about 0.8 lb per shaft horsepower by 1944. New oil sprayers and burners were adopted to recover it and were installed in Duke of York and Anson; the same installation in Vanguard, working on better peacetime oil at the same pressures and temperatures, reached 0.63 lb per shaft horsepower [S70]. This is a tier 3 source and no Admiralty document confirming the figures was found.

Endurance dominated Admiral Tovey's handling of the Bismarck operation. On 26 May 1941 King George V had only 1,200 tons remaining, 32 per cent, and Rodney reported that she would have to part company at 0800 the next morning; Tovey reduced King George V to 22 knots at 1705 to economise, and broke off the final action on 27 May while Bismarck was still afloat because the shortage had become acute and he had to keep enough fuel in hand to return at a reasonably high speed through waters where U-boats were concentrating [S15].

Fuelling at sea came late to the class and was awkward when it came. King George V completed her first refuelling under way on the passage to Sydney in January to February 1945; the naval-history.net chronology gives 10 hours for it and the museum guide 12 hours [S1], [S18]. In July 1945 she became the first Royal Navy warship to refuel at sea abeam of the tanker; before that the hoses were streamed astern of the tanker, an arrangement very liable to parting [S18].

## Electrical plant

Prince of Wales carried eight generators: six 330 kW direct-current turbo-dynamos and two 330 kW diesel dynamos [S24]. The turbo-dynamos stood in the action machinery rooms, one to each machinery unit, and the diesel dynamos in their own rooms; the forensic analysis of her loss names No. 6 Diesel Dynamo Room, immediately abaft 'B' engine room, and No. 8 Diesel Dynamo, and breaker rooms above them [S24].

The single torpedo hit aft on 10 December 1941 showed how much depended on that plant. No. 8 Diesel Dynamo stopped immediately; the turbo-generator in 'Y' action machinery room had its steam cut off when the lights failed in 'Y' engine room and steam pressure failed from 'Y' boiler room; No. 6 Diesel Dynamo stopped once 'Y' action machinery room had flooded completely. By the time the second torpedo attack began at 1219, only three of the eight generators were working [S24]. The loss of power took with it the ship's internal communications, ventilation fans, steering, pumps, the training and elevation of the 5.25-inch mountings and the pom-poms, and the radars [S24].

## Steering gear

Prince of Wales had electric steering with a steam standby connection from 'X' engine room. At the moment of the first torpedo hit on 10 December 1941, No. 2 steering motor stopped and No. 1 slowly failed; the standby steam connection from 'X' engine room was then in operation, and an attempt was made to run emergency power leads to vital functions, but with the steering unresponsive and only the starboard engines turning, the ship could make at best 15 knots and was virtually unmanageable [S24]. The experience fed directly into the design of Vanguard, which was given dual steam-line connections to the steering gear for emergency steering, as well as Bofors in place of the pom-poms and increased separation between the inner and outer shafts [S24].

## Ventilation

Machinery space ventilation was a known weakness in warm climates. Admiral Tovey protested against sending Prince of Wales to the tropics on the ground that recent Mediterranean operations had made clear that "ventilation in the King George V class is most inadequate in a hot climate and health and efficiency of the ship's company will be seriously affected", aggravated by the lack of awnings and side screens in harbour; the forensic analysis judges he was proved right, with officers and crew overcome by heat exhaustion during the action of 10 December 1941 and on the passage from Capetown to Singapore [S24]. On the day itself, many of the electrically driven ventilation fans stopped when the generators were lost, and officers and ratings had to work in relays to keep vital machinery running in the temperatures that followed [S24].
