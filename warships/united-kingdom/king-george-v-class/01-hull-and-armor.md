The tier 3 monograph records a hull fault that bore on gunnery: the low forward sheer let spray reach the 'A' and 'B' turret rangefinders and sometimes let water into 'A' turret [S69]. The naval-history.net chronology for Duke of York records the same finding in her October 1941 trials, that "low forward sheer found to cause excessive flooding even in moderate sea conditions" [S3]. The Prince of Wales gunnery report from the Denmark Strait confirms it in action: while steaming at high speed large quantities of sea water entered 'A' turret around the gun ports and through the joints of the gunhouse roof, and canvas screens had to be rigged in the transverser space and the compartment baled [S14].

## Armor scheme

The Admiralty's own 1939 comparison table, C.B.04039, gives the class in six figures, split between magazines, engine rooms and boiler rooms [S16]:

| Element | Magazines | Engine rooms | Boiler rooms | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Vertical | 15 in | 14 in | 14 in | [S16] |
| Horizontal | 6 in | 5 in | 5 in | [S16] |

Two reference sources give the belt in finer detail and disagree with the Admiralty table's round figures. Navypedia, a tier 3 source, describes an external vertical main belt 7.16 m high and 126.5 m long covering the citadel between the end barbettes. Its upper part, 4.62 m high, is 381 mm abreast the magazines and 356 mm abreast the machinery. Its lower part, 2.54 m high, tapers from those thicknesses at its upper edge to 140 mm abreast the magazines and 114 mm abreast the machinery at its lower edge. The lower strake continues 12.2 m forward and 11.3 m aft of the citadel, at 343 to 305 mm at the upper edge and 127 to 114 mm at the lower edge forward, and 343 to 280 mm and 127 to 114 mm aft [S23]. The tier 3 monograph gives the side belt as 13.75 in tapering to 5.5 in (349 to 140 mm), with the magazine sides at 14.7 in (373 mm) [S69].

## Deck armour over magazines and machinery

The main deck inside the citadel is flat and lands on the upper edge of the main belt; navypedia gives it as 152 mm over the magazines and 127 mm over the machinery [S23], and the Admiralty table as 6 inches over magazines and 5 inches over engine and boiler rooms [S16]. The tier 3 monograph gives a single figure of 5 in (127 mm) for the deck and 6 in (152 mm) for the magazine top [S69]. Outside the citadel the protective deck drops to the lower deck, landing on the upper edge of the extended lower belt strake: 127 to 64 mm forward, running to the first watertight bulkhead, and 114 mm aft, thickening to 127 mm over the steering gear and closed by a 102 mm bulkhead [S23].

The Admiralty's 1939 immunity table, computed at 90 degrees inclination and given in thousands of yards, sets out the consequence [S16]:

| Attacking gun | Chance vital hit in magazines | Speed hit in machinery spaces | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 16 in (Japan, United States) | 14,000 to 33,000 yards | 16,000 to 30,000 yards | [S16] |
| 15 in (Germany, Italy, France) | 13,000 to 31,000 yards | 15,000 to 27,000 yards | [S16] |
| 14 in medium velocity (Japan, United States) | 0 to 32,000 yards | 14,000 to 29,000 yards | [S16] |
| 14 in high velocity (United States) | 0 to 36,000 yards | 14,000 to 32,000 yards | [S16] |
| 12.6 in (Italy) | immune | 0 to 34,000 yards | [S16] |
| 11 in (Germany) | immune | immune | [S16] |

The pamphlet warns that the figures assume foreign shells and armour behave like British ones, that the theoretical angle of descent is compromised when the target lists or rolls heavily, and that shell which should burst well inside may be deflected by boats' davits, deck stiffeners and other obstructions [S16]. It also gives a "shut down" range of about 12,000 yards at 90 degrees inclination for all ships, below which a shell's flat trajectory cannot reach the magazines even if it defeats the side armour [S16]. The tier 3 monograph gives a different immunity zone against 15-inch shells, 17,200 to 32,000 yards (15,727 m to 29,261 m), and stresses that this is a theoretical calculation [S69].

## Underwater protection

The side protection system is a sandwich three layers deep, with the centre layer filled with fuel oil [S24]. Navypedia gives its depth as 4.1 m with a final longitudinal bulkhead 44 mm thick, designed to resist the explosion of 454 kg of TNT [S23]. An Admiralty cross-sectional drawing through the main magazines, credited to ADM 239/269 Addendum 2, shows the armour and the side protection system together [S42].

The design was validated by a series of underwater explosive trials against caissons modelling King George V class hull sections; those trials were called Job 74, and charges of up to 1,000 pounds (454 kilograms) of TNT were used and defeated [S24], [S25]. The 2007 expedition to the wreck of Prince of Wales took its name from those trials, and its report states flatly that in the first real test the system proved inadequate [S25].

The forensic analysis of her loss is more particular about which part failed and which did not [S24]. The system in the way of 'B' turret, at about Frame 109, did work more or less as intended, with one exception: part of the gas jet from the explosion vented into the Seamen's Mess Deck above the impact area. Unlike contemporary American battleships, which had a void over the system to contain structural debris and the gas jet, the British arrangement could not fully contain the explosion within the system [S24]. The counter-flooding done before that hit made it worse, because water already stood in both the inner and outer voids there, so damage extended upward into the spaces above; the analysis judges that a void over the system would probably have prevented this, but that the ship was already seriously damaged and the defect did not materially contribute to her loss [S24]. The two torpedoes that struck aft of 'Y' turret and the one at the bow hit regions where there was no multi-layer torpedo defence system at all [S24].

Two features of the hull structure recur in the analysis of the loss. The ship was of riveted construction, and riveted connections are susceptible to failure under shock loading, so that the whipping set up by the stern hit caused rivet failures around 'Y' turret and contributed to progressive flooding aft [S24]. And the machinery spaces used centreline and longitudinal bulkheads; the analysis calls that a serious design flaw for a hull of this beam, noting that American and French designers avoided centreline bulkheads in their contemporary battleships and that the United States did not accept longitudinal bulkheads in machinery spaces until the 122-foot beam of the Montana class design [S24].

Navypedia records the same verdict in one sentence: the internal anti-torpedo protection was rated against 450 kg of TNT, but the loss of Prince of Wales showed that it did not withstand the explosion of a much lighter aircraft torpedo [S23].

## What the Prince of Wales wreck survey found

Expedition Job 74 dived the wreck over four days in May 2007 with closed-circuit rebreathers and helium-based mixtures, in 68 m (223 ft) of water, and ran a video survey of the whole hull to count and locate the torpedo holes [S25]. The ship lies upside down [S25]; the 2019 Ministry of Defence survey adds that she is oriented 016/196 degrees with a list to port of between 10 and 15 degrees, and that the superstructure is largely compacted into the soft sediment by the weight of the hull, except amidships where it holds the starboard side up off the seabed [S26].

British accounts had recorded at least six torpedo hits, four to starboard and two to port [S25]. The survey found four jagged penetrating holes, three to starboard and one to port [S25]:

| Position | Frame | Size | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| The bow itself, so close to the stem that the stem is fractured and the hole passes right through the ship | Frame 8 | | [S25] |
| Beneath 'B' turret, just forward of the bridge | Frame 109 | | [S25] |
| Stern to starboard, above and slightly abaft the outer strut of the outer propeller shaft A-bracket | Frame 296 | about 4 m high by 11 m wide | [S25] |
| Stern to port, between the outer propeller shaft A-bracket and where the shaft left the hull | Frame 284 | about 4 m high by 6 m wide | [S25] |

Where the other two hits had been reported, beneath the mainmast and the rear funnel, there are no jagged holes at all [S25]. Instead the hull is dished in longitudinally from about Frame 170 to about Frame 282 on the starboard side and about Frame 140 to about Frame 255 on the port side, split longitudinally along the base of the armour belt for a considerable distance in those areas, and crumpled in places [S25]. The survey report presents this as at odds with the historical record and notes that the footage was passed to naval architects and historians for assessment [S25]. The height of the starboard after hole, higher up the hull than any of the others, shows how far the stern had already settled when that torpedo struck [S25].

The survey also established that the port outer propeller, its A-bracket and a length of shafting were missing, with a shaft flange exposed that should have been inside the stern tube; the outer strut arm of the port A-bracket broke off flush with the hull and the inner arm broke in half, and on the starboard side the pattern was reversed [S25], [S24].

The consequences of the first hit for flooding are in facet 07 and in the Prince of Wales ship file.

## Subdivision and stability

No metacentric height or stability curve was found; see gaps.md.

That the subdivision was modelled physically is recorded by a museum object: a metal model of King George V marked with deck and compartment details, its interior sectioned into sealed compartments, with openable apertures on the deck and around the hull for flooding them. The museum has no documentation for it and takes it to have been used for buoyancy testing [S72].

The Prince of Wales flooding diagrams reconstructed from survivor testimony are drawn on four levels, the Lower Deck, the Upper and Lower Platform Decks, and the Hold, and show which compartments flooded after the first torpedo hit and which section of the starboard side protection system was deliberately counter-flooded to correct the list [S24].

## Conflicts between sources

- Beam. The builder's body plan gives 103 ft 0 in extreme and 102 ft 9.25 in moulded [S22]; the museum guide gives 112 ft 4.25 in maximum and 103 ft 2.56 in at the waterline [S18]; the wreck assessment gives 34.29 m [S26] and navypedia 31.4 m [S23].
- Draught. 28 ft 0 in on the 1938 body plan [S22]; 29 ft standard and 32 ft 6 in deep in 1940 [S18].
- Standard displacement. 38,031 tons in 1940 [S18], [S69]; 36,727 t undated [S23].
- Armour weight. 12,413 tons [S18]; 12,410 tons [S20].
- Belt thickness abreast the magazines. 15 in in the Admiralty comparison table [S16]; 381 mm (15.0 in) at the upper edge tapering to 140 mm at the lower edge [S23]; 14.7 in (373 mm) [S69]; "15 inches thick" in the model record [S20].
- Deck thickness over the magazines. 6 in [S16], 152 mm [S23], 6 in magazine top with a 5 in deck [S69].
- Immunity zone against 15-inch fire. 13,000 to 31,000 yards for chance vital hits in magazines and 15,000 to 27,000 yards for speed hits in machinery spaces [S16]; 17,200 to 32,000 yards, undifferentiated [S69].
- Number of torpedo hits on Prince of Wales. British wartime accounts recorded at least six [S25]; the 2007 survey confirmed four penetrating hits [S25]; navypedia gives five in one place and six in another on the same page [S23].
