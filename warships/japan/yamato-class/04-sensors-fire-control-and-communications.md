# Yamato class: sensors, fire control and communications

Status: partial (see gaps.md)

## Main battery optics

Each 46 cm turret carried a 15 metre (49.2 ft) rangefinder with an elevation range of plus or minus 10 degrees and free training of 160 mils right and 130 mils left [S11, p. 17]. Local sights were 10 cm telescopes, one per gun, with the same free training as the rangefinder [S11, p. 18].

Figure 12 of report O-45(N), the sectional elevation of the mount, shows the rangefinder in the after part of the gunhouse [S11, p. 20].

On the 15.5 cm mountings the Yamato-class gunhouses were modified so the rangefinders sat toward the middle of the gunhouse rather than at the rear as on the Mogami-class cruiser mountings [S6].

The German naval attaché, Konteradmiral Paul Wenneker, was shown the bridge, the air defence centre and the main gun director during his one-hour visit on 17 July 1943, and started a discussion with Yamato's commanding officer about the best location for a battleship's reserve main gun director. Areas below deck were not shown and the route avoided the main gun turrets [S1].

## Anti-aircraft fire control

Remote power control on the 25 mm triple mountings was provided as part of the Type 95 Machine Gun Fire Control Arrangement, which used the Ward-Leonard system on the mount taking signals from the Type 95 director, known to the Japanese as the Shageki Sochi. Control was remote only or manual only; there was no combined mode. Maximum rates under remote control were 18 degrees per second in train and 12 in elevation, from two 1 hp, 220 V, 3.6 A motors, one for each axis [S20, p. 13], [S8].

The Type 95 director was based on the French Le Prieur mechanised sight for computing. Where no power drive was fitted, fire control was by open ring sight and tracer [S20, p. 1]. Three sights were in use on the guns themselves: the Le Prieur mechanical lead computing sight, the open ring sight and an optical etched-glass ring sight; the last was considered unsatisfactory [S20, pp. 13 to 14].

The mission's criticism of the whole arrangement was that the Le Prieur sight was not calibrated for sufficiently high target speeds, and that even if it had been, the power drives had insufficient capacity to follow [S20, p. 1]. The Japanese themselves put the sight's inadequacy against high speed targets second on their list of the mounting's deficiencies, behind the rate of train and elevation [S20, p. 15].

Musashi's April 1944 refit raised the number of her anti-aircraft directors to fourteen [S2].

The same compilation records that the Type 21 was first fitted to the battleship Ise in April 1942, and that the Type 22 was fitted to Yamato and Musashi in October 1943. It notes that the Type 22, while not designed for gunnery control, provided moderately accurate data for that purpose [S26].

### Radar in action

At 0523 on 25 October 1944, off Samar, Yamato's Type 13 picked up enemy aircraft, 21 minutes before the enemy carriers were sighted [S1].

On 7 April 1945 her Type 13 detected a large inbound formation at 1107 at the radar's maximum range of 63 nautical miles, and noted the formation splitting into two groups. At 1115 the two groups were at 44 miles and closing [S24]. One of her two air search radars was knocked out by the bomb that exploded near the after command station at about 1240; the remaining set detected the second wave at 1302 [S24], [S1].

## Identification and radar warning

In the refit of February and March 1944 Yamato was fitted with Type 2 infrared identification friend-or-foe and signalling devices, mounted midway up each side of the bridge. combinedfleet states the system may have been based on the German Seehund infrared device. It was built around a telescopic sensor receiving light waves in the infrared range, with a readout in the radio shack, and included a pair of 20 mm binoculars mounted coaxially with the transmitting infrared lamp on the bridge, so that another ship could use the infrared detector for elementary signalling or as a formation light for station keeping [S1].

About the same time Yamato was fitted with multiple E27 radar detectors, copied from the German FuMB 1 Metox R.600 [S1].

## Hydrophones

Musashi carried a Type 0 hydrophone in a compartment in the bow. When USS Tunny torpedoed her in the port bow on 29 March 1944 the forward windlass room and the Type 0 hydrophone compartment flooded, and seven hydrophone operators were among the eighteen casualties [S2].

Shinano also had a hydrophone compartment forward: it was damaged when the dock was flooded incorrectly during her launch on 5 October 1944 [S3].

No source fetched gives the set's performance, its array or its operating position.

## Radio and radio intelligence

Yamato carried a radio-intelligence operator on her last sortie; the NHHC essay records that he was a Nisei, born a US citizen, who had been studying in Japan when the war broke out [S24]. On 6 April 1945 the force intercepted USS Threadfin's contact report, made in the clear [S24]. Yahagi began jamming the shadowing Martin Mariner's communications at 1017 on 7 April, by which time it was too late [S24]. By the time the executive officer recommended abandoning ship, Yamato's radios were destroyed, so Vice Admiral Ito cancelled the operation by flag hoist [S24].

Musashi's staff facilities were rebuilt for flagship duty in February and March 1942: the operations room and the telephone and code rooms were extended [S2].

The damage control communications fit in a Japanese battleship, from report S-84(N), was telephones, loudspeaker and voice tube, with a draft meter receiver, clinometer, helm angle report receiver, speed telegraph receiver, flooding and pumping telegraph receiver, air pipe transmitter and GM indicator at the damage control stations [S23, p. 13]. Aboard Yamato the executive officer and the assistant gunnery officer received damage control reports by telephone and by messenger [S12, p. 11], and an inclinometer was in the conning tower [S12, p. 11].

## Conflicts between sources

- Date the Type 22 was fitted. The radar compilation gives October 1943 for both ships [S26]; combinedfleet's tabular records give February to March 1944 for Yamato [S1] and April 1944 for Musashi [S2].
- Type 13 maximum range. The compilation gives group detection at 100 km and single aircraft at 50 km [S26]; the NHHC essay records a detection at the radar's maximum range of 63 nautical miles, which is about 117 km, on 7 April 1945 [S24].
