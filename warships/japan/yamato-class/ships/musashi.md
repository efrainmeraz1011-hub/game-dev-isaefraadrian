# Musashi

Second ship of the class, built at Mitsubishi's Nagasaki yard as Warship No. 2, hull No. 800.

The first section of keel structure placed on the slipway was 71 feet (21.6 m) long. To hide the slipway from foreign ships visiting Nagasaki a curtain of palm fibre rope was hung from the gantry crane; the Sasebo Naval District bought 400 metric tons of wajuro palm fibre for it, causing a temporary shortage in the Kyushu fishing industry [S2].

## Trials

On 22 June 1942 Musashi made 28.05 knots in overload condition at 70,358 tons displacement on 167,310 shp on the Sata Misaki mile in the Iyo Nada; she returned to Kure after testing the counterflooding system [S2]. On 24 July 1942 she conducted calibration firing during which guinea pigs were used to research the effect of main-gun blast [S2].

## Sensors

A modified Type 21 Mod 3 air search radar was installed on the foretop rangefinder arms on 3 September 1942 [S2]. On 23 September 1942 LtCdr Matsui Muneaki, a student of Dr Yagi Hidetsugu, joined the ship to research using the Type 21 for surface fire control [S2]. In the April 1944 refit two Type 22 surface search and two Type 13 air search radars were installed, and the number of anti-aircraft directors was raised to fourteen [S2]. Later in 1944 the Type 22 sets were replaced by Type 22 Mod 4 with a limited fire-control capability [S2].

Musashi carried a Type 0 hydrophone in a compartment in the bow. When USS Tunny torpedoed her on 29 March 1944 the forward windlass room and the Type 0 hydrophone compartment flooded, and seven hydrophone operators were among the 18 casualties [S2].

## Torpedo damage, 29 March 1944

Hit in the port bow about 20 feet below the waterline by one of six torpedoes from USS Tunny at 1744 off Palau. The hole was about 19 feet across. She shipped 2,630 tons of water and cleared the area making 24 knots [S2]. Repairs were made in Kure No. 4 drydock between 10 and 27 April 1944 [S2].

## Crew training and damage control

The Technical Mission found her crew exceptionally well trained by Japanese standards. Her regular routine included two drills a day on setting a proper and complete condition of closure. Special counterflooding drills were held in which an actual list of 1 degree had to be removed; these found that four or five outboard voids were needed to remove a 1-degree list, about one half to one third of the list Yamato took from a single torpedo in December 1943 [S12, p. 17].

At 0600 on 24 October 1944 the crew went to General Quarters, a condition of complete closure was reported set, and speed was 20 knots. The executive officer's battle station was on the third level behind the conning tower; the chief engineer was in the port inboard engine room control booth, the control engine room [S12, p. 17].

## Loss, 24 October 1944

She had departed Lingga at midnight on 21 to 22 October fully loaded, drawing almost 36 feet. During the passage she burned about 1,000 tons of fuel oil and transferred another 800 tons to accompanying destroyers, so that her draft on the last day was about 34.5 feet [S12, p. 17].

Attack sequence and hits as the mission recorded them [S12, pp. 18 to 22]:

| Attack | Time | Damage |
|---|---|---|
| First | about 1030 | Torpedo to starboard at frame 130, leakage into No. 11 fireroom; near misses flooded two forward peak tanks near frame 20 |
| Second | about 1140 | Two bombs, one a dud at frame 15 port, one at the 01 level frame 138 that pierced two decks and filled the port inboard engine room with steam; that space was abandoned and the port inboard shaft lost. Torpedo to port at frame 143 flooded the port hydraulic machinery room |
| Third | 1215 | Torpedo to starboard at frame 60 into storerooms; trim by the bow exceeded 2 metres |
| Fourth | 1250 | Four bombs at frames 45, 65 and 70 to port and frame 134 starboard on the flying deck; torpedoes port frame 70, starboard frame 70 and starboard frame 138, the last flooding the starboard hydraulic machinery room. Flooding from frame 54 aft to the citadel; speed 16 knots |
| Fifth | 1315 | No damage. Three engine rooms and nine firerooms in operation, 12 knots |
| Sixth | 1520 | Ten bombs; torpedoes port frame 75 into the two lower levels of the No. 1 magazines, port frame 125 into No. 8 fireroom with No. 12 flooding slowly, port frame 145 into the port outboard engine room, starboard frame 105 into the anti-aircraft magazines. List 10 to 12 degrees to port, speed six knots on two starboard engine rooms and seven firerooms |

The chief engineer flooded the remaining starboard outboard firerooms, holding the list at 12 degrees. All power was lost at 1800. By 1900 the situation was hopeless, with the list 12 to 15 degrees and the flying deck submerged as far forward as No. 1 turret. At 1930 the list passed 30 degrees; at 1935 she lurched, turned bottom up and slid under bow first. There were no explosions, which the mission thought noteworthy and attributed to the outboard voids being empty [S12, pp. 20 to 22].

Her voids had steam eductors rated at 200 tons an hour, but no attempt was made to pump. When flooding reached the storerooms there were no sea valves there and few fire pumps available [S12, pp. 19 to 20].

combinedfleet records that she capsized to port and sank by the bow at 1936 in 4,430 feet of water at 13-07N, 122-32E as given by her own damage action report, that as the stern upended the after turret fell off and two explosions were heard, and that Kiyoshimo and Hamakaze rescued 1,423 survivors while 1,023 of her 2,399-man crew were lost [S2]. Those two accounts of the final moments disagree; see `08-service-history.md`.

## Wreck, located 2015

Paul Allen's expedition team found Musashi in March 2015 under the Sibuyan Sea [S25]. National Geographic gives the date the wreck was located as 2 March 2015 and the depth as approximately 3,280 feet (one kilometre) in Philippine waters [S32]. The Associated Press report of 4 March 2015 gives the find as made on 1 March [S35]; Allen's own press release of 11 March 2015 gives 2 March [S34].

The search area was 360 square nautical miles (477 square miles, or 1,236 square kilometres), derived from an analysis of primary sources listing four different sinking positions in the Sibuyan Sea: the official Japanese and US Navy positions, one recorded in the log of a Japanese destroyer standing by to rescue Musashi's crew, and a drawing by a Japanese survivor showing where the ship sank in relation to Sibuyan Island. David Mearns of Bluewater Recoveries assisted with the research and with determining the final search area, work that had begun more than eleven years earlier [S32].

Method and equipment [S32]:

1. Towed side-scan sonar was tried first; an early attempt was hampered by long fishing lines entangling the sensor.
2. The team switched to a multibeam echosounder. A large volcanic ridge in the search area made depths vary between approximately 500 feet (150 m) and 6,560 feet (2,000 m) or more within a very small distance, which made both the echosounder and towed side-scan impractical.
3. The team turned to an autonomous underwater vehicle, a Bluefin 12D built to the search team's specifications by Bluefin Robotics. Each dive lasted 24 hours on average and covered a maximum of 150 square miles (388 square kilometres).
4. Three AUV dives located the target, which the Octo ROV, operated from the motor yacht Octopus with a high-definition camera, then confirmed as Musashi.

Parts identified on the seabed: the main rudder, 36 by 20 feet (11 by 6 m) [S32]; the bow, warped by torpedo damage, with the mount for the Imperial Japanese Navy chrysanthemum crest, the crest itself having been teak and rotted away [S33]; a valve wheel carrying Japanese characters reading "main valve handle" from a lower engineering area, which was the first confirmation of Japanese origin [S35], [S33]; a catapult; a large gun turret; and one of the ship's 15-ton anchors [S35]. The press release also names the bow and stern sections and the bridge among the features shown [S34]. The expedition leader Robert Kraft identified belled-outward hull metal as torpedoes that punctured the hull and exploded inside, and a large dent pushing inward as the concussive force of a torpedo that exploded just outside the hull [S33].

The Japanese naval historian Kazushige Todaka confirmed the identification [S32]. The Associated Press describes him as the head of a private museum specialising in the sister ship Yamato and quotes him saying "Judging from the location, it must be the Musashi" [S35].

No fetched source publishes a latitude and longitude for the wreck, and none states how it lies. The only statement on its disposition is that the wreck landed in pieces on the sea floor [S33]. The depth figures are not consistent: approximately 3,280 feet (one kilometre) [S32] against "nearly 4,000 feet" for the 13 March live tour [S33].

The Yamato Museum in Kure records that the results of the Paul Allen foundation's continuing sunken-warship surveys were provided to the museum, and that video of Musashi's present condition was among the material displayed in its 27th special exhibition, "Warships Sleeping on the Seabed: Yamato and Musashi", which opened on 24 April 2019 [S36].
