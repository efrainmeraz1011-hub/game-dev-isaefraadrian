# Yamato class: propulsion and power

Status: partial (see gaps.md)

Two US Naval Technical Mission reports carry this facet. S-01-2, "Characteristics of Japanese Naval Vessels, Article 2: Surface Warship Machinery Design", December 1945, sets out the arrangement, the piping and the design philosophy, with Yamato as its battleship exemplar. S-01-12, "Article 12: Boilers and Machinery", April 1946, describes the standard Kampon boiler and then takes Yamato's boiler apart in detail as one of two worked examples, the other being the destroyer Akitsuki. Both reports are silent on several things a reader expects: no maker is named for the boilers or turbines, no shaft rpm is printed, and propeller dimensions appear nowhere.

navypedia gives four shafts, four sets of Kampon geared steam turbines, twelve Kampon boilers, 150,000 hp, 27 knots maximum, 6,300 t of oil and an endurance of 7,200 nm at 16 knots [S28].

The boiler report gives a different power figure again: "This ship had 12 boilers and is reputed to have delivered 150,000 shaft horsepower. Japanese acceptance trial reports indicate a maximum of 138,000 total shaft horsepower, using 62.7 metric tons of fuel per hour when 12 boilers were being fired, each using nine burners" [S17, p. 7].

Report O-45(N) records the design as 27 knots on 150,000 shp, with Yamato making 27.7 knots on trials [S11, p. 9]. Musashi made 28.05 knots in overload condition at 70,358 tons on 167,310 shp on 22 June 1942 [S2]. Shinano made 27.5 knots on trials on 11 November 1944 with a 12-degree roll [S3].

## Machinery lineage

"YAMATO, the latest BB, had a plant very much like that of the NACHI class cruiser (begun in 1924 and completed in 1929) except that the horsepower had been stepped up from 130,000 to 165,000" [S16, p. 7].

## Boilers

The Japanese Navy standardised boiler design to a single shape and size. The shape followed conventional three-drum express boilers, with air heaters in the uptakes, superheaters in the banks, symmetrical layout, suspended furnaces and double fronts, fired by eleven burners in three rows of two, four and five. Depth at the furnace was 14 feet, at the superheater about 10.5 feet, and frequently 9.5 feet at the air heater. Large diameter outside downtakes were characteristic. Mud drums were 24 inches internal diameter and 16 feet apart; steam drums were 54 inches internal diameter and 11 feet above. Performance was standardised at a consumption of 16,000 pounds of oil an hour maximum, to generate steam at 30 kg/cm2 and 350 degrees C from 95 degrees C feed water [S17, p. 7].

A feature peculiar to the standard Kampon boiler was a necking down of the boiler bank between the furnace and the superheater: the centre-to-centre length of the first three tube rows was 4,250 mm, while at the superheater and beyond the gas passage was only 3,263 mm, a reduction of nearly 25 per cent [S17, pp. 7 to 9].

The steam collector pipe cluster in the drum had ten hole groups totalling 998 holes and 100 per cent of area, from 108 holes of 5 mm giving 21 cm2 and 2.1 per cent, up to 384 holes of 8 mm giving 193 cm2 and 19.4 per cent. At full power steam velocity at the pipe exit was 1,000 feet a minute and through the holes 2,000 feet a minute [S17, pp. 9 to 10]. Ten baffle plates made the steam turn through about nine right angles between the separating surface and the collector pipe; only the two bottom plates were perforated and there were no submerged plates [S17, p. 10].

Yamato's steam driers were unique. The steam outlet piping from each superheater passed through the side of the shell and ran internally for the length of the steam drum. These internal pipes were 6.25 inches internal diameter and 0.197 inches thick, with several flanged joints near the water level [S17, p. 10].

The mission judged that arrangement harshly: "It is obvious that YAMATO's unit was both ineffective and dangerous. It was ineffective because overall convection transfer rate was too low for any practical use and dangerous because the 10 flanged joints gave constant trouble from leakage… Any flange leakage readily would have created turbine hazard from water slugs" [S17, p. 18]. Interrogation developed that steam quality was only 0.98 at full power, which caused extensive superheater maintenance and high turbine water rates [S17, p. 18].

## Turbines

Each main unit in a battleship consisted of two high pressure and two low pressure turbines operating in parallel, with a cruising turbine connected to one of the high pressure turbines in each space through a reduction gear and a jaw clutch [S16, p. 15].

The working order was staged. The cruising turbine was used up to about 21 knots for battleships. It was then declutched and one high pressure and one low pressure turbine were used up to about 27 knots. At that point the other high pressure and low pressure turbines were brought into service. Cooling steam was not admitted to the idling turbines below that speed. The two high pressure turbines were usually aft of the reduction gear with the low pressure forward, and a main condenser slung under each low pressure turbine [S16, p. 15].

Later vessels had no provision for bleeding, but in Yamato and Oyodo there was provision for the use of auxiliary exhaust in the low pressure turbines [S16, p. 15].

The mission gives one fuel rate for Yamato: with cruising turbines in use, at 21.69 knots, 0.399 kg per shp per hour, which the report converts to 0.891 lb per shp per hour, the shaft horsepower being in Cheval Vapeur units [S16, p. 15].

## Machinery space arrangement

Practically all vessels from cruisers up had centreline bulkheads in the engine room space with one main engine in each engine room, and the same in the boiler rooms. Yamato and Musashi, like the carriers, had only one boiler to a fireroom [S16, p. 9]. The conventional arrangement was firerooms grouped together forward of the engine rooms rather than the alternating fireroom and engine room layout of some US cruisers [S16, p. 9]. In later designs battleships had 12 boilers, heavy cruisers eight, light cruisers six, destroyers three and escorts two [S16, p. 10].

The Yamato-class boiler grouping is set out precisely [S16, p. 19]:

> In the case of the YAMATO class (12 boilers), the boilers may be considered as four groups of three boilers each in a fore and aft row with the rows athwartships from starboard to port. Each group of three boilers supplied the engine most nearly astern of it, the outer groups supplying the outboard shafts and the inner the inboard shafts. Cross-connecting lines were provided in the forward end of the machinery spaces but aft of the bulkhead stops.

That arrangement gives the two inboard shafts, their engine rooms and their boilers the maximum protection [S16, p. 19]. Where engine rooms were abreast each other, as in Yamato, the cross-connecting valves could be worked from both sides of the fore and aft bulkhead [S16, p. 19].

The mission's assessment: "The arrangement in YAMATO class was particularly good as concerns protection. However, this protection was obtained at the expense of a listing movement created in case the outboard firerooms on one side became flooded as a result of rupture of the torpedo defense system" [S16, p. 20]. Yamato's and Shinano's losses both turned on exactly that; see `07-operations-and-doctrine.md`.

In battleships an additional booth was provided in one of the forward engine rooms to serve as a central damage control station for the engineer officer. The booths were not air conditioned but had a separate ventilation system to keep them cooler than the machinery spaces [S16, p. 11]. Musashi's chief engineer fought his ship from the port inboard engine room control booth, which S-06-2 calls the control engine room [S12, p. 17].

## Piping systems

The mission describes Yamato's systems as essentially two cruiser or carrier systems side by side [S16, p. 25].

| System | Arrangement in Yamato |
|---|---|
| Auxiliary steam | Four mains ran aft, one through each group of firerooms, with a connection to each fireroom's auxiliary steam system. The only cross-connection between them was in the forward firerooms, and it was kept closed when steaming with a split plant [S16, p. 20] |
| Auxiliary exhaust, boiler rooms | Four separate systems running down to the outboard sides of the four groups of boilers, with no cross-connections in the firerooms and no bulkhead valves [S16, p. 21] |
| Main steam, engine rooms | A main cross-connection between each engine room and the one adjacent [S16, p. 21] |
| Feed water | The feed line connected to a cross-connection line running through all engine rooms, from which a line led forward to connect with each fireroom feed system. The cross-connection between boiler rooms existed only between adjacent rooms on the same side of the ship, and an additional line on each side ran from the evaporator room through the centre engine room and on through the centre firerooms [S16, pp. 22 to 23] |
| Fuel oil | Each group of two boiler rooms, such as the two forward to port, had its own fuel oil system. Each system was in fact two systems, each with its own pump, heater and accessories, cross-connected on both suction and discharge by valves operable from both sides of the bulkhead. Service pumps could take suction from no other tanks, and the Japanese used no fuel oil booster pump [S16, pp. 22 to 24] |
| Fire main | A cross-connection between the high pressure sides of the two firerooms in the same athwartships section on the same side of the centreline for battleships [S16, p. 25] |
| Lubricating oil standby | Special cross-connections gave a standby steam supply to the lubricating oil pumps from the adjacent engine room on the same side of the ship [S16, p. 20] |
| Steam bilge ejectors | Fitted in all vessels for emergency use, supplied with steam from the adjacent engine room on the same side of the ship in the Yamato class [S16, p. 20] |

Yamato had a pump room forward and another aft of the machinery spaces, from which tail pipes ran to the forward and after tanks respectively. Five pumps were sited in each pump room and two more in the evaporator rooms, one in each. Two electric fuel oil service pumps were provided per battleship for port use [S16, pp. 23 to 24].

## Distilling plant

The evaporators were usually in the engine rooms and, in all vessels larger than destroyers, were in two groups each in a separate space. Yamato had two plants in two separate compartments made by "robbing" the after inboard corner of each inboard engine room [S16, p. 10].

Japanese distilling plants ran on a drowned-tube system with copper coils wound in a double spiral. The distiller was used as the first stage of feed heating and as a vapour heater for the second and last stage; there was no coil-drain heater. About 392 degrees F was maintained in the inlet manifold to the coils. When using exhaust steam a vacuum of about 10 inches of mercury was held in the distiller by an exhaust ejector [S16, p. 26]. No output capacity for Yamato's plants is printed [S16].

## Pumps and auxiliaries

In later vessels all pumps were rotary except the auxiliary feed pump, which was reciprocating. All the rotary pumps were turbine driven except the fuel oil transfer pumps and some fire and bilge pumps. Fire and bilge, main condensate and main feed pumps were centrifugal; the main circulator and lube oil cooler pumps were propeller type, as were the forced draft blowers; all lubricating oil and fuel oil pumps were gear type. Auxiliary turbines were standardised in four rotor wheel pitch diameters, 120, 180, 270 and 360 mm, all driving through reduction gears [S16, p. 26].

The boiler report gives the same picture from the other side: machinery integral to the propulsion plant was turbine driven, while pumps that were not propulsion equipment were reciprocating. Curtis single-stage wheels drove rotary machinery through single reduction gearing, in four sizes only, covering 30 to 360 horsepower. Main feed pumps were turbo-driven and auxiliary pumps were Worthington or Weir. Bilge, ballast, transfer, sanitary, fresh water and fire pumps were mostly Worthington-Simplex or Weir-Duplex. Feed, condensate and circulating pumps were turbo-driven rotary. Heavy oil was handled with gear pumps and light oils with screw pumps, the pump ends being IMO or other standard designs [S17, p. 34].

## Electrical plant

For cruisers and above, all generators were outside the machinery spaces, in at least four groups, forward and aft, outboard of the machinery spaces and at about the same level as the second platform [S16, p. 10]. At sea turbo-generators carried the load; in port diesel generators were used [S17, p. 34]. From cold it took about 15 minutes to put a turbo-generator on the line, so to be anywhere near ready for service they had to be kept running idle [S16, p. 28].

Neither report gives the number, rating, voltage or type of Yamato's generators. See `gaps.md`.

## Fuel

The Japanese Navy used one single boiler fuel, Bunker C, heated on all ships to 90 to 95 degrees C. No variation in grade of fuel or in atomising temperature was permitted [S17, p. 23]. Air pressures ranged up to eight inches maximum and oil pressures up to 230 pounds maximum; air at 300 degrees F was brought down from the top into an ample double front [S17, p. 25]. The fuel specification table gives Bunker fuel oil, general purpose: specific gravity 0.96 maximum; flash point 80 degrees C minimum; viscosity 2,000 seconds Redwood maximum at 0 degrees C; sulphur 3.0 per cent maximum; water 0.5 per cent; reaction neutral for all types [S17, p. 32].

Yamato's fuel capacity is given as 1,900 tons in S-01-2's characteristics table [S16, p. 7] and as 6,300 t of oil by navypedia [S28]; these cannot both describe the same condition. Cruising radius is 7,392 miles at 19.4 knots [S16, p. 7] against 7,200 nm at 16 knots [S28]. Yamato's fuel stowage was reduced in the Kure docking of July 1943 [S1], and she was fuelled to 90 per cent of capacity for her last sortie, taking 3,400 tons at the Tokuyama Oil Depot on 6 April 1945 [S12, p. 11], [S1].

## Burner practice and instrumentation

Yamato ran her flank acceptance runs with two idle burners in each boiler. Interrogation of the engineer officer indicated that all burners in all boilers were used frequently thereafter in service, "after the efficiency of the plant fell off". Tachometer speeds of blowers were taken rather than wind-box pressures. Tank soundings of fuel were taken, but neither oil-flow meters nor capacity curves were available, so the operators could learn nothing about instantaneous fuel rate. Yamato's engineer officer was a Technical Captain. The mission could not establish why eleven burners were fitted when nine would have carried flank load plus 20 per cent in excess [S17, p. 28].

## Documents the mission forwarded

Twenty boiler design drawings for Yamato were obtained and sent to the Bureau of Ships as NavTechJap documents ND50-1950.1 to .20, covering the boiler assembly, steam and water drum details, drum attachments, tubes, downcomers, the superheater drum and its piping, the front casing, firebrick arrangements, the internal feed pipe, the bottom blow valve, the main and auxiliary feed water valve, the main steam stop valve, the safety valve and its pilot, the internal superheater steam pipe in the steam drum, the float box for the feed water regulator, the steam baffle, the dry pipe and the combustion chamber baffle plates. A further 84 documents, ND50-1951.1 to .84, cover Akitsuki [S17, pp. 35, 103].

Machinery acceptance trial test reports were obtained for almost all principal ship classes and were forwarded to the Bureau of Ships without field analysis; ND50-1952.1 and .2 are Yamato's, in two parts, and ND50-1952.3 is Musashi's [S17, pp. 35, 105]. The trial figures themselves are not printed in the report.

## Conflicts between sources

- Shaft horsepower. 163,000 in the mission's characteristics table [S16, p. 7]; 165,000 in its narrative comparison with the Nachi class [S16, p. 7]; 150,000 as design and as navypedia gives it [S11, p. 9], [S28]; a maximum of 138,000 total shaft horsepower in the Japanese acceptance trial reports [S17, p. 7]; 167,310 shp on Musashi's overload trial [S2].
- Speed. 28 knots in the characteristics table [S16, p. 7]; 27 knots designed [S11, p. 9], [S28], [S12, p. 10]; 27.7 knots on Yamato's trials [S11, p. 9]; 28.05 knots on Musashi's overload trial [S2]; 27.46 knots [S9].
- Fuel capacity. 1,900 tons [S16, p. 7] against 6,300 t [S28].
- Endurance. 7,392 miles at 19.4 knots [S16, p. 7] against 7,200 nm at 16 knots [S28].
- Fuel consumption at full power. 62.7 metric tons an hour with twelve boilers fired on nine burners each [S17, p. 7], against the standardised boiler figure of 16,000 pounds of oil an hour maximum per boiler, which for twelve boilers would be a different total [S17, p. 7].
