# Iowa class: armament and ammunition handling

Status: partial (see gaps-03.md)

Sources for this facet are numbered S81 to S103 in `sources-03.md`. The deepest primary source is OP 769, the Bureau of Ordnance pamphlet for the 16-inch three-gun turret. The copy online is the edition dated 15 October 1967, prepared for the 30 April 1968 reactivation of New Jersey; it supersedes the June 1943 preliminary edition and the December 1953 first revision [S82]. Every number taken from it is therefore labelled (OP 769, 1968 ed.) and cross-checked where a 1944 or 1945 source exists. The 1945 Gun Mount and Turret Catalog, OP 1112, is the wartime check [S86]. The 1944 Naval Ordnance and Gunnery text uses the 16-inch/45 turret of the North Carolina and South Dakota classes as its worked example and notes that the newest battleships carry 16-inch guns of either 45 or 50 calibers; it is cited only for arrangements it states as common [S89, p. 78].

## Main battery: 16-inch/50 Mark 7 gun

### Count and placement

Each ship carries nine 16-inch 50-caliber guns in three three-gun turrets on the centerline, two forward and one aft of the superstructure [S81, p. 1-1]. The gun house of turret II stands above the level of the first superstructure deck; the turret III gun house is higher above the main deck than that of turret I [S81, p. 1-1]. The arrangement gives nine guns on either beam, six ahead, and three astern [S81, p. 1-1]. The gun is the 16-inch Gun Mark 7 Mod 0 [S81, p. 3-1; S86, p. 562].

The slide assembly for each gun is over 33.5 ft long, 79 in across the trunnions, 116.5 in high, and weighs 51.5 tons; it rides in trunnion bearings of 18.5 in diameter, each holding 24 rollers 2.5 in in diameter and 3.5 in long [S87, §7C1, §7C3].

### Construction and liner

The gun is a built-up gun of forged steel: a liner, a tube, a jacket, hoops, locking rings, a liner locking ring, and a yoke ring [S81, p. 3-3]. The pieces are assembled by heating and expanding each one and slipping it over the tube; the liner is inserted into the tube, jacket, and hoop assembly from the breech end until its shoulder engages the breech end of the tube, and it is locked by the liner locking ring, which threads into the jacket [S81, p. 3-3]. Navweaps lists the parts as liner, A tube, jacket, three hoops, two locking rings, tube and liner locking ring, yoke ring, and screw box liner, and says some components were autofretted [S84]. The 1957 text lists a liner, a tube, a jacket, three hoops, and a yoke ring [S87, §7B1]. Navweaps adds that the ring joining jacket and hoop was cast, machined, cut in half, and screwed onto the barrel because its rims were too deep to shrink on in a gun pit [S84].

Barrel life was rated at 290 equivalent service rounds when the gun entered service in World War II, with later estimates of about 350; an HC round at 2,690 fps counted as 0.43 ESR [S84]. The gun with the highest serial number proved was S/N 487, proved on 23 October 1947 [S84].

### Breech mechanism, firing lock, gas ejector

The gun is chambered for a bag charge and has a carrier-type breech mechanism, the 16-inch Breech Mechanism Mark 4 Mod 0, with Firing Lock Mark 14 Mod 5, Gas Ejector Mark 5 Mod 0, and Yoke Mark 5 Mod 0 [S81, p. 3-1]. OP 1112 describes it as a stepped-thread, rotating and swinging plug type [S86, p. 562]. Navweaps calls it a Smith-Asbury Welin block that opens downward, with the screw box liner and plug threads arranged in fifteen sectors of 24 degrees; a hand lever rotates and unlocks the plug, which is then pulled open by hand, and after loading the plug is swung back up pneumatically and locked with the lever [S84]. The 1957 text says the plug rotates 29 degrees to lock, the mushroom alone weighs over 220 lb, and plug and carrier together weigh about 2,000 lb [S87, §7B2]. Counterbalance and closing cylinders take air from the gas ejector system reduced from 200 to about 40 psi; the swing of the operating lever is always completed by hand [S87, §7B2].

A salvo latch prevents the breech from being opened after closing until the gun has fired; it is released by the recoil of the gun and locks again when the plugman opens the breech [S87, §7B2; S81, p. 3-1]. For drills the latch is pinned in its unlocked position so the crew does not form the habit of unlocking it by hand [S87, §7B2].

The firing lock is a hand-primed wedge type that fires the primer electrically or by percussion only when the breech is completely closed [S81, p. 3-2]. Its safety features prevent electrical contact and prevent cocking until the wedge is closed, and the wedge cannot be fully closed unless the breech is closed and locked [S87, §7B3]. The primer cartridge is ejected automatically when the breech opens; after a misfire the wedge can be retracted and reprimed without opening the breech [S87, §7B3; S84]. OP 1112 lists the primer as Mark 15 Mod 1 [S86, p. 562].

The gas ejector is an automatic low-pressure air system that clears the bore when the breech is opened after firing and also supplies the breech-closing cylinders [S81, p. 3-2]. OP 1112 rates the Mark 5 Mod 0 ejector at 14.5 cu ft of free air per second per gun at 175 psi [S86, p. 561]. Air at 150 to 200 psi comes from the ship's compressors through the turret's central column; the ejector valve opens automatically as the plug is turned and is closed by hand when the bore is clear, and three nozzles in the screw box liner direct the jets forward [S87, §7B4]. An auxiliary ejector hose is stowed overhead near each breech [S87, §7B4]. In film the purge is visible as a puff of white smoke a few seconds after the shot [S84].

### Rate of fire

OP 769 gives two rounds per minute per gun and says that in continuous fire each of the turret's three guns fires approximately every 30 seconds [S81, p. 1-1, p. 2-1]. Navweaps gives 2 rounds per minute [S84]. The three guns are fired left, right, center, with a 0.06 second delay between them and no delay for the left gun [S81, p. A2-2; S82]. Navweaps attributes the delay to delay coils and says it, with the wide gun spacing, improved the salvo pattern [S84].

The rotating structure has six levels: the shelf plate at the bottom of the gun house, the pan floor at the bottom of the gun pits, the electric deck (machinery floor) beneath the gun pits, the upper and lower projectile flats, and the powder handling platform at the bottom [S81, p. 1-3]. Turret II has a seventh level, which belongs to the fixed structure [S81, p. 1-1]. The 1957 text gives the same six levels and says the gun house holds the turret officer's booth, three gun rooms, and two sight stations, all separated by flametight bulkheads, with the only entrance hatch from the weather deck opening into the booth [S87, §7A4]. The gun girders divide the gun pit into three gun pockets, and the powder hoist trunks rise inside the two box girders [S81, p. 1-3]. Guns can be removed and replaced without dismantling the gun house [S84].

### Armor (cross-reference facet 01)

Turret armor thicknesses as given in Sumrall and in Garzke and Dulin: face 17.0 in (43.2 cm) class B over 2.5 in STS, sides 9.5 in (24.1 cm) class A over 0.75 in STS, rear 12.0 in (30.5 cm) class A over 0.75 in STS, roof 7.25 in (18.4 cm) over 0.75 in STS [S84]. OP 769 lists the rear plate at 12.00 in and the rear intermediate roof plate at 7.25 in [S81, p. 1-4]. The upper barbette is 17.30 in (43.9 cm) thick as standard and 11.80 in (30.0 cm) at the front and rear sides; the lower barbette is heavy steel plate about three inches thick between the second and third decks [S81, p. 1-7, p. 1-9]. Each gun house has eleven openings in its armor: three gun ports, two access openings, four sight hood openings, and two periscope openings; turrets II and III have two more for the rangefinder hood [S81, p. 1-4]. Each gun port is sealed by splinter plates, shield plates on the slide, and a three-ply rubberized fabric buckler [S81, p. 1-4]. See facet 01 for the full armor scheme.

Navweaps records that training arcs were reduced during the 1980s activations to protect new electronics (turret I 139 degrees, turret II 126 degrees, turret III 128 degrees each side) [S84].

Each gun has its own elevating gear: a 60 hp electric motor driving a variable-displacement hydraulic pump (A-end) and fixed-displacement hydraulic motor (B-end) that turns an elevating nut on an elevating screw pinned to the rear of the slide [S87, §7D1]. OP 1112 lists three 60 hp elevating motors, one 300 hp training motor, three 75 hp projectile hoist motors, three 100 hp powder hoist motors, and two 15 hp parbuckling motors per turret [S86, p. 561]. The training motor of 300 hp (540 hp overload) drives one A-end and two B-ends that turn the training pinions on the training circle; spring-set brakes hold the turret when power is off [S87, §7D2]. The training circle has 210 teeth [S81, p. 1-9]. Training pinions were crowded together because the turret was sized like the 16-inch/45 turret of the preceding classes [S84]. Elevating gear is Mark 5 Mods 0, 1, and 2, training gear Mark 2 Mod 0, with elevation receiver-regulator Mark 10 Mod 0 and train receiver-regulator Mark 18 Mods 5 to 7 [S86, p. 561; S81, p. 1-9].

### Gun separation

The center gun bore is on the turret centerline; the right and left bores are parallel to it and each 122 in (3.10 m) from it [S81, p. 4-1, p. A2-2]. Navweaps notes that Campbell's figure of 117 in is a mistake carried over from the 16-inch/45 turrets [S84]. The common trunnion axis is 198 in (5.03 m) above the plane of the roller path rollers and 182 in (4.62 m) forward of the turret transverse centerline [S81, p. 3-3].

### Sights and rangefinder

Each turret has duplicate sight stations in enclosed compartments outboard of the wing guns, roughly on the turret's transverse centerline; each holds a pointer's telescope, a trainer's telescope, and a sight setter's indicator, and either station can transmit gun elevation and train orders to the machinery floor [S87, §7D3]. The pointers' and trainers' telescopes are Mark 66 [S81, p. A3-1]. A sight pointer's handwheels transmit elevation orders to the gun elevation indicator of each of the three gun layers; a sight trainer's handwheels offset the training gear A-end directly [S81, p. 2-5]. The sight setter matches dials on his indicator by hand crank to enter sight angle, sight deflection, and parallax range [S81, p. 2-5].

As built, all three turrets carried 46-foot, 25-power rangefinders: the stereoscopic Mark 52 in turrets II and III and the coincidence Mark 53 in turret I; the Mark 52 weighed 10,500 lb and the Mark 53 was removed from all four ships in the 1950s and its openings plated over [S84]. OP 1112 lists the turret rangefinder as Mark 52 Mod 0 or Mark 53 Mod 0 [S86, p. 561]. In the 1968 edition the rangefinder operator's station exists in turrets II and III only, and only those turrets have the extra armor openings for the rangefinder hood [S81, p. 2-4, p. 1-4]. The rangefinder is mounted across the after end of the turret officer's booth, with the local auxiliary computer (Mark 3 Mod 2 in 1945) beside it [S87, §7A4; S86, p. 561].

### Crew, by station

OP 769 requires a minimum crew of 77 for three-gun operation: 46 men below the electric deck and at the tops of the powder hoist trunks serve ammunition, 12 men in the gun compartments work the guns, and the remaining 19 are the three gun layers and the train operator on the electric deck, three men in each of the two sight stations, and nine turret controlmen in the gun house [S81, p. 2-1]. Navweaps reads the same passage as one officer and 76 men, and puts the normal crew nearer 94 once magazine and powder handling spaces are counted [S84]. The 1957 text says 77 inside the turret, plus 6 powder passers in the annular space between the powder handling room and the magazines, plus 12 powder passers in the magazines [S87, §7E6]. All levels are isolated with intercommunicating hatches closed while the turret operates, and the pan floor is unmanned [S81, p. 2-3].

Both AP Mark 8 and HC Mark 13 have a radius of ogive of 144 in (9 calibers) [S81, p. A2-1; S84]. All full-caliber projectiles have a bourrelet diameter of 15.977 in (40.058 cm) and two rear bourrelets flanking the driving band [S84]. The AP Mark 8 grew out of the 2,240 lb AP Mark 5 planned for the original 16-inch/50 design; the shell handling system was redesigned for the heavier round before any ship of the class was laid down [S84]. Around the end of 1944 the Mark 8 Mod 6, with better hardening and a blunter nose, replaced earlier Mods, and BuOrd ordered ships to return the older Mods as Mod 6 became available [S84]. OP 769 states that AP projectiles can penetrate up to 16 in of armor plate and that HC projectiles are fuze-set to detonate on contact or at a set time [S81, p. 1-1].

The HC Mark 13 was issued with a steel nose plug that could be exchanged on board for a Mark 29 point detonating fuze or a Mark 42 or Mark 62 mechanical time fuze settable to 45 seconds; base fuzes were the Mark 28, Mark 39, or Mark 48 [S84]. In the turret the cradle operator acts as fuze setter for HC rounds, taking orders from the secondary battery computer's fuze follow-up operator [S81, p. 2-9]. Navweaps believes time fuzes were set by hand on the loading tray [S84].

### Color codes

OP 4 of May 1943 prescribes black for armor-piercing projectiles and dark green for antiaircraft, antiaircraft-common, field, and bombardment projectiles, with the ogive painted for one caliber from the point in a color for the filler: yellow for Explosive D, dark green for TNT; target and blind-loaded projectiles are unpainted or red [S94, pp. 47 to 48]. Navweaps confirms AP bodies black and HC bodies green with a yellow nose for Explosive D, and a narrow band below the nose for the splash dye color [S84]. The AP Mark 8 carried a nominal 1.5 lb dye bag (up to 3.0 lb to bring light projectiles to weight); the dye colors assigned were Iowa orange, New Jersey blue, Missouri red, and Wisconsin green [S84].

### Where projectiles are stowed

Each turret has two circular projectile flats, upper and lower, in the rotating structure; the 1944 text puts them about 25 ft and 33 ft below the turret proper [S89, p. 92]. Each flat is split by a circular bulkhead into an inner machinery space, 17 ft in diameter, and an outer stowage and handling compartment bounded by the turret foundation, about 35 ft inside diameter [S81, p. 7-1; S89, p. 93]. The outer compartment floor is three concentric rings. The outer ring is a fixed shelf attached to the foundation and holds the fixed stowage in two concentric rows of projectiles (one row on turret I's upper flat) [S81, p. 7-2]. The center ring is part of the rotating structure and is the handling platform; the three projectile hoist tubes pass through its rear part [S81, p. 7-2]. The inner ring is a roller-mounted, power-driven platform that rotates either way with respect to the center ring and holds two concentric rows of projectiles; its power drive is a 40 hp motor per ring [S81, p. 7-2; S84]. Projectiles stand on their bases and each is lashed by chain to a coaming flange and securing plates; the chains have pelican fasteners for quick release [S81, p. 7-2, p. 2-33]. Turret II's third level is fixed stowage in the powder handling room immediately below the outer ring of the lower flat, again two rows lashed the same way [S81, p. 7-2].

Projectiles are brought aboard horizontally onto the main deck, fitted with a two-position carrier, lowered by a 1.5-ton electric hoist down an ammunition loading trunk on either side of the turret to the magazine level, moved horizontally by an overhead trolley conveyor around the annular handling space, and hoisted vertically through a pair of round hatches at the rear of each flat's outer ring by a chain hoist [S81, p. 7-2, p. 2-32 to 2-34; S87, §7E1]. The trunks are reachable only with the turret trained to 266 degrees (turret I), 280 degrees (turret II), or 193 degrees (turret III), after which portable beams with sheaves are bolted to the gun house roof [S81, p. 2-32]. Navweaps quotes the Navy figure of three projectiles per minute per hoist as the supply rate to the gun house [S84; S87, §7E1].

Propellant bags were sewn from raw silk; the primer patch at one end was quilted for even distribution and usually colored red, and the other end carried a handling strap [S84]. The 1943 D839 grain for full charges was 2 in long, 1 in in diameter, with seven perforations of 0.060 in and a web of 0.193 to 0.197 in [S84]. Reduced charges are the same length as full charges so that six bags still span the chamber, but smaller in diameter, so the last bag can tumble when the gun elevates and cause a hangfire [S84]. OP 4 of 1943 states that unstacked charges for 14-inch and 16-inch guns had been replaced by stacked charges, in which the grains stand on end in layers for a tighter charge, easier handling, and longer bag life [S94, pp. 121 to 122]. Fresh smokeless powder in World War II gave shot-to-shot velocity variation of no more than plus or minus 10 fps, and often 5 fps [S84]. A sheet of lead foil for decoppering is placed between the first and second bags [S85].

### Powder tanks

Each powder tank holds three bags [S84]. Mark 4 tanks held full service charges and Mark 8 tanks held reduced charges [S84]. OP 4 states that aluminum tanks were provided for 6-inch/47 bag guns, 8-inch/55 guns on heavy cruisers, and 16-inch guns on battleships beginning with BB 55, with steel substitutes during the aluminum shortage [S94, p. 106]. Every tank holding all or part of a charge carries a 2-inch white band around the edge of the cover, stencilled in black with the caliber, the powder index, the proportion of the charge, and the initial velocity, and a wired tag repeating the bag's identification tag [S94, p. 47]. In the magazines the tanks are stacked horizontally in bins between floor and overhead, held by their interlocking flanged ends and by portable battens; gravity-actuated powder handling cars inside the magazine hoist bags singly so that a tank can be emptied, stacked, and refilled in the top rows [S81, p. 2-33]. Tanks are carried through the loading trunks by a powder tank carrier of two wire rope lengths joined by a ring [S81, p. 2-33].

### Magazine locations, count, and capacity

The powder magazines are outside the turret foundation on the platform deck beneath the lower projectile flat [S87, §7E5; S89, p. 100]; the 1944 text places the powder handling rooms and magazines on the second platform deck below the lowest projectile flat [S89, p. 79]. Navweaps states there were originally twenty powder magazines: six for turret I, eight for turret II, and six for turret III [S84]. Powder passes from the magazines through powder scuttles into the annular handling space (the powder circle), and through a second set of scuttles into the powder handling room at the bottom of the rotating structure; the double wall isolates the hoists from the magazines, and this room does the work of a working chamber, which the design does not have [S84; S81, p. 1-7]. OP 769 gives six powder passing scuttles in the lower part of each circular foundation (four in turret I) aligned with scuttles in the powder handling room bulkhead, plus two watertight doors; the scuttles are hand-rotated horizontal cylinders that pass bags singly while keeping a mechanical seal between turret and magazine spaces [S81, p. 1-7]. Navweaps gives the same counts as four scuttle sets for turret I and six each for turrets II and III [S84]. The powder handling flat outside the foundation gives a working strip 4.50 ft wide in front of the scuttles [S81, p. 1-9]. Magazine capacity in tanks or charges per magazine is not stated in any fetched source; the only whole-ship tank counts are the 1955 Missouri figures above [S84]. No source found for capacity per magazine; see gaps-03.md.

### Sprinkling and flooding

The turret sprinkling system is designed to wet all ammunition in the turret, whether in transit in the hoists, waiting in the loading trays, or ready for loading into the gun [S81, p. 1-19]. It draws from the ship's firemain and from two sprinkling tanks in the gun house kept under air pressure, and it can be worked from local and remote stations inside the turret and from one station outside [S81, p. 1-19]. Nine control stations, A to I, cover the powder hoists, gun rooms, annular space, powder trays, projectile flats, and the powder handling room; station A is the turret officer's booth, station B is beneath the turret overhang outside, station C is in each gun room, stations D to G are in the powder handling room and annular space, station H is on each projectile flat, and station I is in the turret officer's booth [S81, p. 1-27]. A separate electrically operated system sprinkles the projectile stowage areas of each flat and the powder passing scuttles of the powder handling room, and it can be controlled from inside the turret, from the overhang outside, or from damage control stations [S81, p. 1-27]. The petty officer in charge of each projectile flat operates the sprinkling controls of station H if a fire hazard appears [S81, p. 2-11]. Water-filled tanks for powder submergence stand next to all powder handling personnel in case a bag breaks in transit [S81, p. 2-12]. The 1944 text says the turret and its handling rooms are water, gas, and weather sealed, that ventilation keeps a slight positive pressure, and that semi-automatic sprinkling covers gun compartments, hoist trunks, and projectile stowage [S89, p. 79]. Navy Regulations required all immersion tanks to be filled before firing, and tanks receiving turret drains to be filled to cover the drain pipes [S89, p. 101]. No fetched source describes magazine flooding valves or flooding rates for the 16-inch magazines; see gaps-03.md.

## The loading cycle, magazine to breech

### Overview and timing

OP 769 shows the cycle as a 30-second dial, figure 2-1, with the gun-room actions on the outer ring and the ammunition service actions on inner rings [S81, fig. 2-1]. The sequence below is read from that dial; the second marks are approximate positions on the dial, not printed values, and the dial describes the 1968 configuration [S81, fig. 2-1].

| about 5 to 8 s | open breech and wipe mushroom | [S81, fig. 2-1] |

On the inner rings of the same dial, the projectile hoist raises the next projectile from about the 26-second mark through the firing instant to about 4 seconds, while the powder car is loaded from about 0 to 3.5 seconds (lower door open, then closed) and hoisted from about 4 to 9 seconds; parbuckling the next projectile into the hoist, rotating the projectile ring, and loading the powder trays fill the rest of the dial [S81, fig. 2-1]. Recoil lasts 0.43 s and counterrecoil 0.90 s at 15 degrees elevation [S81, p. A2-2; S84]. During runout the gun is lowered automatically to the loading angle [S84; S81, p. 2-24]. The rammer's projectile stroke takes 1.7 s at a little under 14 ft per second, and the powder stroke is slower [S87, §7E4].

### Stage 1: magazine to powder handling room

The magazine crew opens the powder tanks and passes bags to the magazine scuttle; Navy Regulations allow no more than one charge per gun to be exposed in a magazine or handling room, and no more than one charge per gun to accumulate in each later flameproof stage [S89, p. 100; S81, p. A4-2]. The third powderman of each three-man team rotates the magazine scuttle and passes one bag into the annular handling space, turns a mechanical indicator to tell the magazine crew to refill the scuttle, then passes the bag into the handling room scuttle; he repeats this while there is a demand [S81, p. 2-12]. Each flame-proof compartment of the ammunition train is kept closed except during the actual passage of ammunition, and no stage is open to both the preceding and following stage at once [S89, p. 100; S81, p. A4-2].

### Stage 2: powder handling room to powder car

The first and second powdermen of each team squat at the handling room scuttle, rotate it, take out the bag, and place it in the two-level loading tray at the foot of the hoist trunk with its red ignition end toward the rear; each man repeats this three times to put six bags in the tray [S81, p. 2-12]. Navweaps notes that the bag weighs about 115 lb and that this repeated crouch-lift-carry, three times every 30 seconds per man, was what led gunnery officers to add men to the room [S84]. The powder door operator at the bottom of the trunk unlatches the lower doors with a foot pedal and opens them by hand once the car is at the loading station; the first and second powdermen roll the six bags into the car's two trays, tell him to close the doors, and he sets his indicator switch to signal the hoist operator that the car may be hoisted [S81, p. 2-11, p. 2-12]. The lower door cannot be opened unless the car is at its loading position, and the car cannot be hoisted or lowered until the door is closed [S87, §7E5; S81, p. 11-3].

### Stage 3: the powder hoist (car type)

Each gun has an independent 16-inch Powder Hoist Mark 9: a car-type vertical lift, electric-hydraulically driven, with selective servo or manual control, whose trunk runs from the powder handling room to the gun house roof inside a gun girder box [S81, p. 11-1]. The car rides on rails between lower and upper door assemblies and carries a full service charge of six bags on two vertically spaced trays that tilt separately by hand levers [S81, p. 11-1; S87, §7E5]. Mods 0, 1, and 2 serve the right, center, and left guns of turret I, Mods 3 to 5 turret II, and Mods 6 to 8 turret III [S81, p. 11-1].

The car is hoisted by a 5/8-inch wire rope over a sheave under the turret roof and lowered by gravity [S87, §7E5; S89, p. 100]. The 1944 text, describing the 16-inch/45 turret, gives the hoist motor as 75 hp with a 135 hp overload rating and mentions a power-failure brake on the hoisting drum [S89, p. 100]. The car, both door assemblies, and the starting lever are interlocked: both doors cannot be open at once, the lever is locked at neutral unless both doors are closed or the car is unloading at the upper station, and neither door can open until the car is at that station [S81, p. 11-3]. The hoist operator at the top of the trunk hoists the car when the lower door operator signals by the visual system, and dumps the trays on the gun captain's signal; a window in the bulkhead lets him see the gun captain [S81, p. 2-9].

### Stage 4: projectile flats to cradle

The turret officer designates which flat serves the hoists and whether AP or HC is loaded; the hoist operators on that flat clutch in their control handles and the parbuckling gear and ring drive are started [S81, p. 2-21]. A shellman and a parbuckler work as a team: the shellman hooks the end of the snubbing rope into an eye in the deck and steadies the projectile, the parbuckler loops the rope twice around a gypsy head and heaves the projectile, sliding on its base, into the hoist aperture, where spring-loaded shutters retain it [S81, p. 2-21]. Six gypsy heads on each flat are driven at constant speed by one electric motor through miter gears and shafting; each head has an adjustable slip clutch [S81, p. 8-1]. The ring operator rotates the inner ring one cycle every three rounds per hoist, and the ring stops automatically with three projectiles beside each hoist aperture; a three-dial indicator, lit by contact makers at the hoist stations, tells him when he may rotate [S81, p. 2-10, p. 2-21]. The 1957 text says the ring moves through 30-degree arcs with automatic stops and that all three hoists can be served from one ring at once [S87, §7E1]. The forward three-man team replenishes the inner ring from the fixed stowage with the same gear [S81, p. 2-10].

Each gun has a 16-inch Projectile Hoist Mark 8 (Mods 0, 1, 2 for right, center, left), a reversible hydraulic rack-and-pawl tubular lift with a separately controlled power-driven cradle [S81, p. 9-1]. The left and right tubes rise vertically from the lower flat to above the electric deck, then curve aft and up to the cradles; the center tube is straight [S81, p. 9-1; S87, §7E2]. A hydraulic cylinder between the projectile flat decks drives a rack in the tube; rack pawls and tube pawls spaced one stage apart lift the projectile, base down, one stage per stroke, and the tube pawls hold it while the rack returns [S87, §7E2; S84]. OP 769 gives the total lift as five stages from the lower flat and four from the upper, and the 1944 and 1957 texts give four from the lower and three from the upper [S81, p. 9-1; S89, p. 97; S87, §7E2]. The tube holds four projectiles with a fifth in the cradle, and the rack carries a full load of 13,500 lb of AP projectiles; the lift from the lower flat to the cradle opening is 34.78 ft [S81, p. 9-3]. The 1957 text gives a piston of about 6 in diameter, a stroke of about 8 ft, and 700 psi from a 75 hp motor to lift four projectiles; a solenoid brake holds the hoist on power failure [S87, §7E2; S81, p. 9-3]. There is no shell cage and no ready-use stack in the gun house; the loaded hoist tube is the ready supply [S84]. Duplicate control handles at the loading apertures on both flats are locked by mechanical and electrical interlocks while a projectile is being loaded, while a projectile is in the cradle, and during the ramming cycle [S87, §7E2].

### Stage 5: the gun room

The gun captain stands on the loader's platform, the primerman on a platform below the shelf plate, the cradle operator at the cradle, and the rammer operator at the rammer hand lever just forward of the booth bulkhead [S81, fig. 2-2, p. 2-9, p. 10-1]. The verbatim OP 769 firing procedure, page 2-21, runs as follows [S81, p. 2-21; S85].

1. Breech opening. The gun captain opens the breech, wipes the mushroom, and looks through the bore for gas or burning remnants; he announces "bore clear," presses the bore clear switch, and trips the gas ejector valve. For the first load he must release the salvo latch by hand [S81, p. 2-21].
2. Priming. As soon as "bore clear" is announced the primerman inserts a live primer in the firing lock [S81, p. 2-21].
3. Cradle opening. With a projectile latched in the cradle and the bore clear switch pressed, the cradle operator presses a foot pedal to unlatch the cradle and sets the control valve to LOWER CRADLE; the cradle tips forward and the spanning tray enters the open breech. The cradle interlock in Ready Light Circuit 1R prevents this until the bore clear switch has been pressed [S81, p. 2-21].
4. Projectile ramming. The rammer lever is moved toward RAM, the projectile is rammed and seated in the rifling, and the lever is moved to WITHDRAW; the upper powder door opens with these movements [S81, p. 2-21]. The chain rams at full power to a jammed stop so that the rotating band bites the rifling and the projectile cannot slide back when the gun elevates [S87, §7E4]. Navy Regulations forbid ramming a projectile with a powder bag between it and the rammer head [S89, p. 101; S81, p. A4-3].
5. Powder transfer. With the rammer withdrawn, the car hoisted, and the upper door open, the hoist operator tips the lower car tray; the powder door interlock in circuit 1R keeps the upper door shut until the rammer is back. Three bags roll down the door, which forms a shelf between trunk and spanning tray, and the gun captain and cradle operator spread them two forward and one aft to leave room for the next three. The hoist operator lowers the car about 23 inches and tips the second tray [S81, p. 2-21; S87, §7E6].
6. Powder ramming. With six bags on the tray the rammer lever goes to RAM, the bags are rammed into the chamber, and the lever goes to WITHDRAW; the upper door closes with these movements. The 1957 text says the rearmost bag is placed no more than 4 in from the mushroom [S81, p. 2-21; S87, §7E6]. Dick Landgraff's note records that in practice crews rammed the first three bags slowly after placing the lead decoppering foil behind the first bag, then dumped and rammed the next three, with the gun captain watching so the last bag lay close to the mushroom [S85].
7. Cradle up and breech closed. When the rammer is fully withdrawn and the upper door closed, the cradle is set to RAISE CRADLE and retracts from the gun; the gun captain releases the breech hold-down latch, opens the air valve to the closing cylinder, latches the operating lever as the plug rotates closed, steps off the platform, and sets his ready switch to READY [S81, p. 2-21; S87, §7E6].
8. Firing. With the breech closed, the ready switch at READY, the firing circuit closed in the turret, and the turret officer's selective switch at DIRECTOR, the gun fires electrically by remote control; recoil trips the salvo latch and the cycle repeats [S81, p. 2-21].

The rammer for each gun is a 16-inch Rammer Mark 5 (Mods 0, 1, 2), an electric-hydraulic drive turning a sprocket that pushes a folding chain, mounted on the shelf plate overhang aft of the booth bulkhead; the hand lever has automatic stops at each end of chain travel and the A-end tilt box returns to neutral at the end of a withdraw stroke [S81, p. 10-1]. Projectile travel is 226.43 in, the chain head starts 7.925 in behind the projectile and 108.025 in from the breech, and the motor is 60 hp weighing 1,750 lb with its reducer [S81, p. 10-2, p. A2-1]. The 1957 text gives a 25-foot chain, a head 8 in behind the projectile base and 9 ft from the breech face, and 19 ft of projectile travel [S87, §7E4]. The rammer lever has three ramming speeds through dump valves: full forward, fastest, for the projectile, and the two slower ones for bags [S85]. The spanning tray and catwalks are brass to reduce sparking, and the gun captain wears a gauntlet to wipe the gas check pad [S84].

### Interlocks that keep flash from the magazines

- Flame seal: a ring bracket between the lower projectile flat and the powder handling room isolates the powder room from the rest of the turret while allowing the turret to train [S81, p. 1-7].
- Double scuttles: bags pass magazine scuttle to annular space to handling room scuttle, each a rotating cylinder that keeps a mechanical seal [S81, p. 1-7; S84].
- Powder hoist doors: lower and upper doors cannot both be open; each opens only with the car at its station; the car cannot move with the lower door open [S81, p. 11-3; S87, §7E5].
- Ready Light Circuit 1R: cradle cannot lower until the bore clear switch is pressed; upper powder door cannot open until the rammer has withdrawn after projectile ramming [S81, p. 2-21].
- Projectile hoist controls locked during loading, with a projectile in the cradle, and during ramming [S87, §7E2].
- Salvo latch holds the breech closed until the gun has recoiled [S87, §7B2].
- Firing lock cannot fire until breech and lock are fully closed [S87, §7B3].
- Circuit DS opens a gun's firing circuit and lights a warning when its line of fire approaches the ship's structure [S81, p. 1-28].
- Hatches between levels are closed and secured during operation, and the pan floor is unmanned [S81, p. 2-3].
- Navy Regulations article 972 limits exposed powder to one charge per gun per flameproof stage and forbids opening a breech with a live primer in the lock [S89, pp. 100 to 101; S81, pp. A4-2 to A4-3].

### Misfire

On a misfire the gun captain pulls the release and sets his ready switch to SAFE, which opens that gun's firing circuit and brings the gun to the 5-degree loading position; the primer is extracted and replaced, and if the fault persists the gun may be fired by percussion until the electrician checks the circuit [S81, p. 2-26]. A firing lock with a live primer is never opened unless the firing circuit is broken outside the lock [S89, p. 101].

AA Common bodies took a mechanical time or proximity nose fuze for AA use and a point detonating fuze for surface use [S91]. Proximity-fuzed AA VT rounds came into use from late 1942, and by mid-1944 most front-line ships carried about three VT rounds for each time-fuzed AA Common round and fired them in that ratio [S91].

### Handling rooms, hoists, and stowage

The twin enclosed mount has its own ammunition handling room beneath the gun platform, with ready service powder and projectiles there and a lower ammunition hoist bringing rounds up from below [S89, pp. 118 to 119]. Each gun has a projectile hoist and a powder hoist on the rotating structure; the hoists for the right gun come up on the left side of that gun and those for the left gun on its right, inboard between the guns, with the powder delivery tubes abaft the projectile hoists [S91; S89, p. 146]. The projectile hoist is a semi-automatic endless-chain dual-tube hoist with a 10 hp electric-hydraulic drive; two flights alternate so that one is at the top of one tube when the other is at the bottom of the opposite tube, and a fuze-setting mechanism turns the time ring as the projectile rises nose down, controlled by a regulator on the carriage inboard of the right gun that follows the computer in automatic or a hand crank in manual [S88, §8B24; S89, p. 137]. Interlock cams stop the drive while a projectile is being placed in the hoist, when a loaded flight is at the top, and while the projectile is being removed [S88, §8B24]. The powder hoist is a single-tube endless chain with five flights, reversible, with a HOIST-STOP-LOWER lever; cases are loaded and removed by hand [S88, §8B25]. In the gun room the powder man takes the case from the hoist, removes the primer cap, and lays it on the tray; the projectile man takes the projectile from the hoist, lays it ahead of the case, and pulls the rammer lever to RAM; up to 40 degrees elevation the hot case ejects through the chute, above that the hot-case man removes it [S88, §8B21]. The upper handling room also holds a voice tube to the gun room and a 6-volt firing battery [S88, §8B28].

Navweaps gives the design outfit for North Carolina, South Dakota, and Iowa as 450 rounds per gun, and states that by 1945 the new battleships carried 500 rounds per gun in the magazines, mostly AA Common and AA VT, plus 40 special rounds per gun, plus 55 ready rounds per gun in the upper handling rooms directly beneath each mount [S91]. Magazine locations for the 5-inch battery by deck and frame are not stated in any fetched source; see gaps-03.md.

### Crew

About 27 men man the mount and the upper handling room, with more in the lower handling room and magazines for sustained fire [S88, §8B28]. The 1944 text puts the minimum for a single enclosed mount and its handling rooms at 17 men [S89, p. 144]. Stations named in the sources are mount captain, pointer, trainer, sight setter, checker, gun captain, projectile man, powder man, hot-case man, and fuze setter, with passers in the handling rooms [S88, §8B21, §8B26; S91; S89, p. 141].

## Anti-aircraft battery by date

### Designed battery

The design carried four quadruple 28 mm (1.1-inch) Mark 1 mounts and twelve 12.7 mm (0.50-inch) machine guns; the light battery was changed during building to 40 mm Bofors and 20 mm Oerlikons [S95]. The 1944 text confirms that 1.1-inch quad mounts had been used on the larger ships and were replaced by 40 mm twin and quad mounts, and that the 20 mm had almost entirely replaced the 0.50-caliber gun on large ships [S89, p. 150].

Ammunition is fixed, in four-round clips [S89, p. 150; S92]. OP 1112 gives one AA projectile (Mods of Mark 1 or Mark 2) as 1.98 lb, one round as 4.75 lb, and one clip as 20 lb, with cartridge cases Mark 1 (1.89 lb), Mark 2 (1.93 lb), and Mark 3 (1.53 lb) [S86, p. 21]. The 1944 text gives 4.8 lb per cartridge and slightly under 2 lb per projectile, with a tracer and a nose impact fuze armed by rotation [S89, p. 150]. Navweaps gives a burster of 0.148 to 0.150 lb cast TNT, a muzzle velocity of 2,890 fps (881 m/s) for US HE, a clip weight of 19 to 20 lb, and self-destroying HE set for 4,000 to 5,000 yards [S92]. US ships normally stowed about 2,000 rounds per gun [S92]. Tracer burned out at 5,000 yards horizontal [S92].

The autoloader holds two clips (eight rounds, ten with two loose rounds between clips); the first loader drops clips into the feed lips, and a loader interlock stops fire with two rounds left so that firing resumes as soon as a fresh clip drops in [S92]. In twin and quad mounts one gun of each pair fires at a time, tripping the other [S92]. The basic crew for a twin or quad mount is the mount captain, pointer, trainer, one loader for each gun, and at least one ammunition passer for each gun, more if the ready boxes are far from the mount; the pointer fires with a foot pedal and the passers bring clipped cartridges from the ready boxes or magazines [S89, p. 164; S92]. Each mount normally had a Mark 51 director crew of a pointer and a range setter as well [S92]. A December 1944 photograph in New Jersey shows a gun tub lined with racks holding four-round clips while crewmen clean and grease 40 mm rounds [S97]. Mark 57 radar directors for the 40 mm guns were fitted to New Jersey by June 1945 [S97].

The gun is an open-breech, air-cooled, blowback weapon whose barrel does not recoil; the breechblock is never locked and is moving forward when the round fires, and there is no counterrecoil brake [S89, p. 164; S93]. Usually every second or third round in a magazine carried a tracer [S89, p. 164]. The crew includes the gunner, a loader, and ammunition passers who hand loaded magazines to the loader from the ready boxes [S89, p. 165]. In 1943 the Mark 14 gyro gunsight was introduced, and a photograph of Iowa's forecastle 20 mm guns in spring 1943 shows a gunner using it [S93; S96]. Manually controlled mounts had safety rails instead of mechanical stops to keep the barrels from pointing into the ship [S93]. Late in the war many ships replaced pairs of single mounts with twin Mark 24 mounts to save weight and men [S93]. Effective range against aircraft rarely exceeded 1,000 yards, with fire opened at 1,200 to 1,300 yards [S93]. Navweaps notes that by late 1944 the 20 mm was judged too light to stop kamikazes and was replaced by 40 mm where possible [S93].

### Catapults, crane, and removal

The ships carried two catapults on the fantail and an aircraft crane on the after deck used to hoist the floatplane aboard after it landed in the slick made by a sharp turn [S97; S98]. Iowa's catapults were removed during her 1948 deactivation and Missouri's in a yard period of 9 to 26 May 1949 at Norfolk Naval Shipyard; navypedia dates the removal of catapults and seaplanes on all four to 1946 to 1948 [S96; S98; S95]. The catapult area became a helicopter landing area, and Sikorsky HO3S-1 helicopters operated from it in Korea [S96; S97]. Iowa's fantail floatplane crane was gone by her 1957 to 1958 configuration [S96]. The catapult mark and type, the crane type, and the aviation gasoline stowage and handling arrangements are not stated in any fetched source; see gaps-03.md.

## Weapon placement in words

This list combines the turret data of OP 769 with the dated photograph captions of NavSource. No fetched source gives frame numbers for the 5-inch, 40 mm, or 20 mm mounts; see gaps-03.md.

- Main battery: turret I centered 224 ft from the bow, turret II 296 ft, turret III 642 ft; turret II superfires over turret I; each turret's gun trunnion axis is 132 in forward of the turret's axis of rotation [S81, p. 1-1; S82].
- On the turret roofs: a quad 40 mm on turret II in New Jersey, Missouri, and Wisconsin from completion (Missouri's was 40 mm mount 1) and in Iowa by 1945; Iowa as completed had a 20 mm gallery on turret II and in December 1944 three 20 mm on turret I, where her sisters carried a quad 40 mm [S98; S96; S81, p. 1-4].
- Bow: a 20 mm tub at the stem head (not fitted in Iowa in April 1943 or New Jersey in August 1943) and, by late 1943, 40 mm tubs on the forecastle ahead of turret I (Missouri's were mounts 17 starboard and 18 port) [S96; S97; S98].
- Amidships: six quad 40 mm mounts between the stacks (Iowa, May 1943), three on each side on high platforms where the design's boats would have been [S96].
- Aft: 40 mm mounts forward of turret III (absent in New Jersey in August 1943) and many 20 mm singles on the after deck (Iowa, mid-1944) [S97; S96].
- Secondary battery: ten twin 5-inch mounts, arranged with bow-and-stern and broadside mounts per navweaps' train limits, positions by mount number not sourced [S91; S96].
- Fantail: two catapults and the aircraft crane [S97; S98].
