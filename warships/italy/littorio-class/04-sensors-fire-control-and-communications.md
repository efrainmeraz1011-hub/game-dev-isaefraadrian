# Littorio class: sensors, fire control and communications

Status: partial (see gaps.md)

## Directors and rangefinders

Three director towers were stacked on the conning tower, one above the other, and could train independently: anti-aircraft control at the top, then main battery, then secondary battery [S1, photograph caption].

The main battery director, the apparecchio di punteria generale or APG, stood at the top of the conning tower. Its lines of sight were stabilised, as were those of the sighting gear and the night fire-control stations of the 152 mm and 90 mm batteries, and even those of the machine-gun aiming columns [S19].

Each 381 mm turret carried its own rangefinder in the rear of the gunhouse, cut off from the guns by a splinter bulkhead [S1].

The day fire-control turrets of the 152 mm battery and the control turrets of the 90 mm battery were fully stabilised: their floors stayed horizontal whatever the ship did, so that the men inside felt neither pitch nor roll, and they held their training on the target while the ship was under helm [S19].

A separate small bridge for controlling the machine guns was built out from the forward funnel [S19; S20].

## Stabilisation of the sighting and aiming lines

A gyroscopic central computed the ship's changes of attitude in both rate and acceleration, that is the roll and pitch oscillations and the rotation under helm. Through special electrical machines called metaconformatori and metadinamo the gyro system drove electric motors acting on the elevation of the guns and the training of the 381 mm and 152 mm turrets, so that the lines of sight were not disturbed by the ship's motion. The same principle carried through to the stabilised platforms of the 90 mm mountings and their director turrets, driven by electric motors turning worm screws set orthogonally beneath them [S19].

## Anti-aircraft lookout stations

Roma carried twelve stabilised anti-aircraft lookout stations, six a side, immediately below the 90 mm director turrets. Each was shaped like a large egg with a wide window of glass, entered through an opening at the back, and each lookout watched a 30 degree spherical sector through a powerful binocular. A sighting was passed by an acoustic signal and a pointer on a dial to both the command bridge and the director turret on the same side [S19].

## Radar

Italian naval radar came from the Regio Istituto Elettrotecnico e delle Comunicazioni della Marina at Livorno. Work ran from the continuous-wave E.C.1 of 1936 through the E.C.1-bis and E.C.2 of 1937 to the pulsed E.C.3, modified from December 1940 as the E.C.3-bis. Interest revived only after the night action off Cape Matapan on 28 March 1941 made British possession of radar a certainty. Two sets came out of the prototypes: Folaga for coastal surveillance, working between 150 and 300 MHz, and Gufo for shipboard use, working between 400 and 750 MHz [S21].

Production went to SAFAR, with Marelli ordered to build 150 Folaga sets and SAFAR 50. Before 8 September 1943 only 13 Gufo and 4 Folaga sets had been delivered, plus four more prototypes, and on that date twelve operational ships carried the E.C.3 Mark III Gufo, six of them fitted only in the preceding August [S21].

The only Gufo performance figure found in a citable source is the working band, 400 to 750 MHz, that is 75 to 40 cm [S21]. Pulse length, beamwidth, rotation rate, detection range and power are in gaps.md.

From the spring of 1943 the Italian high command told ships to switch the radar on only close to enemy forces, on a mistaken German warning that the British had a warning receiver of the Metox kind; crews are reported to have used the Gufo as a search set anyway and left it out of the log to avoid punishment [S21].

The Italian base at Taranto had no radar on the night of the British attack in November 1940, and the aerophones then in use did not help the gunners find the target or direct the fire, which lengthened reaction time [S15].

## Radio and signals

One of the four bridges joining the fore mast to the conning tower served as the signal station [S19; S20].

The 200 mm armoured tube inside the conning tower carried the cable runs that passed every order and every report on weapons, machinery and safety systems, as well as the men moving between levels [S19; S25, p. 61].

The wreck survey of 2018 identified, among the structures of Roma, the command bridge, the steering position, the signal station, the rangefinders and the admiral's bridge [S12].

## Conflicts between sources

Dates of the radar fits differ. Littorio is given an EC 3 bis in August 1941, an updated set in April 1942 and the EC 3 ter in September 1942 [S28, p. 172]; another list puts the EC-3/bis in 1941, its removal and the EC-3/ter in spring 1942, and a further EC-3/ter in September 1943 [S22].
