# King George V class: compartments and deck plans

Status: partial (see gaps.md)

No booklet of general plans for the class was found in an open archive. What follows is built from three kinds of document: a builder's inboard profile of King George V dated 12 July 1941, read sheet by sheet for its compartment labels and frame numbers [S21]; the catalogue record of an Admiralty as-fitted plan held by the National Maritime Museum [S19]; and the reconstructed arrangement schematics in the forensic analysis of the loss of Prince of Wales, which were drawn from her original plans [S24], [S25].

## Deck names

The Admiralty as-fitted plan of King George V, drawn to 1:192 and showing the ship in July 1944, is titled for the levels it covers: the inboard profile, the bridges, the signal decks, No. 1 and No. 2 platforms, and the upper deck. It carries a Directors of Naval Construction Department stamp dated 25 January 1946 and is sheet 1 of 3 [S19]. The reverse gives the scale in the builder's terms, one-sixteenth inch to one foot [S19].

Below the upper deck the names in use come from the flooding diagrams of the Prince of Wales analysis, which are drawn on four levels: Lower Deck, Upper and Lower Platform Decks, and Hold [S24]. A Middle Deck lies between lower and platform levels; water that came up the port shaft alley trunk on 10 December 1941 poured onto the Middle Deck, and there was substantial flooding on the middle deck in the compartments forward of the after main battery turret [S24]. The builder's profile labels a Shelter Deck across the amidships superstructure [S21].

## Watertight subdivision

The builder's profile marks watertight bulkheads along the base line with the abbreviation W.T.B. against frame numbers; in the region of the forward magazines and shell rooms they fall at frames 94, 96, 99, 101, 104, 108 and 112, and in the machinery spaces at frames in the 137 to 164 range [S21]. Frame numbers run from a low number at the bow, the bow torpedo hole on Prince of Wales being centred at about Frame 8, to Frame 296 at the stern [S25].

How the openings in those bulkheads were governed is Royal Navy general practice, described in the Gunnery Pocket Book [S68]. Nearly every watertight compartment has watertight openings, and each is marked so that the risk it carries is visible at a glance. A red flash across the corner of a door or hatch, or a red disc beside a valve, means that leaving it open is an immediate risk to watertight integrity; a blue one means a less immediate risk; an unmarked opening carries no risk. A letter beside the colour says what must be done before opening it. X means permission must first be obtained from the officer of the watch or damage control headquarters. Y means it may be opened without permission to pass through but must be closed and secured immediately, and keeping it open needs permission and a sentry on it. Z means it is usually open except at action stations or in an emergency, red Z doors being openable or closable by pipe independently of other Z openings. O means always open, closed only by special order. ROUTINE means a special routine, posted nearby, governs it [S68].

Further symbols qualify the letter: 2 CLIPS, meaning two clips only except in an emergency; a yellow disc, meaning the opening is closed at the gas alarm; VENT. 2 CLIPS and VENT. NO CLIPS, for hatches over magazines in a venting condition; ACTION, for doors that may be opened for action purposes under the ship's orders; and FIRST GUN, for a Z door left open when Battle State is assumed until the men are at stations [S68]. A clip is put on from above downwards, so that pressure on the door jams it tighter rather than shaking it loose [S68].

The subdivision was tested on a model as well as on paper. The National Maritime Museum holds an all-metal model of King George V whose hull is marked with deck and compartment details, whose interior is sectioned into sealed compartments, and which has openable apertures on the deck and around the hull for flooding them; the museum has no documentation for it and takes it to have been a buoyancy test model [S72].

## Machinery spaces

The machinery occupies eight compartments in four units, each unit a boiler room, an engine room and an action machinery room [S24]. The builder's profile labels one pair directly: 'A' Boiler Room to starboard and 'B' Boiler Room to port on the upper of the two machinery levels, with 'A' Action Machinery Room starboard and 'B' Action Machinery Room port below them, reserve feed water tanks port and starboard alongside, and oil fuel spaces beneath the whole group [S21].

The four units are lettered 'A', 'B', 'X' and 'Y' [S24]. Behind them, on the port side, lie No. 6 Diesel Dynamo Room immediately abaft 'B' Engine Room, with the thrust block at its forward end, and 'Y' Action Machinery Room abaft that [S24]. Breaker rooms sit above: No. 12 Breaker Room is directly above 'Y' Action Machinery Room, reached by a hatch, and Breaker Room 1 lies forward in the ship [S24]. The power control room, with duplicate gauges and switches for all four engine rooms, is a separate space in 'X' Engine Room [S24]. The after bulkhead of 'B' Engine Room, through which the port outer shaft passes, is at Frame 184; the forward bulkhead of the Diesel Dynamo Room aft of it is at Frame 206; the after bulkhead closing the shaft alley is at Frame 270, and the bulkhead nearest the stern tube at Frame 274 [S24].

Each shaft runs aft from its engine room through a shaft alley or tunnel, passing a watertight gland at every transverse bulkhead, to a stern tube and a strut [S24]. The trunk from the port shaft alley rises to the Middle Deck, with doors on that deck [S24].

## Magazines, shell rooms and handing rooms

The turret ammunition spaces are a stack. At the bottom, at hold level just above the double bottom, is the magazine handing room, square in plan and surrounded by eight magazines that form a rectangular complex around it [S7]. Above that sit the shell handling room and its shell rooms, reached from the shell handling room down through a hatch to the cordite handing room [S7]. Above the shell rooms are the trunk, the working chamber, the gun loading well, the turntable compartment with its silent cabinet, and the gunhouse [S7]. Magazines lie below the shell rooms throughout [S6].

The builder's profile names these spaces individually in the forward group: 'B' Fore Shell Room port, 'B' Outer After Shell Room port, 'A' shell rooms, 'B' Starboard Magazine, 'A' magazines, with a pump room to port among them, and oil fuel below and outboard; 'A' Gun Support and 'B' Gun Support are labelled at the barbette structure above, with a gunners' party store and a lobby alongside [S21]. Cordite and shell spaces are cross-hatched on the drawing [S21]. The per-space stowage counts for those magazines and shell rooms are in facet 03.

The 5.25-inch magazines were concentrated in the ship's hold, in two sections of the hull; the 2019 wreck assessment identifies those sections on the multibeam image of Prince of Wales [S26]. The schematic in the forensic analysis shows the boiler and engine room machinery and the 5.25-inch and 14-inch magazines in relation to one another, and shows the centre layer of the three-layer side protection system filled with fuel oil [S24].

How such spaces were run is again general practice [S66]. Cartridges are kept in magazines, projectiles in bays or sliding shell stowages in shell rooms, and cartridges pass from the magazine to a handing room outside it and thence to the guns; in some ships shell-handing rooms are fitted as well. In big ships the magazines are built as watertight compartments protected by armour, with ventilation and, where they sit in hot positions, cooling; temperatures are read every watch where cooling is fitted and daily everywhere, and go into the magazine log. Magazines, handing rooms and shell rooms are inspected every 24 hours and after work, drill or firing. All doors and hatches into magazines and shell rooms are kept locked, the keys grouped 1, 2 and 3 by importance on a special key board under a sentry, with every issue and return recorded against signature in the magazine log [S66].

## Living spaces along the profile

Reading the builder's inboard profile from forward aft, the labelled living and working spaces include, at the forward end, chief petty officers' sleeping cabins and bathrooms, a telegraphists' office, cabins, offices, lobbies and capstan machinery [S21]. Amidships, below the shelter deck, are marines' mess spaces and, below and forward of them, seamen's mess spaces, with A.A. lockers, a seamen's drying room with a washing machine, and seamen's wash and access dressing rooms [S21]. A canteen with its store and office, and the admiral's steward's space, lie in the forward superstructure block [S21].

## Superstructure

The as-fitted plan's title names the superstructure levels covered on its first sheet: bridges, signal decks, and No. 1 and No. 2 platforms [S19]. No source found lists what each level held; see gaps.md. The builder's profile shows the shelter deck running across the amidships block [S21].

## Hull form

The body plan, drawn at half an inch to one foot and dated 21 April 1938, gives the section lines and the principal dimensions box reproduced in facet 01 [S22].

## Conflicts between sources

None found. The three plan sources cover different levels and do not overlap enough to disagree.
