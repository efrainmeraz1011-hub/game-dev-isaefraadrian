A nucleus of 65 officers and ratings stood by the ship from April 1940, joined by 60 gunnery ratings in June 1940, berthed meanwhile in the "Ozeana" or the "Adiga"; the rest joined on commissioning day [S15, §IV, §II]. Cadets under training from Tirpitz took part in the Baltic trials and left before the sortie [S15, §V(1)]. A Propaganda Ministry party of journalists, cameramen, radio commentators and technicians was embarked; none survived [S15, §II]. Two officers of the fleet staff and Major Grohe as Luftwaffe liaison are listed among the staff [S29].

Losses: about 2,131 dead by a German recount, 115 survivors, 110 rescued by British ships (one of whom died the next day) and 5 by German or Axis-friendly ships [S37]. KBismarck.com counts 116 picked up alive (Dorsetshire 86, Maori 25, U-74 3, Sachsenwald 2) with Maschinengefreiter Lüttich dying in Dorsetshire on 28 May [S22, footnote 5]. Tovey's despatch gives Dorsetshire four officers and 75 ratings and Maori 24 ratings before the rescue was abandoned at 1140 [S24, ¶87]; Naval-History.net gives four officers and 78 ratings and 24 ratings [S33, 27 May]; the interrogation report says 110 men including four officers were hauled aboard the two ships [S15, §VI]. U-74 found three men in a raft at the end of 27 May [S36]. About 800 men left the ship before she sank by the KBismarck.com estimate [S22]. Kapitänleutnant Junack estimated at least 300 killed aboard before the sinking, besides those trapped below [S15, §II].

## Divisions

The crew was divided into twelve divisions [S29; S15, §II]:

| Division | KBismarck.com [S29] (180 to 220 men each) | Interrogation report [S15, §II] (150 to 200 men each) |
|---|---|---|
| I to IV | Main and secondary battery personnel | Seamen's divisions crewing primary and secondary armament |
| V | Anti-aircraft | Close-range AA (leichte Flak) |
| VI | Anti-aircraft | Long-range AA (schwere Flak); V and VI together 360 men |
| VII | Functionaries: carpenters, stewards, cooks, tailors, cobblers | Daymen (Funktionäre): cooks, canteen staff and 28 bandsmen |
| VIII | Artillery mechanical personnel | Gunnery artificers, gunner's mates; also ammunition supply ratings, under Oberleutnant Hinz [S15, §VII(6)] |
| IX | Signalmen and radio-telegraphists | Navigational: helmsmen, telegraphists, signalmen |
| X to XII | Engineers, technicians and stokers | Technical: turbine and boiler room ratings |

Each division was split into two or more watches (Züge), each of which was divided into Korporalschaften of 10 to 12 men under a petty officer [S15, §II]. Prisoners called the ship's company exceptionally young and inexperienced, many on their first ship after a few months of training, drawn from civil trades across Germany, Austria and Czechoslovakia, with friction between petty officers and men [S15, §II]. Lindemann wrote in the war diary on 28 April 1941 that he would use the waiting period for training, give the crew more rest, and return time to division duties and the outer upkeep of the ship, and replenish stores weekly to three months' requirement [S17, p. 96]; the same day he reported the ship fully ready and provisioned for three months [S20].

The officer corps as listed: Kapitän zur See Ernst Lindemann (commander), Fregattenkapitän Hans Oels (executive officer), Korvettenkapitän Adalbert Schneider (I.A.O.), Korvettenkapitän Helmut Albrecht (II.A.O.), Korvettenkapitän Wolf Neuendorf (navigation), Korvettenkapitän (Ing.) Walter Lehmann (chief engineer), Korvettenkapitän Max Rollmann (Rollenoffizier), Korvettenkapitän Kurt-Werner Reichard (B.N.O.), Korvettenkapitän Wilhelm Freytag (electrical), Korvettenkapitän (V) Rudolf Hartkopf (administration), Kapitänleutnant Burkard von Müllenheim-Rechberg (IV.A.O.), Kapitänleutnant Karl Gellert (Flak), Kapitänleutnant Rudolf Troll (I.W.O.) [S29]. Three medical officers served under Oberstabsarzt Dr. Hans Günther Busch [S15, §VII(23)]. Three meteorologists were embarked under Dr. Externbrink [S15, §VII(16)]. Four officers survived: von Müllenheim-Rechberg, Junack, Blazer (meteorological) and Stiegler (midshipman, electrician promoted from the lower deck) [S15, §II].

## Berthing

The crew lived on the battery deck and the armour (main) deck, messing by division; both decks were linoleum covered [S15, §VII(1)]. Officers' quarters were in the superstructure and the deckhouse; the wardroom held a portrait of Bismarck [S15, §VII(1)]. Living spaces forward of turret Anton, which could not take direct air supply in heavy weather, had circulating fans with filters and purifiers; heating was by steam heaters separate from the ventilation, and some living spaces could get cooled air [S15, §VII(13)]. Prisoners stationed below decks appeared to the Admiralty to have suffered from lack of fresh air [S15, §II]. On 21 May 1941 at anchor near Bergen the crew lay about the forecastle in sports gear [S15, §V(1)]. Damage control parties used hammocks to contain the flooding in the port No. 2 boiler room after the Denmark Strait hit [S21]. No source fetched describes the bunk or hammock allocation aboard Bismarck; see gaps.md.

## Messing

Two canteens served the ship's company, forward on the battery deck in Section XV and aft in Section VIII, each manned by six to eight men and each with a part reserved for petty officers; they sold beer, cigarettes, chocolate, biscuits and writing materials [S15, §VII(22)]. Beer sold in half-litre glasses at 30 Pfennig and was stowed in 50-litre casks in special compartments in Sections XVII and VIII, 500 to 1,000 casks, oxygenated before consumption [S15, §VII(22)]. Two galleys (Kombüse) served the ship's company and one the petty officers, in the same sections as the canteens; the officers' galley was in the superstructure [S15, §VII(22)]. The ship carried 500 pig and 300 ox carcases; prisoners calculated the provisions could feed a city of 250,000 for a day [S15, §VII(22)]. The refrigerating plant (electric, CO2) stood in Section XV on the upper platform deck and the cold rooms in Section XVI on the lower platform deck under turret Bruno, with "Aka" thermostatic control [S15, §VII(22)]. The standard displacement carried 1,023 mt of equipment including crew and provisions, 155 mt of drinking water and 510 mt of washing water [S2].

The bill of fare for the last two days, typed by a survivor who had been responsible for typing it [S15, §VII(22)]:

| Day | Breakfast | Lunch | Dinner |
|---|---|---|---|
| 25 May 1941 | Coffee, butter, jam | Potatoes with dumpling pudding | Tea, butter, egg, sausage |
| 26 May 1941 | Coffee, dripping | Soup, potatoes, sauce, meat, lemon | Coffee, butter, cheese, sausage |

The Admiralty noted the food was chiefly bulk (soup and potatoes) while prisoners called it a great improvement on civilian diet [S15, §VII(22)]. When Hitler inspected the ship in May 1941 a meal of pea soup and sausage was served in the wardroom [S15, §IV]. On the evening after Hood was sunk there was an abundant issue of sausage, chocolate and cigarettes; one party of ten men received 800 cigarettes [S15, §V(2)]. A Kriegsmarine ration scale in grams per man per day was not found; see gaps.md.

## Watch bill and daily routine

The war diary logs the shipyard routine at Hamburg in August 1940: a Sunday duty plan with the commander's inspection of the crew (Kommandantenmusterung), Rollendienst (orders-of-the-day duty), loading of machinery stores, and a Seewache (underway watch) kept from 0800 to 1600 during a steam-raising drill [S17, pp. 3 to 6]. Later entries log "Divisionsdienst" as the personnel routine, "Flak-Kriegswache" (Flak war watch) at anchor, and "Flak-Wache. Heimatbeurlaubungen. Ausbildungsdienst." (Flak watch, home leave, training) alongside yard work [S17, entries of October 1940 to January 1941]. War watches were set at 2030 on 20 May 1941 off the Norwegian coast [S15, §V(1); S15, Appendix III]. "Alarm" was the call to battle stations [S30]. Magazine temperatures were logged each watch, with 25 degrees C as the permitted maximum and 21 degrees C the usual [S15, §VII(12)]. The weather watch reported to Germany every three hours from 0300 [S15, §VII(16)]. By the night of 26/27 May the gun crews, on almost continuous watch since Bergen, were falling asleep in the turrets between salvoes; a junior officer described the First Gunnery Officer, the navigating officer and the senior meteorologist lying at their stations "like dead men" before dawn [S15, §V(3)]. At 0630 on 27 May all off-watch officers were summoned to the charthouse shortly before the alarm [S17, 27 May, 0630 entry]. On Tirpitz at Fættenfjord, Flak war watch was set during air-raid warnings and the ship's aircraft flew anti-submarine patrols at varied times daily [S35, 1 to 4 February 1942].

## Medical

The sick bay was forward on the battery deck; two action dressing stations (Verwundetenlagerraum) stood forward on the main deck in Section XVI abreast turret Bruno and aft on the main deck in Section V [S15, §VII(23)]. A direct hit in the after dressing station on 27 May killed the medical staff and the wounded there [S15, §VI]. Five men were lightly wounded in the Denmark Strait [S17, 24 May, 0620 entry; S21]; the first death was a petty officer thrown against a bulkhead by the torpedo hit of 24/25 May, and three airmen broke legs [S15, §V(3)]. Bed capacity of the sick bay was not found; see gaps.md.

## Other services

Gas masks tested with tear gas, most recently in February 1941, and tubes of "Losantin" tablets against blister gas were issued to every man; six anti-gas parties of a petty officer and two stokers had rubber suits, boots, gloves and sou'westers [S15, §VII(11)]. Duffel coats lined with sheep's wool were issued for the sortie; no tropical kit [S15, §V(1)]. The ship's cells were in the capstan motor room in Section XX [S15, §VII(3)]. Loudspeakers on the upper, battery and main decks carried orders, the Admiral's addresses and battle news [S15, §VII(4), §V(1), §V(2)]; W/T office C broadcast music and news in peacetime [S15, §VII(15)]. Twenty-eight bandsmen belonged to Division VII [S15, §II]. Short shore leave was given to men not on watch on 18 May 1941 [S15, §IV]. Laundry, barber, post office and chapel were not found in any source; see gaps.md.

## Tirpitz

Tirpitz's complement was 2,608 (source undated) [S19]. The air attack of 3 April 1944 killed 122 and wounded 316 [S19]; the US Naval Technical Mission put it at 16 bombs with extensive damage to the superstructure [S18, §1]. On 12 November 1944 KBismarck.com counts 971 dead [S19]; the US report says about 1,200 officers and men lost and about 600 saved [S18, §2]. The German report of 19 November 1944 states there was no panic during or after the capsizing and that the commanding officer had told the crew before the raid to stand fast [S18, Appendix A-2].

## Conflicts between sources

- Complement: 2,065 [S29] against 2,100 to 2,200 [S15]; aboard in May 1941 "more than 2,200" [S29; S1] against 2,300 to 2,400 [S15].
- Division strength: 180 to 220 men [S29] against 150 to 200 [S15].
- Survivors: 115 [S37] against 116 picked up alive [S22]; Dorsetshire's rescue 4 officers and 75 ratings [S24] against 4 officers and 78 ratings [S33] against 86 [S22].
- Tirpitz dead on 12 November 1944: 971 [S19] against about 1,200 [S18].
