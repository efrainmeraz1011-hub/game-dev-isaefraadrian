## Radar fits by date

Prince of Wales was fitted at Rosyth between January and March 1941 with Type 281, Type 284 and four Type 285 sets [S2]. Navypedia adds four Type 282 sets alongside four Type 285 in May 1941, Type 271 in July 1941, and seven more single Oerlikons together with the December 1941 outfit [S23]. Type 282 itself went to sea first in her [S12].

Duke of York's outfit, fitted at Rosyth in October 1941, is listed set by set in her chronology [S3]:

Howe completed a radar fit of 14 sets at Rosyth by August 1942 [S5]. King George V received Type 271 in 1941, then in late 1941 lost it and gained Type 273 and five Type 282 sets, and four Type 285 sets in mid-1942 [S23].

The late-war refits replaced the centimetric gunnery and warning sets. Howe's Devonport refit of December 1943 to May 1944 replaced Type 284 with Type 274 [S5]; navypedia dates the same refit to May 1944 and lists Type 274, Type 277, Type 281B, two Type 282, Type 285 and Type 293 fitted while Type 273, Type 281 and Type 284 came out [S23]. King George V in July 1944 lost Type 273 and Type 279 and gained Type 277, Type 279B, two Type 282, Type 285 and Type 293 [S23]. Duke of York in March 1945 gained two Type 274, Type 277, Type 281B, two Type 282 and Type 293 [S23]; her chronology records the Type 284 removed at that refit and a target indication radar added with its aerial at the top of the mast [S3]. Anson's Devonport refit of July 1944 to March 1945 renewed the radar outfit [S4]; navypedia lists the March 1945 result as seven Type 262, two Type 274, four Type 275, Type 277, Type 281B and Type 293, together with a Type 651 electronic countermeasures suite, the heaviest fit in the class [S23].

## Directors, rangefinders and the fire control table

The main armament was controlled from a director control tower, with 'B' turret as the tertiary fire control position and local control available in each turret from an Admiralty Fire Control Box Mark IV in the silent cabinet; the guns could be fired from the director control tower, from 'B' turret, or locally [S7]. Each gunhouse also held a main training position with a telescopic sight, an auxiliary training position with a follow-the-pointer receiver but no telescope, a layer's position, and a local control position with a look-out periscope [S7].

Rangefinder baselengths were 41 feet (12.5 m) in 'A' and the after quadruple turret and 30 feet (9.10 m) for the duplex instrument in 'B' [S6]; the ADM 234/271 essay gives 42 ft for 'A' and notes that the projecting ends were protected by armour-quality castings 3 in thick [S7]. The fore director control tower carried a 15-foot rangefinder [S14].

The main armament computer was an Admiralty Fire Control Table Mark IX, in the 14-inch transmitting station [S14]. Prince of Wales' report on the Bismarck operation records that the table ran continuously for 32 hours and proved most reliable, with only two incidents: after salvo 12 of the first action a heavy hit was felt to starboard, the director setting mechanical pointer was seen oscillating violently and a fuze blew at the panel in the transmitting station, cured by using the director setting control trigger to move the pointer off and letting it settle; and immediately after the third action the safety diaphragm in the air supply to the table's motors blew with a loud report and filled the transmitting station with blue smoke from vaporised oil in the air system, after which a double diaphragm was fitted [S14]. Cross-levelling gear was used throughout both actions with success, and the spotting rules were applied successfully in the transmitting station [S14].

The Gunnery Pocket Book describes what a table of this generation does, for the Royal Navy generally rather than for this class in particular [S67]. It carries a true range counter beside the own-ship dial as well as clock range and gun range counters; two dials showing director elevation sent down from the director control tower by magslip; two scrolls for correcting gun elevation for target height in bombardment; a director and transmitting station changeover switch and a target visible lamp; and, over the enemy dial, two wires, a dummy ship engraved on glass, and a pointer showing the inclination measured by the inclinometer in the director control tower [S67]. Plots stand at either end of the central clock. The range plot takes cuts from each rangefinder in the ship, marking the paper with a typewriter when the cut lamp burns, and the range tuning operator positions the pen in the middle of the marks to tune the table to the best true range; the same plot doubles as a diary of events for analysis [S67]. The spotting plot takes over, short and straddle calls from spotters stationed around the ship through a lamp box and lets the operator order the spotting corrections [S67]. Range matching receivers over the table follow radar ranges sent from the radar ranging panels, and an error-in-range plot on the same paper as the spotting plot compares the table's calculated range with the radar's measured range; a speed across plot at the other end compares the relative speed across the line of sight measured by the director with the value calculated by the table. Both feed suggestions to the rate officer through the two wires over the enemy dial [S67]. The speed across plot also lets the director be kept on an invisible target behind smoke, which the book calls blind fire [S67].

The fire control team is led by the control officer from the centre rear of the director control tower, with a primary spotting officer heading a spotting group that includes a spotter in 'B' turret and a spotter in the after director, and a rate officer heading the rate group [S67].

## What the radar did and did not do at the Denmark Strait

Prince of Wales exercised range and inclination with Hood on 22 and 23 May 1941 and her radio direction finding sets worked well on both days [S14]. In the action on 24 May they did not. The report states that no results were obtained from either the Type 281 or the Type 284, and that Signal School officers afterwards concluded that the Type 281 had suffered interference and the Type 284 was defective, although at the time it had appeared that the 284 was suffering interference as well [S14]. The optical alternative also failed: the rangefinders could not develop a satisfactory range plot before opening fire, the fore director control tower's 15-foot instrument was the only one with a reasonable chance because the closing rate was high, and the 'A' and 'B' turret rangefinders could see the enemy's superstructure only briefly before the table was turned. Two down ladders were needed to find the target [S14]. In the third action, at 0131 on 25 May, a good radar range was obtained on a ship that might have been Bismarck or Prinz Eugen, but the target was lost in funnel smoke and mist before fire could be continued [S14].

The report's recommendation from watching the enemy is a sensor observation: all enemy cruisers and capital ships appeared to carry their large rangefinders considerably higher than British ships did, and the report asks that this be kept in mind in future British designs provided vibration can be eliminated [S14].

## High Angle Control System

The first four ships of the class carried the HACS Mark IV table, which was also fitted to Town and later cruisers and to the new carriers. It was similar to the Mark III but used magslip data transmitters in place of stepper motors; roll compensation was provided from the start and cross-roll compensation added later. The Mark IV* was a modification allowing easier input of radar information, and ships with 5.25-inch guns had special modifications to suit the slower rate of fire of that calibre [S13].

The Mark IV director used magslips to control elevation, training and roll correction, with a coincident rangefinder of 15 feet baselength. Fitting a Type 285 brought the director's weight to about 5.5 tons (5.6 mt), enough to overload the drive units. Adding the gyro rate unit reclassified them Mark IVG, and adding hydraulic cross-level stabilisation Mark IV(GB)HA, the 'B' modification allowing the table to drive the director by remote power control in a blind-fire mode. Most Mark IV directors had been brought to the Mark IVGB standard by the end of the war [S13].

The system worked as follows [S13]. The director layer and trainer held undisturbed telescopes and transmitted director setting and training data to the table; training was hydraulic with handwheel backup, elevation by handwheel supplemented by hydraulic drive from the level corrector, and the height finder could move independently of the layer. The control officer kept a graticule in his binoculars aligned on the target aircraft's fuselage and estimated its speed. The table was given manual estimates of target speed, height and course and generated a position from them, which was matched against the slant range, bearing, elevation and angle of presentation measured by the director crew until observed and calculated agreed. Elevation was corrected for roll imperfectly: a liquid pendulum induced errors of several degrees, and the later gyro-driven roll corrector could still introduce dynamic errors as large as 2 degrees. The table's paper plot carried two prickers, one dotting present range from the director every half second and one dashing the table's range every 1.5 seconds; with Type 285 fitted a third pricker plotted continuous ranges as a series of small holes. The operator had to judge which line, or which combination, represented the real distance and set a wire to continue it, and that drove the fuze calculation, which took in ballistics, time of flight and dead time and sent a fuze number to the gun position for the fuze setter to match. Future position came from the deflection screen, which projected a ring and an arrow pointer, the ring tilted by the future angle of sight into an ellipse whose size was set by target speed divided by average projectile velocity; the screen operator kept a pair of crossed wires on the intersection of pointer and ellipse.

The resulting limitation is stated plainly: the table could really only compute firing solutions for targets holding a steady course, height and speed, which is to say for the least successful kind of attack made during the war [S13]. Adding the Type 285 improved matters, because the radar determined range, speed and course more accurately than the control officer could [S13]. From early 1942 an Auto Barrage Unit was added, coupled to the Type 285M, which could fire a barrage salvo automatically so that the bursts occurred when the target reached a selected range, usually between 1,000 and 5,000 yards (900 to 4,600 m); fire had to be held until the target was inside 5,000 yards, leaving a single chance before the aircraft released its weapon [S13].

Anson alone in the class, and alone among British ships before the end of the war, received the complete HACS Mark VI system, a redesign built around the Type 275 radar that let the radar operator track a new target while the director stayed on the one being engaged, gave a limited blind-fire capability, and had level and cross-level stabilisation with Metadyne power, scooter control and rate aiding. The table itself was unchanged from earlier marks and the tachymetric fittings gave trouble. The Mark VI directors in Anson weighed about 12.5 tons (12.7 mt) [S13].

An IWM photograph shows a HACS Mark IV table aboard Duke of York, with the deflection operator seated in front of the deflection screen and the range plot operator standing directly opposite [S43].

## Type 284 in the Duke of York's night action against Scharnhorst

The North Cape action of 26 December 1943 was fought largely on radar, and the chronology gives the sequence [S3]:

| Time | Event | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1617 | Duke of York's Type 273QR located Scharnhorst at 45,500 yards, bearing 020 degrees | [S3] |
| 1632 | the main armament gunnery set picked up Scharnhorst at 29,700 yards | [S3] |
| 1636 | range closed to 14,000 yards and still closing | [S3] |
| 1717 | at 13,000 yards Duke of York began firing broadsides under radar control, settling into a stern chase | [S3] |
| 1808 | with the range opened to 16,000 yards, shell splash observation by radar became difficult, and Fraser signalled his destroyers to report Duke of York's fall of shot | [S3] |
| 1819 | Bey signalled Group North that "the enemy is firing by radar at a range of more than 18.000 meters" | [S3] |
| 1824 | accuracy dropped because of damage to the Type 284M (3); fire was checked after 52 broadsides, 31 of them reported as straddles | [S3] |
| 1842 | the repair was completed; on restarting, the range to Scharnhorst was 22,000 yards, bearing 070 degrees | [S3] |
| 1901 | fire reopened at 10,400 yards, several shells of the first broadside seen to land on Scharnhorst's quarterdeck | [S3] |
| 1922 | on the fall of the 72nd broadside the radar echo, after appearing as a normal straddle, developed into a bunch 800 yards either side of the target, as a large explosion occurred at Scharnhorst's stern | [S3] |
| 1929 | fire checked after 77 broadsides | [S3] |

What interrupted the shooting was a hit on the radar itself. A shell from Scharnhorst's last salvo passed through Duke of York's foremast and damaged the Type 284M (3) gunnery set; Lieutenant H R K Bates RNVR, in charge of the Type 273 office, climbed the mast and fixed the aerial, after which the set worked again, and he was awarded a DSC. Another shell from the same salvo hit the after mast and damaged the cables to the Type 281 air warning set [S3]. The 18 minutes between the set failing and Bates finishing the repair are the 18 minutes in which Duke of York's fire was checked [S3].

## Communications

Within the turret, the captain of the gun spoke to the working chamber through a voice pipe while working the control levers; an IWM photograph of Duke of York in December 1943 shows exactly that [S38]. 'A' gunhouse alone carried a navigating cabinet with a rudder indicator, a steering order transmitter, a speed indicator, communication telephones, and a changeover switch with four positions for the lower steering position, the after steering position, 'X' engine room, and off [S7].

Between the bridge and the director control tower, Prince of Wales found the voicepipe unusable with a high wind blowing past the director; her staff rigged a direct sound-powered handset and the report recommends it for future construction [S14].

One interference problem is recorded at the fleet level. In King George V, wireless telegraphy transmission on power on certain wavelengths interfered with reception on radio direction finding, and for that reason Tovey could not keep the Admiralty fully informed during the final action against Bismarck; he notes that the limitation applies in some degree to all ships and must be borne in mind in future [S15].

## Radar as the Royal Navy described it to its own people

The 1945 Gunnery Pocket Book sets out the service's own account of what radar could and could not do [S67]. Radio waves travel at about 163,000 sea miles per second and reflect off objects; sets pair a pulse transmitter and a receiver with directional aerials, and display echoes on cathode ray tubes designed variously for range, bearing and angle of sight, a ranging tube also serving to measure rate. Sets differ by function: warning of aircraft and fighter direction, warning of ships, target indication, control of long-range gunfire against aircraft and against surface targets, and control of close-range weapons, with navigation and torpedo control besides. The limitations named are that an echo gives no indication of the nature of the target, that radar can never be quite as accurate in bearing as the human eye, that transmitting may give away own ship's position to an enemy with the right gear, and that sets can be jammed, against which "the uses of Radar are so great as entirely to outweigh its limitations". Operators are to line up and check their sets at every dawn and dusk action stations and at Quarters Clean Guns, check their telephones, and practise taking ranges and bearings at least once a day, because a week in harbour without using the set will usually reveal a defect only on going to sea [S67].

## Conflicts between sources

- The set that picked up Scharnhorst at 29,700 yards at 1632. The chronology prints "Type 274M (3)" at that entry but calls the same set "Type 284M (3)" in every later entry, including the damage and repair [S3]. Type 274 did not become operational until 1944 [S12].
- Number of broadsides fired by Duke of York at North Cape. 77 when fire was checked at 1929 [S3]; 80 broadsides and 446 shells in the NavWeaps account, which states that it follows the tables in "Loss of Scharnhorst" by A. J. Watts because the references conflict [S6].
- Rangefinder baselength in 'A' turret. 41 feet [S6]; 42 ft [S7].
