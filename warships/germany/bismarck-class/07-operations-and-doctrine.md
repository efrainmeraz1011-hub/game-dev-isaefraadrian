# Bismarck class: operations and doctrine

Status: partial (see gaps.md)

## Watch system and readiness states

The war diary records the readiness states the ship used: "Gefechtsgrundschaltung" (basic battle configuration of boilers and turbines), "verschärfter Verschlusszustand" (heightened watertight closure), "Flak-Kriegswache" (Flak war watch), "Seewache" (underway watch) and "Kriegswache Ruhe" (war watch stood down) [S17, entries of September 1940 to January 1941]. At sea on 20 May 1941 the order "Close watertight doors!" followed the sinking of drifting mines at 1345 and war watches were set at 2030; an air raid alarm at 0230 on 21 May sent the crew to action stations until 0830 [S15, §V(1)]. On 27 May the "Klarschiffverschlusszustand" (clear-ship watertight condition) was set on the alarm at about 0630 [S17, 27 May, 0630 entry]. "Alarm" is the German call to battle stations [S30].

## Gunnery procedure from detection to salvo

Detection on 24 May 1941 came in this order: Prinz Eugen's hydrophones reported propeller noises to port at about 0530 with no radar echo [S21]; B-Dienst reported a new unit at 0537 and at 0543 another, later identified as Hood [S17, 24 May, 0537 and 0543 entries]; at 0547 the alarm was sounded on sighting smoke to port [S17, 24 May, 0547 entry; S23, p. 3]. The First Artillery Officer asked the bridge repeatedly for permission to fire while British salvoes were already landing; Lindemann's reported words were "I will not let them shoot my ship from under my ass. Open fire!", and the flag signal "JD" (Jot-Dora) released Prinz Eugen to fire [S23, p. 5; S21, footnote 2]. Fire was opened at 0555 [S17, 24 May, 0555 entry; S20; S21].

Ranging and fire for effect followed the AVKS doctrine: bracketing groups of fore and aft partial salvoes of four rounds, or turret salvoes of two, then partial salvoes A+B / C+D for effect, full salvoes only in special cases [S16, ¶3]. The British saw the Germans alternating forward and aft turret groups a few seconds apart [S23, p. 5]; prisoners described firing salvoes of four with the fore and after turrets alternately [S15, §V(2), §VI]. Bonomi's reconstruction of Bismarck's salvoes on Hood [S23, pp. 5 to 7]:

| Salvo | Range | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 1st, 0555 | 22,000 m | short, ahead of Hood's bow |
| 2nd | 20,000 m | over, between Hood and Prince of Wales |
| 3rd, 0557 | about 18,500 m | a shell hit Hood's fire control tower |
| 4th | 17,000 m | short but with correct ladder |
| 5th | 15,700 m | first four (A+B) in the water; the next four (C+D) straddled Hood at the mainmast, one shell entered the hull; explosion at 0600 |
| 6th | 15,000 m | fired at Hood's presumed position after she blew up |
| 7th | about 15,000 m | ranging on Prince of Wales |
| 8th, 0602 | 14,000 m | hit Prince of Wales's compass platform |
| 9th | under 14,000 m | two hits on Prince of Wales |
| 10th | 15,500 m | fourth and last hit on Prince of Wales, amidships |
| 11th, 12th, 13th | 16,000 to 18,500 m | no hits; cease fire at 0609 |

KBismarck.com gives the opening range as around 20,300 m at 0555, the fifth salvo hitting Hood at under 16,668 m at 0600, and four hits on Prince of Wales [S21]; its timeline says 20,800 m [S20]; prisoners gave 23,000 m [S15, §V(2)]. Norfolk observed the first salvo 100 yards short, the second and third straddling and hitting, and a further straddle at 0600 followed by the explosion [S15, §V(2)]. Von Müllenheim-Rechberg, interrogated in Dorsetshire, said Hood blew up on Bismarck's fifth salvo and "cost Germany forty shells", that hits came with the second and third salvoes (a bright white flame without smoke, taken for petrol) and the fourth, and that Hood's forward turrets fired again after she broke her back [S15, §V(2); S26, pp. 3 to 4]. The war diary logs the detonation at 0601 and 20 seconds, cease fire at 0620 with 93 rounds of heavy artillery expended, and Prinz Eugen's target change after her sixth salvo [S17, 24 May, 0601, 0559 and 0620 entries]. Lütjens reported the running battle as fought between 20,800 and 18,000 metres, Hood destroyed after five minutes [S17, 25 May, 0912 to 0948 entry]. Time of flight for the 38 cm shell at those ranges was 22.3 seconds at 15,000 m and 32.0 seconds at 20,000 m [S9, range table].

In the final action the fore and after turrets fired alternately in salvoes of four at Rodney from 0849; the first four-gun salvo fell 1,000 yards short and the next straddled with one round 20 yards short; fire was controlled from the foretop until a hit wrecked it after about 25 minutes, then from the after station, where von Müllenheim-Rechberg fired two salvoes over and one short at King George V before his post was hit; the turrets then went to local control [S15, §VI; S24, ¶82; S22]. Turret Dora was disabled when a shell burst in its starboard gun, causing a flash back into the turret and magazine without explosion; turret Cäsar fired last, hit between the guns, and at the end would not train [S15, §VI; S22]. Rodney's report of her own firing shows double salvoes of 5 and 4 guns and the failures that stopped three of her guns [S25].

Night action: on 26/27 May Bismarck fired four-gun salvoes at destroyers often invisible to her, by radar according to the British, with fire "more correct for elevation than direction" [S15, §V(3); S24, ¶74]. Star-shell (Leuchtgranaten) fire was controlled through a star shell guide unit whose knob, if turned, put it out of action [S16, ¶54]. The 15 cm battery carried 240 star shells [S4].

## Anti-aircraft doctrine

Barrage fire (Zonenfeuer, "Eisenhagel" or "Splitterregen") with fuse zones 1 (close) and 2 (far) pre-set by the Flak commander according to visibility was used against simultaneous attacks and against dive bombers from the moment of tip-over; controlled fire (Geleitschiessen) against single aircraft; with many attackers the weapons were divided into four 90 degree sectors, each under its Flakleiter; at under 1,000 m the ship relied on 3.7 cm and 2 cm [S15, §VII(6)(B), (C)]. Prisoners said the 15 cm and once even the 38 cm guns fired at aircraft at long range, which blanketed the Flak with their own smoke; the 15 cm fired time-fuzed barrages only at night [S15, §VII(6)(D)]. Against the Victorious attack the barrage used everything including the heavy guns; the starboard No. 1 15 cm turret shot down an aircraft at 150 yards without orders when it crossed the sights (prisoner claims) [S15, §V(3)]; the Admiralty recorded two Swordfish and two Fulmars lost that night and none in the Ark Royal attack [S15, §V(3)]. Ark Royal reported heavy and accurate fire even on aircraft in cloud at 3,000 ft [S15, §VII(6)(B)].

## Damage control organization

The main damage control centre (Leckwehrzentrale) was in Section XIV on the upper platform deck beside the Kommandozentrale, with a reserve centre in the turbine control centre in Section X below the armour deck [S15, §VII(9)]. Six damage control parties (Leckwehrgruppen) of 26 men each, under a chief mechanician, each covered three or more sections and were responsible for pumping and flooding, fire, gas and leaks; each party comprised a leak party (L.A., one petty officer and three ratings), a fire party (B.A., one and three), an anti-gas party (G.A., one and three), a pump group (one and three), messengers (B.U., one and two) for when the telephones failed, a carpenter (Meister), a ventilation petty officer, and six safety posts (Sicherungsposten) at key positions [S15, §VII(9)]. Parties stood on the armour deck with telephone to the centre and could work the suction and flooding valves from there or from the battery deck by shaft gearing [S15, §VII(9)].

Ten fixed electric rotary salvage pumps (Siemens motors, Blohm & Voss pumps) of 600 m³/hr (21,189 cu ft/hr) each [S15, §VII(9)]:

| Pumps | Position | Sections served |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | XX, lower platform deck | XX, XXI, XXII |
| 2 | XVII, lower platform deck, S and P | XVII to XIX; in emergency XX to XXII and XVI to XV |
| 2 | XV, lower platform deck, S and P | XV to XVII |
| 2 | VII, lower platform deck, S and P | VII to IV |
| 2 | IV, lower platform deck, S and P | V, IV, III; in emergency VII and VI |
| 1 | III, middle platform deck | III to the stern |

Sections VIII to XIV were drained by the bilge pumps of the boiler, turbine and auxiliary machinery rooms [S15, §VII(9)]. A chief petty officer "Pumpmeister" in the centre had charge of the pumps; flooding of Flutzellen and Trimmzellen went through the sea connections of the same pumps; portable rotary salvage pumps ran on compressed air at 140 lb/sq in; air pressure was not used to blow compartments [S15, §VII(9)]. Six electric fire pumps (Sachsenwerke motors, Blohm & Voss pumps, 80 amps, 128 lb/sq in at the pump and about 60 lb/sq in at the highest point) fed a fire main to every compartment; normally two ran, all six in action; Minimax hand extinguishers about 1.5 ft long stood in all compartments and back-carried foam extinguishers went with each party [S15, §VII(10)]. Six gas parties of a petty officer and two stokers covered stern to III, IV and V, VI to VIII, IX to XIII, XIV to XVI, XVII to bow [S15, §VII(11)]. Magazines had CO2/brine cooling from plants in Sections XVII and VI, flooding valves worked locally or from the main deck by shaft gearing (the larger magazines in two minutes), roof sprays from the fire main worked from the loading chamber or the main deck, local ventilation valves, and Minimax extinguishers in each loading chamber [S15, §VII(12)]. On Tirpitz the repair parties had standing instructions to open the sea valves to all counterflooding tanks on the side opposite a list once it reached 5 degrees [S18, §3(e)].

## Damage control record, 24 to 27 May 1941

Denmark Strait, 24 May. Three 35.6 cm shells hit [S20; S21]. The bow hit entered Section XXI at battery-deck height to port and left a hole about 1.5 m across under water to starboard, damaging the bulkheads between XX and XXI and XXI and XXII; the three forward sections flooded to about 1 m above the battery deck, the bow sank two or three metres, the salvage pump room in Section XVII flooded, and Oberleutnant (Ing.) Richter took charge forward: the entry hole was plugged, a plate was welded over the exit hole from inside under water by a diver, all portable pumps were brought forward, some after compartments were flooded to restore trim, oil was pumped from Sections XVI to XIX to bunkers aft, and the port bower anchor cable was slipped (prisoner statements) [S15, §V(2)]. The war diary's reconstruction records the damage control unit rigging a hose line to pump the forward oil aft and trying to seal the hull with collision mats [S17, 24 May, status entry], and the group reducing to 24 knots at 1300 to help the sealing work forward [S17, 24 May, 1300 entry]. The midship hit struck below the belt in Section XIV, buckled the side, and put out the turbo-generator room and auxiliary boiler room, scalding five ratings by a burst steam pipe; the section flooded and was evacuated and the lights failed briefly (prisoner statements) [S15, §V(2)]. KBismarck.com adds that the port No. 2 boiler room's leak was held with hammocks, that about 1,000 tons of water entered the forecastle, that the ship was 3 degrees down by the bow with a 9 degree list to port, and that starboard void tanks in Sections II and III were flooded to reduce trim and list [S21]. Lütjens's report at 0801 listed the loss of E-Werk 4, water in the port No. 2 boiler room held, water in the forecastle, and maximum speed 28 knots [S17, 24 May, 0801 entry]. Prisoners estimated 3,000 tons of water in the forward sections [S15, §V(2)].

Night of 24/25 May. One aircraft torpedo hit the starboard side against the belt abreast Section XIV without penetration; one man was killed by the shock and the temporary repairs forward loosened as the ship dodged [S15, §V(3); S20; S37].

26 May. Two, possibly three, aircraft torpedoes hit: one on the port side amidships against Sections VII and VIII without damage, one on the starboard quarter outside an unarmoured trimming compartment below the steering motor compartment, flooding Section II to the main deck and jamming the rudders; divers centred one rudder after considerable effort; an attempt to seal the leak in II with a collision mat failed in the seaway; the ship listed about 5 degrees (prisoner statements and survivors' testimony) [S15, §V(3); S17, 26 May, 2030 entry]. The proposal to blow the jammed rudder off with an explosive charge got the reply "Do what you like; I have finished with it!" from the Captain (one prisoner) [S15, §V(3)]. At 2359 the Fleet reported the ship intact in weapons and machinery but unsteerable by engines [S17, 27 May, 0034 entry]. On the morning of 26 May the crew had been building a dummy second funnel and painting the turret roofs yellow; the paint washed off the 15 cm turrets [S17, 26 May, entries at 0942 and 1155].

27 May. Prisoners described hatches and doors jammed by distortion and wreckage, crews trapped in two magazines that were then flooded when fire above made explosion likely, 200 men trapped in the forward canteen, the forward damage control centre wrecked by a direct hit, fires on the battery deck cutting off the forward half of the ship, lights out forward, men suffocating without gas masks, and hatches too narrow for men in inflated lifejackets [S15, §VI]. The survivors' testimony in the war diary adds fire in the magazine of the starboard No. 3 15 cm turret, about 20 cm of water on deck aft, and turret Dora no longer training [S17, 27 May, 0630 entry]. Scuttling: several prisoners received orders such as "Clear ship for scuttling; apply explosive charges"; a scuttling party fired charges placed earlier at the sea inlets and discharges and in the turbine, boiler and auxiliary machinery rooms with all doors and valves left open, and the ship sank ten minutes later, capsizing to port with propellers still turning [S15, §VI]. KBismarck.com dates the demolition charges in the turbine room to about 1000 [S20]. The 2001 survey concluded that flooding from shell hits, the Swordfish torpedoes, the German counterflooding and the last torpedoes sank her and that scuttling only hastened it by minutes; Cameron's 2002 expedition concluded that scuttling alone was responsible [S28, expedition findings and footnote].

## Shore bombardment

Tirpitz, with Scharnhorst and nine destroyers, bombarded Spitsbergen on 8 September 1943 and destroyed the port facilities [S19]. No fetched source describes the bombardment procedure; see gaps.md.

## Doctrine and orders

Group North's operational orders reached the ship on 25 April 1941: Operationsbefehl Nr. 16 (sortie of Bismarck and Prinz Eugen into the Atlantic), Nr. 17 and 18 for the tankers Weissenburg and Heide, and the signals order for Rheinübung; on 29 April came Group West's operative directive for the Fleet Command, the Fleet Commander's operational order, signals arrangements, and the passage order for the Great Belt, Kattegat and Skagerrak [S20]. Lütjens's address at noon on 19 May named the objective as commerce raiding (Handelskrieg) in the Atlantic [S15, §V(1)]. Command passed from Group North to Group West at 1200 on 24 May [S17, 24 May, 1200 entry]. Raeder told Hitler on 6 June 1941 that Lütjens's task was to fight only when the enemy prevented him from attacking merchant shipping, which is why he did not pursue Prince of Wales [S22]. Group West's staff recorded on 26 May that Bismarck's signals were shaped by the short-signal manual's limited vocabulary [S17, 26 May, 2007 entry]. Recognition: a blue pennant was to be hoisted in the U-boat areas BE and BF [S17, 26 May, 0211 entry]; prisoners mentioned the blue flag [S15, §V(3)].

The 1941 German staff considered the loss of secrecy from the start: the Swedish cruiser Gotland sighted the group on 20 May and Bismarck reported that the formation could be assumed compromised [S20]. Tovey's despatch records that Bismarck was located by Coastal Command at 1030 on 26 May about 35 miles from her reported position, that the Ark Royal striking force first attacked Sheffield by mistake with duplex pistols that failed, and that the second strike with contact pistols scored at least two hits [S24, ¶61, ¶66 to ¶69].

## Underway refueling

Fuelling at sea was exercised repeatedly in the Baltic in 1941, including with Prinz Eugen on 13 May [S15, §IV; S20]; the procedure with hose, hawser, pumps and rates is in 02-propulsion-and-power.md [S15, §VII(21)]. The planned refuelling south of Greenland from a tanker sailed from Brest did not happen because of early contact with British forces [S15, §V(1)]; Group West listed the tankers Belchen, Lothringen, Gonzenheim, Kota Penang, Spichern, Esso Hamburg and Brehme by quadrant on 24 May and sent Ermland from La Pallice at 0500 on 27 May [S17, 24 May, 1238 entry; 27 May, 0202 entry].

## Conflicts between sources

- Opening range in the Denmark Strait: 20,300 m [S21], 20,800 m [S20; S17], 22,000 m [S23], 23,000 m [S15].
- Which salvoes hit Hood: 2nd, 3rd and 4th (Norfolk and von Müllenheim-Rechberg) [S15]; 3rd and 5th [S23]; 5th [S21; S26].
- Time of sinking: 1037 [S15, §VI], 1039 [S20; S22; S28], 1101 [S33].
- Cause of sinking: scuttling charges hastened an inevitable end [S15, §VI]; flooding from torpedoes and gunfire with scuttling hastening it by minutes [S28]; scuttling alone (Cameron) [S28, footnote].
- Steering after the torpedo hit: one rudder centred by divers [S15] against both rudders jammed at 12 degrees to port [S20] against a hand rudder coupled in with only makeshift effect [S17].
