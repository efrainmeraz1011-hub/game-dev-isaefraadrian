# King George V class: crew and daily life

Status: partial (see gaps.md)

Much of what follows is Royal Navy general practice, taken from the Admiralty's own 1945 Gunnery Pocket Book, rather than anything specific to these five ships. Where that is so it is said. The ship-specific evidence is thinner and comes mostly from official photographs taken aboard the class in 1940 to 1943, each of which is cited to its Wikimedia Commons file page carrying the Imperial War Museums caption.

## Messing

The general arrangement profile of King George V labels the living spaces across the ship's length: seamen's mess spaces and marines' mess spaces on the decks below the shelter deck, seamen's wash and dressing rooms, a seamen's drying room with a washing machine, a canteen, the admiral's steward's space, lobbies, cabins and offices forward, and, at the forward end, chief petty officers' sleeping cabins and bathrooms [S21]. Marines and seamen messed separately, in spaces so labelled on the drawing [S21].

That the mess drew its own bread from a ship's bakery is shown by an IWM photograph of November 1942 captioned "a naval rating drawing bread for his mess from the battleship's bakery"; the museum's own caption is tentative about the ship, adding "HMS KING GEORGE V?" [S35]. The phrase "for his mess" is the only direct evidence found that food was drawn by mess rather than served centrally in these ships; no source found states which messing system the class used, and the broadside mess against general messing question is recorded in gaps.md.

Food at action stations is Admiralty general practice and is described in the Gunnery Pocket Book [S68]. When a ship is closed up for long periods it is not always possible to let men leave their action stations at all, so sanitary arrangements and mess traps must be available at every quarters. When possible certain cook ratings are fallen out to prepare food, and action cooks detailed from each quarter fetch it from the galley to their own gun or station, where the meal is eaten closed up. Where part of the armament can be fallen out in turn, certain mess decks are used as action messes by each quarter in turn [S68].

Where the mess decks sat relative to the armour mattered. When the torpedo struck Prince of Wales at about Frame 109 on 10 December 1941, part of the gas jet vented through the side protection system into the seamen's mess deck above the impact area, and washrooms on the lower deck and living spaces on the middle deck were flooded with a mixture of salt water and oil [S24].

## The rum issue

An IWM photograph titled "Rum Ration Aboard HMS King George V" was taken in 1940 [S31].

The issue itself was Royal Navy practice, not a ship's arrangement. A naval museum account, drawing on the 1939 BR 93 Manual of Victualling, gives the tot as half a gill, one eighth of a pint, approximately 71 ml. Senior rates, meaning warrant officers, chief petty officers and petty officers, drew it neat. Junior rates drew it as grog, one part rum to two parts water. The 1939 manual describes the spirit as a blend of West Indian rum, mainly Demerara and Trinidad, with proportions of Natal and Mauritius rums [S71].

Two occasions in these ships are recorded. In March 1943, on leaving King George V after a four-day visit to the Home Fleet at Scapa Flow, the King ordered "Splice the Mainbrace", giving every rating an extra tot of rum [S1]. In November 1943, Admiral Sir Bruce Fraser was photographed aboard Duke of York pouring the rum into the Christmas pudding mix, using a raft paddle to stir a very large pudding bowl, watched by several officers including Mrs V. Laughton-Matthews, Director of the WRNS [S37].

## Sleeping

An IWM photograph shows an able seaman asleep in his hammock aboard Anson [S32]. Another, from the same Anson series in October 1942, catches an able seaman "showing a leg" and yawning to begin the day, the phrase itself the traditional call to turn out of a hammock [S53].

No source found states whether bunks were fitted anywhere in the class, or how berthing was distributed by deck; see gaps.md.

## Sick bay

Two photographs taken aboard King George V in 1940 show the medical spaces: two doctors looking at a chart in the sick bay, and the operating theatre attached to it [S33], [S34]. No source found gives the sick bay's bed capacity; see gaps.md.

## Sunday and the ship's chapel

A series of photographs taken aboard Anson at Scapa Flow in October 1942, published as "A Quiet Sunday on HMS Anson", records the harbour routine. Ratings attend divine service in the ship's chapel at 7.30 am [S36]. After divisions, the hands take a deck promenade on the fo'c'sle [S53] and light up [S54].

## Laundry and canteen

The builder's profile marks a seamen's drying room with a washing machine, seamen's wash and dressing rooms, and a canteen with an adjoining canteen store and office [S21]. No source found describes a barber's shop, a ship's post office or a ship's store as such; see gaps.md.

## Living conditions in the tropics

The class was not comfortable in warm climates and the Commander-in-Chief said so before Prince of Wales sailed east. Admiral Tovey protested against her assignment to tropical regions on the ground that recent Mediterranean operations had made clear that "ventilation in the King George V class is most inadequate in a hot climate and health and efficiency of the ship's company will be seriously affected, which will be aggravated by lack of awnings and side screens in harbour" [S24]. The forensic analysis of her loss records that officers and men alike were overcome by heat exhaustion during the action of 10 December 1941, and that the same shortcomings had shown on the passage from Capetown to Singapore [S24]. On the day, most of the electrically driven ventilation fans stopped when the generators were lost and men had to work in relays to keep vital machinery running [S24].

## Dress and precautions at action stations

Royal Navy general practice again. When battle is imminent men should wear clean underclothing to reduce infection of wounds, and should be fully protected against flash by anti-flash clothing and overall suits; the Gunnery Pocket Book singles out the tropics, "where the tendency is to strip to the waist", as the place this matters most. First aid posts are distributed about the ship and each quarters has some first aid facilities [S68]. Photographs of the class show the practice followed: the turret crews of Duke of York in December 1943 are all in anti-flash clothing [S39], and the hands of Anson closing up in October 1942 are in anti-flash gear and steel helmets [S47].

## Watch organisation

The degrees of readiness in the Gunnery Pocket Book set the rhythm of a day at sea, and they are Royal Navy general practice [S68]. Four degrees are allowed for in both the low-angle and the high-angle armaments, and a ship can be in different degrees for each. The third degree of low-angle readiness rests on a two-watch organisation and is sometimes called Defence Stations; it is the usual state of the armament at night. The third degree of anti-aircraft readiness is the two-watch state for a long-running air threat and is sometimes called A.A. Defence Stations. The fourth degree of anti-aircraft readiness rests on a four-watch organisation [S68]. The first and second degrees are normally required only when the fleet is at sea, and if an operation is prolonged men will not get enough sleep closed up all the time, so guard ships may be detailed in turn so that the rest of the force can drop to a lower degree while the guard ship stays at the first [S68].

The alarm itself is a series of hoots on warning alarm hooters fitted throughout the ship, worked from the bridge, followed by the bugle call for Action Stations, Night Action Stations or Repel Aircraft; on hearing them every man goes to his action station whatever he is doing, and a man already manning a gun other than his own does not leave it until relieved. The hooters are never to be used for exercising action [S68]. Royal Marine buglers made the calls; one is photographed aboard Duke of York sounding the "Fall In" for a practice landing party, with two 5.25-inch turrets behind him [S48].

No ship's watch bill or daily routine for any ship of the class was found; see gaps.md.
