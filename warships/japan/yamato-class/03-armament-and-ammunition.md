# Yamato class: armament and ammunition

Status: covered

The deepest primary source for this facet is US Naval Technical Mission to Japan report O-45(N), "Japanese 18 inch Guns and Mounts", written at Kure over five weeks in late 1945. Its authors state plainly that all Japanese documents and drawings for the mount were destroyed by bombing or deliberately burned, and that the report was reconstructed from sketches made from memory by the Japanese engineers and officers who built and fought the mount, among them T. Otani and R. Sugiyama of Kure Arsenal, Captains Iwashima, Date and Makino, and Constructor Captain S. Makino, who supplied the magazine and shell room layout and the ring bulkhead [S11, pp. 5, 7]. Every figure from that report carries that qualification.

## Main battery, 46 cm/45 Type 94

The gun was officially designated the 45 calibre Type 94 40 cm gun to hide the real calibre, which was 46 cm (18.1 inches) and was a guarded secret until the end of the war [S5]. The Technical Mission was told the type number came from the year the design was commenced and attached to the breech mechanism, and that the gun was referred to as the 40 cm/45 Type 94 for secrecy [S11, p. 9]. Engineer C. Hada, who died in 1943, designed it [S11, p. 9].

A 48 cm/45 gun had been tried after the First World War; the project was reopened in 1934 and the 46 cm calibre selected [S11, p. 9]. During design discussions in 1934 to 1935 a 50 calibre gun was considered. It would have given 40 mps (131 fps) more muzzle velocity and 2,600 m (2,850 yards) more range on the same projectile and charge, and cut 1.9 seconds off the time of flight to 20,000 m (21,870 yards). The extra weight was judged not worth the extra power [S5].

Twenty-seven guns were produced, the first completed in March 1938 and tested at the Kamegakubi proving ground about 15 miles south-west of Kure [S5], [S11, p. 5]. Eighteen were lost with Yamato and Musashi, two test guns at Kamegakubi were demolished in November 1945 under the general disarmament orders, and the remaining seven were found in various states of completion on a beach in a cove north of Kamegakubi. Five of those were destroyed; No. 23 and No. 27 went to Dahlgren Proving Ground in Virginia, arriving 6 and 7 June 1946, with two slides following on 7 May 1948 [S5], [S11, p. 9].

### Gun construction

The A tube, designated 2A, had the 3A tube shrunk on for somewhat over half its length from the breech end. That assembly was wire-wound, then had two tubes shrunk on over the full length, then a two-part jacket at the breech. Tube locating shoulders carried Belleville spring washers. The inner A tube, 1A, was radially expanded into place by hydraulic pressure applied in three separate operations, and was rifled after it was in place. There was a short breech ring and a breech bush screwed into the 3A tube. The breech mechanism was a Japanese version of the Asbury type with a Welin block [S5].

The Technical Mission's account matches and adds detail: the 2A tube ran the full length, the 3A was shrunk on about three fifths of the way from the breech, wire winding ran from about half way back to the muzzle, the 4A covered about two thirds of the length from the breech and the 5A the breech and the after part of the powder chamber. The Belleville springs were silicon steel. The 1A tube was radially expanded at 110,000 to 120,000 psi in three parts [S11, p. 16].

Because the liner could be removed only by machining, the gun was discarded rather than relined [S11, p. 16]. NavWeaps notes the same and records that neither battleship appears to have been regunned during the war [S5].

### Propellant charge

The charge was 794 lbs (360 kg) of 110 DC1 (navweaps) [S5]; the Technical Mission recorded the service charge as 330 kg (728 lbs) [S11, p. 16]. Both give six sections. 110 DC1 grain was 11.0 mm in diameter and 370 mm long (0.433 x 14.6 inches) [S5]. Bags were wool until 1942 and silk after, with a separate 1.1 lb (0.5 kg) silk bag of gunpowder igniter at one end [S5]; the mission gives a 500 g black powder igniter in a red-dyed silk bag loaded toward the breech [S11, p. 16].

Reduced and weak charges were carried for training and target rounds. Reduced charges were about three quarters the weight of a full charge and gave about two thirds of service velocity, rated 0.5 equivalent service rounds; weak charges were about half weight, rated 0.06 ESR; a strong proof charge about 20 per cent larger than full was rated 2.0 ESR [S5]. The mission gives the reduced charge as about two thirds velocity at 1/4 ESR and the weak charge at 1/2 ESR [S11, p. 16].

The Technical Mission's figures are coarser: armour-piercing and target projectiles 1,460 kg (3,220 lbs) at 780 m/s, incendiary and common 1,360 kg (3,000 lbs) at 805 m/s [S11, p. 16].

Type 91 projectiles were 6/infinity crh with a boat tail; the bourrelet was 18.07 in (45.898 cm) across. The original Type 91 was L/4.25 or 76.9 in (195.35 cm) long, the later Type 1 L/4.47 or 81 in (206 cm), the difference being the windscreen, whose conic angle went from 23.5 degrees to 21 degrees. The Type 1 added a dye bag, which slightly increased weight, and replaced the two driving bands of the Type 91 with a single double-width band with a copper ring in the middle. The Type 1 superseded the original in 1941, so these ships may never have carried any but the newer shell [S5].

Japanese TNA bursters were shock sensitive, so Type 91 and Type 1 armour-piercing projectiles had about one third to one half of the cavity filled with a shock-absorbing liner. That reduced prematures at the cost of a thinner shell wall, and post-war US Navy tests at Dahlgren showed that hits at high obliquity would break the projectile up without penetrating [S5].

#### The Type 91 as USNTMJ report O-19 describes it

Report O-19, "Japanese Projectiles, General Types", of 6 February 1946, is the tier 1 account of the projectile itself [S21].

The Type 91 was originally designed in 1931 and superseded the earlier Type 88, which had itself replaced the Japanese copy of Hadfield's armour-piercing projectile, the Mk 5 [S21, pp. 30, 59]. It was produced in calibres from 15.5 cm (6.1 in) to 46 cm (18.1 in); the major calibre projectiles had true armour-piercing caps and the medium calibres only a small cap-head [S21, p. 1]. The report is explicit about the designation: "The 40cm, Type 94, was so designated for security purposes. It was actually a 46cm (18.1") projectile. The Type 94 refers to the gun. The projectile was nevertheless a Type 91 design" [S21, p. 30].

The whole design was built around striking below the waterline. The Japanese concentrated on the possibility of hitting the enemy ship below the waterline and therefore possibly below the armour belt, and the projectile was designed to continue an undisturbed trajectory under water by weakening the forward section so that it separated on water impact and left a flat end. The fuze delay was adjusted to give satisfactory functioning after being initiated on striking the water and then passing through the water and the ship's side, 0.4 seconds [S21, pp. 1, 30]. The mission adds a doubt of its own in parentheses: "It is debatable whether success in this attempt will give an increased number of hits below the water level at long ranges" [S21, p. 1].

In the major calibres the cap was divided transversely into a small forward cap-head and the cap proper. The after face of the cap-head and the forward face of the cap were flat and assembled flush, secured to each other only by the windshield threading, which screwed onto both parts. The windshield had an internal circumferential groove at the cap and cap-head interface, weakening the connection so that the cap-head and windshield broke off on water impact. The cap was soldered and crimped to the body. All Type 91 projectiles were boat-tailed and had a base plug with an adapter [S21, p. 30].

Figure 16 of the report keys the projectile as: windshield, cap-head, cap, body, aluminium anti-inertia block, cork lining, wool wrap around the filler, two copper rotating bands, base plug, fuze adapter, copper gas-check rings and copper caulking, Type 13 fuze, and trinitroanisol filler, with the windshield apex angle marked 23 degrees 30 minutes [S21, p. 31]. The aluminium anti-inertia block at the forward end of the cavity, the cork lining, the lacquered cavity and the wool wrapping of the filler were all safety features, the aluminium and cork principally to prevent premature deflagration of the filler on impact [S21, p. 32].

The 18-inch projectile used fuze Type 13 Mk 5 with a 0.4 second delay; the 16, 14 and 8-inch used the same fuze with a wooden cap to fill the space left by the shorter Mk 5 in fillers preformed for the Mk 4, and the 6-inch used the Type 13 Mk 3 with a 0.08 second delay [S21, p. 32].

General figures for the Type 91 family: a weight ratio 2M/D3 of about 1.08, a length of approximately 4.3 calibres, and in the major calibres an analysis of roughly 0.50 per cent carbon, 3.8 per cent nickel, 0.7 per cent chromium and 0.5 per cent molybdenum, with maximum nose and cap hardness exceeding 80 shore [S21, pp. 1 to 2].

Painting and marking: all armour-piercing projectiles were to be white overall with an orange band at the centre of gravity. A loaded round had the end of the windshield painted green; a base-fuzed round had a red tip forward of the green; a dye-loaded round had a band of the same colour as the dye just aft of the green. The whole projectile including the bourrelet and base was painted, the two rotating bands excepted, and the characters for "9" and "1" were painted on the windshield [S21, p. 32]. O-19 names no dye colours.

Ballistic proof required a complete penetration with the cavity effective, tested against a corrected DeMarre formula, which the mission calls similar to US Navy specification [S21, p. 2]. The nominal test condition for the 18-inch projectile was a 410 mm (16.12 in) VH plate at 30 degrees obliquity, striking velocity 525 m/s (1,723 f/s), with Vd 337.8 m/s (1,109 f/s) and F.M.s 1.554 [S21, p. 52].

The 18-inch projectile was the only calibre that gave the Japanese acceptance trouble. Under the specification first set up, at 37 degrees obliquity and an F.M.s of about 1.38, it failed more often than it passed, either being rejected because the nose was chewed or broken, or rupturing into the cavity through the after edge of the rear band score, probably from severe base slap on the hard face. The first problem was remedied by raising the F.M.s to 1.554 and the second by reducing the obliquity from 37 to 33 and later to 30 degrees [S21, pp. 52, 55].

An experimental 48 cm (19-inch) Type 91 projectile was found in a magazine at Kamegakubi: approximately 81 in long, 19 in in diameter, estimated weight 3,800 lb, made in 1941, with the same design characteristics as the other major calibre Type 91 projectiles except for a single large rotating band instead of two. No test results for it were found [S21, p. 55].

NavWeaps records three competing weights for the armour-piercing shell: 3,215 lbs (1,458.3 kg) in US documents written shortly after the war, 3,219 lbs (1,460 kg) in more recent secondary sources, and 1,500 to 1,520 kg (3,307 to 3,351 lbs) claimed from original documents by the Japanese historian Yoshifuru Otsuka. NPG Report No. 289 gives 3,215 lbs (1,458 kg) complete, with burster and liner, and 3,142 lbs (1,425 kg) empty, and NavWeaps concludes Otsuka is mistaken [S5]. Burster weight is likewise disputed: NPG Report No. 289 gives 52.6 lbs (23.9 kg), while "The Yamato Class and Subsequent Planning" gives 33.85 kg (74.6 lbs), which NavWeaps identifies as explosive plus liner; NPG 289 gives liner plus explosive as 73.0 lbs (33.1 kg) [S5].

Common Type 0 HE was issued with time fuzes for anti-aircraft use. USNTMJ report O-19 gives it an effective radius of 74.4 yards (81 m), which NavWeaps calls optimistic [S5].

Yamato was issued yellow splash dye for her Type 1 shells, Musashi green [S5].

### Common Type 3, the sankaidan incendiary shrapnel round

Officially 3 Shiki tsujodan. The round weighed 2,998 lbs (1,360 kg) and was filled with 900 incendiary tubes. A time fuze set the bursting distance, usually about 1,000 m (1,100 yards) from the muzzle. The projectile was designed to expel the tubes in a 20 degree cone toward the oncoming aircraft, with the shell body itself destroyed by a bursting charge to add steel splinters. The tubes ignited about half a second later and burned for five seconds at 3,000 degrees C, producing a flame about 5 m (16 feet) long [S5].

Fuzes were set in the shell handling room, with fuze protectors used to prevent damage before loading. The round may have made up as much as 40 per cent of the outfit by 1944 [S5].

#### The Type 3 as USNTMJ report O-19 describes it

O-19 files the round under incendiary projectiles and notes that the Japanese referred to it indiscriminately as incendiary, as shrapnel, or as Common Projectile Model 3. The shells were filled with steel tubes containing an incendiary mixture with a piece of quickmatch in the centre. Burst was initiated by a time mechanical fuze; the tubes ignited about half a second later and burned for five seconds at 3,000 degrees C, giving a flame about 17 feet long [S21, p. 8].

Table II also gives the shell body composition, shared with the Type 0 common: carbon 0.4 to 0.5, silicon 0.35, manganese 0.3 to 0.7, phosphorus 0.035, sulphur 0.035, nickel 1.5 to 2.0, chromium 0.2 to 0.6; yield point 55 kg/mm2; tensile strength 80 to 100 kg/mm2; elongation 10 per cent; Izod impact 10 ft-lbs; muzzle velocity 2,640 ft/sec [S21, p. 13].

The mission recorded the Japanese view and its own doubt in the same passage. Gunnery officers at sea generally considered these projectiles more effective than common projectiles against approaching aircraft, because the tubes and fragments continued in an effective cone after the burst. The mission thought the officers had been misled by the impressive appearance of the bursts into overestimating the efficiency of the type, and noted that effective use would need the difference in ballistic coefficient between it and common projectiles to be allowed for, and the burst placed above the line of sight, since shrapnel drops towards the earth [S21, p. 12]. Its summary judgement: "The Japanese Navy placed great faith in the effectiveness of incendiary shrapnel as an anti-aircraft weapon. It is considered probable that they rather overestimated its value as compared to HE" [S21, p. 1].

Yamato fired Type 3 from her main battery at aircraft on 24 October 1944 at 1026 in the Sibuyan Sea [S1], at B-24s on 26 October 1944 alongside Nagato [S1], at the shadowing PBM Mariners at 1017 on 7 April 1945, and at the first inbound strike at 1234 that day from her two forward turrets [S24]. By the second wave she was firing them set to detonate one second from the ship, about 3,000 yards, which US aircraft simply flew through [S24]. At the Battle off Samar on 25 October 1944 only two of the six rifles in her two forward turrets were initially loaded with armour-piercing shell, the rest with Type 3 [S1]. One Type 3 reportedly exploded in the barrel aboard Musashi, disabling the gun [S5].

## Main battery turret

Three three-gun turrets per ship [S5]. The Technical Mission was told the Japanese built six complete mounts, one trial mount and one partial turntable for Shinano [S11, p. 9].

### Laying and training

Training used two entirely independent sets of gear, only one in use at a time, each driven by a 500 hp vertical swashplate engine through straight-toothed pinion gears with no worm and worm wheel [S11, p. 18]. Elevation used a normal piston and cylinder, but the connection of the piston rod to the slide was movable longitudinally along the gun, a design the mission singled out as novel [S11, p. 18].

The movable crosshead acted as the slide locking gear. With the crosshead locked in the "Out" position the elevating handwheel was put hard over to "Depress" and the gun was stopped automatically at 3 degrees by the normal cut-off gear as the piston reached the limit of its travel. The slide was not locked during loading; the handwheel was simply kept at "Depress", holding the gun at 3 degrees. A tell-tale operated by the rammer operator showed the pointer when loading was finished and the rammers were withdrawn. A hand-operated slide locking bolt held the guns at 1 degree when secured for sea [S11, p. 50]. NavWeaps describes the same sliding cross-head as selectable between -5/+45 and +3/+41, the latter needing no locking pins [S5].

Guns were individually sleeved [S5].

Recoil used two recoil cylinders charged with glycerine and water and two pneumatic run-out cylinders per gun, with an additional cylinder controlling run-out speed [S11, p. 19].

### Hydraulic power

Report O-45(N) states that three turbo hydraulic pumps, one of them a standby, supplied pressure at 1,000 to 1,100 psi through a ring main to the centre pivots below the lower cordite handling rooms, with water as the pressure medium, and that no local pump was fitted in the turrets for auxiliary purposes [S11, p. 17]. It refers the reader to report O-53(N), "Hydraulic Pumps in Japanese Naval Ordnance", for the pumps themselves [S11, p. 17].

O-53(N) describes those pumps directly. They were the only turbo-hydraulic pumps used in Japanese naval ordnance, fitted for the 18-inch turrets in Yamato and Musashi, with one trial pump in the battleship Hiei. Pumps and driving turbines were designed by Brown Boveri in Switzerland; the first set was built there and sent to Japan, and the remainder were built at the Hiro Navy Yard from the same designs [S22, pp. 1, 7].

### Turret sights and rangefinder

Each turret carried a 15 metre (49.2 ft) rangefinder with elevation of plus or minus 10 degrees and free training of 160 mils right and 130 mils left [S11, p. 17]. Local sights were 10 cm telescopes, one per gun, with free training the same as the rangefinder [S11, p. 18].

### Ventilation

Supply by three 2.5 hp fans; exhaust by five 5 hp fans [S11, p. 18].

### Turret build

Figure 12 of the report shows a sectional elevation of the mount, which was built and transported in five distinct sections [S11, p. 18]:

1. Centre pivot and cable "lead in" gear.
2. Lower and upper cordite handling rooms.
3. Lower and upper shell handling rooms.
4. Turntable, complete with gunhouse deck and trunnion brackets.

The ring bulkhead was secured to the ship at lower shell handling room level on the lower deck, with a free cylinder above [S11, p. 19].

### Centre pivot and services

Hydraulic pressure entered through a centre fixed stalk secured to the triple bottom, with two pressure and two exhaust ports, and ran to distributing panels in the lower shell handling room. The stalk was roughly 4 feet high and 30 inches in diameter. High pressure blast air and electric cables came up a fixed inner tube through the centre pivot into the lower cordite handling room and up to just under the upper cordite handling room deck; the cables passed through four holes above the lower cordite handling room deck into the revolving structure, with sleeves limiting the twist [S11, p. 19].

## Ammunition supply, magazine to breech

This is the sequence O-45(N) reconstructs, with the report's own paragraph structure.

### Stowage

Design requirement was 100 rounds per gun. Of those, 60 per gun (180 per turret) were stowed in the revolving structure so that a surface action could be fought without transferring from the shell rooms; 40 per gun were in the fixed shell rooms, along with practice shells. Within the revolving structure, 120 rounds sat in the lower shell handling room, the outer shell hoists and the outer gunhouse waiting positions, and 60 in the upper shell handling room, the centre hoist and its waiting positions [S11, pp. 18, 36]. NavWeaps gives the same split: 300 rounds per mounting, 180 in the two handling rooms on the rotating structure and 120 in the shell rooms, with the practice of feeding from the lower handling room until it was exhausted and then from the upper [S5].

Shells were stowed vertically throughout, on twin skids between heavy girders, occupying the whole deck space of the shell rooms and shell handling rooms [S11, p. 31].

Magazines were fitted around both the upper and lower cordite handling rooms. The lower magazine supplied the centre gun only; the upper magazine supplied the two outer guns [S11, pp. 18, 19]. Shell rooms and shell handling rooms sat above the magazines [S11, p. 18].

### Cordite, magazine to gun

1. Cordite was stowed horizontally in one-sixth charges weighing 121 to 132 lbs in racks in the magazine, removed from their canisters [S11, p. 22].
2. Charges were manhandled onto a cordite roller chute, loaded end to end. On the trial mount the rack-to-chute distance of 60 cm was too small and was increased to 80 cm to one metre [S11, p. 22].
3. Charges were pushed by hand over rollers into a revolving flashtight scuttle long enough to hold all six one-sixth charges. The scuttle was power revolved by a hydraulic cylinder and piston with a rack. A mechanical interlock stopped it revolving until the cordite transfer bogie was in line and locked to the ship [S11, p. 22].
4. The transfer bogie was originally hand worked. That proved impracticable at a 10 degree list and was never fitted; Yamato and Musashi had a powered bogie with a hydraulic motor driving against a rack around the base of the revolving structure, with a control rack and a fixed rack for hand traversing. Trip levers declutched the drive on collision. The upper handling room had two bogies and five racks [S11, p. 27].
5. At the loading position the bogie declutched from the driving racks and clutched to the revolving structure. A pivoting tray brought the charges into line with the flash door of the cordite hoist for ramming into the gun loading cage [S11, p. 27].
6. Anti-flash doors at the hoist entrance were worked by levers. A cage-down indicator and an interlock chain locked the gunhouse cordite hoist control lever in DOWN, opened the flash door and freed the rammer control [S11, p. 27].
7. The cordite rammer was a piston, rack and pinion type in a casing above the rammer; the rammer head swung down into line. After ramming, the flash door closed, freeing the hoist interlock in the gunhouse [S11, p. 29].
8. The cordite hoist was a flashtight trunk 3 ft 1 in by 9 ft 5 in internally, running straight from the cordite handling rooms to the gunhouse, about 55 feet in "A" turret, with a single cordite cage and an anti-flash door just above the turntable floor operated automatically by the cage [S11, p. 29].
9. Cage lifting gear sat at the front of each hoist in the lower shell handling room: a hydraulic cylinder and piston with a rack driving pinions to a wire winch drum, at a velocity ratio of 1 to 10 between piston and cage. An older wire and sheave press design had been discarded [S11, p. 29].

The lower cordite handling room had one bogie and one hoist, for the centre gun; the upper had two bogies and two hoists, for the outer guns [S11, p. 29].

A blast vent trunk stood in front of the upper handling room with light steel doors opening upwards, intended to vent an explosion from the lower handling room into the lower shell handling room through holes in the deck. The mission noted that those vent holes were directly below stowed shells, and that the Japanese were adamant about the arrangement, although Captain Date said the trunks led to the space between the fixed and revolving structures [S11, p. 29].

### Shell, magazine to gun

1. Shells were moved about the shell rooms and shell handling rooms by a push-pull gear of the Japanese design the mission called the most interesting feature of the mount. Shells were held and moved between two gates, an upper fixed gate that rotated 90 degrees and a lower moving gate. Junctions allowed a change of direction [S11, p. 31].
2. The push-pull sequence ran: moving gate lowered, fixed gate raised, moving gate moved one shell space carrying all shells in the gate (six), fixed gate lowered, moving gate raised, moving gate returned. On the trial mount the gates were linked 90 degrees out of phase, which was unsatisfactory; the final design used separate control levers and hydraulic cylinders with smaller fingers [S11, p. 34].
3. Hooks pulled a shell from the athwartships bay into the fore-and-aft bay and pushed it into the shell hoist [S11, p. 34].
4. Shell hoists were pusher type with a single set of moving pawls and fixed retaining pawls, and no scuttle at the bottom. A shell was pushed by the push-pull gear directly into the hoist through a shaped opening and toppled onto a platform sloped 5 degrees below the horizontal, the hoist itself being raked 5 degrees from vertical. A spring-loaded crank closed the anti-rolling stops [S11, p. 36].
5. All three hoists ran down to the bottom of the lower shell handling room and had entrances in both the upper and lower handling rooms. Normally they were supplied from the lower shell handling room; in action more shells came up from the shell rooms. When the lower room was exhausted, supply continued from the upper by inserting portable platforms in the hoist [S11, p. 36]. Each hoist had four moving pawls and three fixed pawls [S11, p. 36].
6. The only interlock on the shell hoist was manual: a man at the hoist entrance worked a lever mechanically connected to tell-tales and locking pins at the push-pull operator's position and in the gunhouse, to prevent the hoist being raised before a shell was loaded [S11, p. 36].
7. In the gunhouse the top pawl lifted the shell into a U-shaped tilting bucket set to the side of and above the gun bore axis, where a spring pawl retained it. There was no interlock to stop the hoist being raised when the bucket was down; drill was relied on. A safety interlock for nose-fuzed shells was being designed but was never fitted [S11, p. 37].
8. The bucket was tilted to 8 degrees above the horizontal by a hydraulic rack, crank and connecting rod. Retaining clips opened by link gearing and a cam rail, and the shell rolled onto a waiting tray, held by stops until rolled onto the receiving tray of the combined shell loading bogie and rammer [S11, p. 37].
9. The shell loading bogie and rammer weighed about three tons and ran on four wheels on rails parallel to the bore. Stowed, its upper surface was 8 degrees above the horizontal. Moved forward, the front wheels ran down a ramp to bring bogie and shell to the 3 degree loading angle, which was necessary to let the cordite rammer and gun loading cage cylinder swing under the receiving tray. A tray protected the breech thread. Power came through telescopic pipes. The forward movement was about 13 feet, and the bogie was held forward by hydraulic pressure [S11, p. 37].
10. The rammer was a chain type driven through a spring shock absorber by a pinion, rack and hydraulic piston, with hydraulic buffer stops at each end and a rigid bar supporting the chain. The original bogie had three pairs of wheels and friction drive; both types were shipped to the United States [S11, p. 37].

Separate gunhouse rammers were used for shell and cordite, the cordite being loaded in six one-sixth charges by a single rammer stroke [S11, p. 18]. NavWeaps describes the same: each gun had its own pusher shell hoist and cordite cage hoist, shells were delivered vertically and tipped for ramming, the cylindrical flashtight charge container held all six charges in line for a single rammer stroke, two auxiliary hoists were fitted in each turret, and transfer of shell in the gunhouse was done manually by overhead travellers and chain purchase [S5].

### Embarking ammunition

A conventional grab on the upper deck lowered a shell to skids on the shell room deck, and the push-pull gear filled the bays. Two shell transfer bogies sat in the fixed structure outside the lower shell handling room and were traversed by power or by hand to one of three entrances; the bogie was locked to the turret and the shell pulled out by the end hook of the push-pull gear in the handling room. The only way to get a shell into the upper shell handling room was to load it into the lower room and lift it by the auxiliary shell hoist or the main hoists, which was very slow [S11, p. 34].

Estimates for ammunitioning ship differed. Lt Cdr Matsumura of Kure gave 24 hours working day and night. Captain K. Kuroda, Yamato's gunnery officer at her sinking, who had been three weeks in the post and had never taken on ammunition, gave four days working daylight only, and thought his the better estimate [S11, p. 34]. Cordite was loaded through the normal embarking hoists directly to the magazines [S11, p. 34].

### Loading cycle times

O-45(N) paragraph 27 records that estimates of the loading cycle varied from 40 seconds at full elevation to 28 seconds at presumably low elevation. Lt Cdr Matsumura stated that a prolonged series of trials had been run to establish the actual rate of fire; the best result obtained was one round in 28 seconds, but one round in 30 seconds was the normal rate, and the elevation at which those results were obtained is not known [S11, p. 50].

Mr Sugiyama gave the individual times approximately as follows, which he could not remember exactly [S11, p. 50]:

| Step | Time |
|---|---|
| Depressing gun from 20 degrees elevation to loading angle at 8 deg/sec, allowing for cut-off | 2.75 to 3 sec |
| Opening breech | 2.0 to 2.5 sec |
| Moving shell bogie forward | 3 sec |
| Ramming shell | 3 sec |
| Withdrawing rammer and returning bogie | 5 sec |
| Swinging cordite cylinder and rammer to loading position | 3 sec |
| Ramming charge | 3 sec |
| Withdrawing rammer | 3 sec |
| Returning cordite cylinder and rammer to firing position | 3 sec |
| Closing breech | 2 sec |
| Elevating gun | 2.75 to 3 sec |
| Recoil and run-out | 2.5 to 3 sec |

That totals 35 to 36.5 seconds at 20 degrees elevation neglecting time on aim, say 36 seconds, which the report says agrees with a 30 second cycle at 3 degrees and approximately 40 seconds at 41 degrees. Captains Iwashima and Date gave the rate of fire as 1.5 rounds per minute at full elevation, which the report accepts as correct. The speed of the ammunition hoists and the handling room operations allowed an ample margin to supply shell and charge in the gunhouse with no delay in firing [S11, p. 50].

NavWeaps gives a firing cycle of about 30 seconds at the +3 degree loading angle, which equates to a range of no more than 6,560 yards (6,000 m), with about 11 seconds added by the elevation and depression time needed to reach 41 degrees, and suggests 35 seconds as reasonable for most battle ranges [S5].

### Centre gun delay

When mounted in a triple mount with bore separation of 350 cm (11.48 feet), a delay of 0.08 seconds was introduced in the firing circuit of the centre gun [S11, p. 17]. NavWeaps notes that more recent works give 0.300 seconds, which would put about 755 feet (230 m) between the outer shells and the centre shell [S5].

### The mission's overall judgement of the mount

Report O-45(N) states that the 46 cm triple mount was the first large mount designed and produced by the Japanese that was not practically identical with the large British mounts built before the First World War for Kongo. Shell and powder hoists were copied, but the mechanisms were generally unique. Compared with British and US 16-inch, 14-inch and 8-inch practice the mount was simple: no complicated hydraulic safety interlocks and comparatively few mechanical ones, with reliance placed on good drill to avoid accidents. Even allowing for the size of the gun the mission judged that an unduly large factor of safety had been allowed, producing a very heavy mount [S5, quoting S11].

The mission listed the features of most interest as the method of stowing and moving shells, the powder cage and rammer designed to load a full charge in a single rammer stroke, and the attachment of the elevating piston rod to the slide, which avoided a complicated slide locking gear and cut the locking and unlocking time out of the loading cycle. It also named the powder bogie and its fixed-to-moving transfer mechanisms, the gunhouse bogie and rammer, the wormless training gear with its coaster gear substitute for friction discs, and the electric cable leading-in gear [S5, quoting S11].

The Japanese said considerable attention had been paid to flashtightness. Service performance was considered very satisfactory apart from the large quantities of lubricating oil consumed by the training and powder hoist gears, which the Japanese could ill afford [S5, quoting S11].

Most mountings were worked by hand. Some triples had Ward-Leonard remote power control driven by the Type 95 anti-aircraft director, giving 12 degrees per second in elevation and 18 in train, through 1 hp motors [S8]. Crews were nine on a manually controlled triple (one pointer, one trainer, one sight setter, two loaders per gun), seven on a twin and three on a single (gun layer, loader, gun captain) [S8]. All guns were air cooled; crews sometimes wrapped wet cloths around the barrels [S8].

Most mountings carried no shield. The exception was the Yamato class, whose mountings were given Ducol steel shields to protect against blast [S8].

Report O-47(N)-2 tabulates the shipboard installation for Yamato and Musashi as 40 triple mounts, no twins and 30 singles. The table is introduced only as "the most recent 25mm armament", with no date printed; the report itself is dated 19 February 1946. Shinano is not named in that table or anywhere else in the report's 25 mm section [S20, p. 14]. The Technical Mission's Figure 7 in report O-45(N) tabulates 46 25 mm mounts for Yamato and 52 for Musashi [S11, p. 13].

The Japanese listed the deficiencies of these mountings to the mission in decreasing order of seriousness [S8]:

1. The multiple mounts could not be trained and elevated fast enough, by power or by hand.
2. The gunsights were inadequate against high speed aircraft.
3. The guns vibrated excessively, making them hard to hold on target.
4. The ammunition supply equipment had inadequate capacity, causing interrupted fire and a greatly reduced operating routine.
5. Muzzle blast caused problems for gun crew and equipment.

The 15-round magazine forced frequent stoppages for change-outs, and all magazines had to be loaded by hand because no loading equipment was ever developed [S8].

### The 25 mm as USNTMJ report O-47(N)-2 describes it

Report O-47(N)-2, "Japanese Naval Guns and Mounts, Article 2: AA Machine Guns and Mounts", of 19 February 1946, is the tier 1 account [S20].

Japan decided in 1935 to adopt the French 25 mm Hotchkiss to replace the 40 mm Vickers, ordering a few guns and mounts made to Japanese requirements that specified a cyclic rate of 180 to 200 shots a minute and a 250 gram projectile. Those were designated Type J. Firing trials were held at Yokosuka Naval Arsenal in 1935, and shipboard installations in quantity began in 1936 with manual-drive twin mounts of French and Japanese manufacture. Japan then devised a power drive for remote control, designed the triple mount in 1941 and the single in 1943, and designed submarine mounts in single, twin and triple form [S20, pp. 8 to 9].

Ten mount models are listed: Model 1 (single, twin and triple, surface craft and land bases, single free-swinging, twin and triple with gearing and handwheel control); Model 2 (twin and triple, surface craft); Model 3 (single, surface craft, free swinging); Model 4 (single, twin and triple, submarines, the single lowerable by hand); Model 4 Mod 1 (single, submarines, not lowerable); Model 4 Mod 2 (single, submarines, lowerable from a remote station by mechanical drive); Model 5 (twin and triple, submarines, manual gear drive); Models 6 and 8 (single, land use on a two-wheel carriage, free swinging); Model 10 (single, torpedo boats, traverse by body inside a ring, elevation by manual gear drive) [S20, p. 12].

On armour the report is specific about this class:

> Armor was not usually supplied as part of the mount. An exception is a Ducol steel shield furnished on some of the multiple mounts of YAMATO class battleships, but even here the primary purpose was protection against blast. Splinter shields were provided around some gun mounts as part of the ship. [S20, p. 12]

The report footnotes Ducol steel as approximately equivalent to US High Tensile Steel [S20, p. 12].

No special means were provided for disposing of fired cartridge cases; when they piled up the crew had to sweep them away, and ejection was a problem at high elevation, where stoppages were frequent [S20, p. 13].

The power drive was not designated separately but formed part of the Type 95 Machine Gun Fire Control Arrangement, using the Ward-Leonard system on the mount and taking signals from the Type 95 director. Control was remote only or manual only. Maximum rate of train with all guns firing was 18 degrees per second and of elevation 12 degrees per second, driven by two separate motors of the same design and rating, one for each axis, each 1 hp, 220 V, 3.6 A, rated continuous at no load, ten minutes at half load and three minutes at full load [S20, p. 13].

Three sights were used: the Le Prieur mechanical lead computing sight, commonly called LPR; the open ring sight; and an optical ring sight of etched glass. The Le Prieur sight was used extensively on multiple mounts, as standby equipment where a power drive and director were fitted, and the wartime policy was to reserve most of them for manual multiple mounts aboard ship, where the need was greater. Single mounts and all land-base mounts used the single open ring sight. A few optical ring sights were in service and were considered unsatisfactory [S20, pp. 13 to 14].

Production totalled about 33,000 guns and 20,000 mounts of all types [S20, p. 14].

Crews as the report gives them, which match the NavWeaps figures: nine men on a manual-controlled triple (one pointer, one trainer, one sight setter and two loaders per gun); seven on a manual-controlled twin, manned the same way; three on a free-swinging single (one gun layer, one loader, one gun captain) [S20, p. 14].

Four mounts of each of the three types, complete with guns, were shipped to the United States from Sasebo Naval Gun Factory as NavTechJap equipment numbers JE10-3201-0 to 3 (twin), JE10-3202-0 to 3 (triple) and JE10-3203-0 to 3 (single) [S20, p. 15].

The mission's summary places the weapon: "The 25mm Hotchkiss gun was by far the most important of the six service guns described in this report… employed in single, twin, and triple assemblies on practically every type of vessel." The Japanese rated it second only to the 10 cm Type 98 for anti-aircraft defence and considered it only slightly less effective. The mission's own criticism was that the Le Prieur sight was not calibrated for sufficiently high target speeds, and that even if it had been, the power drives had insufficient capacity to follow; and that the Japanese had not been able to design a free-swinging mount carrying more than one gun, or an easily controllable mount in the 25 mm size larger than the triple [S20, pp. 1, 7].

Water cooling was avoided because of the added manufacturing complication and maintenance difficulty, and a crude method of laying wet cloths on the barrel was sometimes practised. Quick barrel removal was recognised as desirable but the designs were not changed to allow it, and development of higher-capacity ammunition supply equipment appeared to be nil [S20, p. 7].

The mission read the Yamato-class outfit as evidence of policy: "The installation of 40 triple and 30 single mounts of 25mm caliber on the newer battleships and aircraft carriers demonstrates that the Japanese Navy was keeping pace with the other major navies in the policy of increasing tremendously the anti-aircraft defense" [S20, p. 1].

## 13.2 mm machine guns

Both ships carried four 13.2 mm machine guns in two twin mounts as designed [S28], and the Technical Mission's Figure 7 counts two 13 mm mounts on each ship [S11, p. 13]. Musashi's outfit on completion is given as four 13.2 mm guns in two twin mounts [S2]. Ammunition carriers for the Type 93 13 mm twin mount beside the bridge were among the few ratings allowed to use the bridge lift in an emergency [S41].

The 13.2 mm Hotchkiss was chosen to replace the obsolete 12.7 mm Vickers; production stood at 1,200 guns a month at the end of the war [S20, p. 18].

## Aircraft

Yamato and Musashi were designed with two catapults and seven F1M2 seaplanes [S28]. Yamato operated an F1M2 "Pete" spotter, which confirmed the fall of her first salvo at Samar on 25 October 1944 [S1]. Musashi's F1M2 capsized in rough seas on one occasion, and another attempted an interception [S2].

## Torpedoes

No torpedo tubes are recorded in the armament lists for either ship [S28], [S4].

## Conflicts between sources

- Chamber volume of the 46 cm gun: about 680 dm3 [S5] against 480 litres [S11, p. 16].
- Propellant charge weight: 794 lbs (360 kg) [S5] against 330 kg (728 lbs) [S11, p. 16].
- Turret weight: 2,730.2 tons (2,774 mt) [S5] against a total revolving weight of 2,510 metric tons [S11, p. 17].
- Armour-piercing shell weight: 1,458.3 kg, 1,460 kg, or 1,500 to 1,520 kg depending on the source; NavWeaps sets out the case for the lightest figure [S5].
- Burster weight: 23.9 kg [S5, from NPG Report No. 289] against 33.85 kg [S5, from "The Yamato Class and Subsequent Planning"], the latter being explosive plus liner.
- Centre gun firing delay: 0.080 seconds [S11, p. 17] against 0.300 seconds in more recent works [S5].
- Turret elevation rate: 6 deg/sec designed and 8 deg/sec obtained [S11, pp. 18, 50]; Skulski's text gives 10 deg/sec, which NavWeaps believes is an error [S5].
- Number of hydraulic pumps. O-45(N) says three turbo hydraulic pumps supplied the ring main, one of them a standby [S11, p. 17]. O-53(N) says four per ship, one per turret and one standby [S22, p. 7].
- Hydraulic working pressure. O-45(N) gives 1,000 to 1,100 psi [S11, p. 17]; O-53(N) gives 1,000 lbs/in2 [S22, pp. 7, 8].
- Speed of the 500 hp training engines. O-45(N) gave 53 rpm at the maximum training speed of 2 deg/sec and said the figure could not be confirmed; O-53(N) Enclosure (C) gives 250 rpm at 503.1 hp and 350 rpm at 704.3 hp from test bench trials [S22, p. 8].
- Type 3 incendiary tube count and burst pattern. NavWeaps gives 900 incendiary tubes expelled in a 20 degree cone with a flame about 5 m (16 feet) long [S5]. USNTMJ report O-19 Table III gives 996 tubes and 504 stays, 1,500 in all, an angle of dispersion of 15 degrees and an effective diameter of burst of 242 m, with a flame about 17 feet long [S21, pp. 8, 14].
- Type 91 projectile weight in the primary record. O-19 Table IX gives 3,215 lbs and Table XVIII gives 3,212 lbs for the same projectile [S21, pp. 30, 53].
- Type 91 muzzle velocity. O-19 gives 2,560 ft/sec [S21, pp. 30, 53]; NavWeaps gives 2,559 fps (780 mps) [S5].
- Type 91 maximum range. O-19 Table XVIII gives 44,700 yds [S21, p. 53]; the range table in O-45(N) and NavWeaps gives 45,960 yds at 45 degrees and 46,050 yds at 48 degrees [S11, p. 17], [S5].
- Type 91 projectile length. O-19 gives 77.0 in [S21, p. 30]; NavWeaps gives 76.9 in for the Type 91 and 81.0 in for the Type 1 [S5].
- Type 0 common weight. O-19 Table I gives 2,992 lbs [S21, p. 9]; NavWeaps gives 2,998 lbs [S5].
- 25 mm single mount weight. O-47(N)-2 gives 185 kg (407 lb) [S20, p. 12]; NavWeaps gives 1,731 lbs (785 kg) [S8].
- 25 mm barrel life. O-47(N)-2 says figures from 3,000 to 15,000 equivalent service rounds were obtained from various sources [S20, p. 10]; NavWeaps gives 12,000 rounds [S8].
- 25 mm maximum range and ceiling. O-47(N)-2 gives 7,500 m (8,200 yds) at approximately 50 degrees, maximum ordinate 5,500 m and maximum effective range 3,000 m [S20, p. 11]; NavWeaps gives 7,439 yds at 45 degrees, 8,200 yds at 50 degrees, and an anti-aircraft ceiling at 85 degrees of 9,843 ft effective and 18,040 ft maximum [S8].
- 25 mm production. O-47(N)-2 gives about 33,000 guns and 20,000 mounts [S20, p. 14]; NavWeaps gives about 33,000 guns and about 20,000 mountings of all types [S8], which agree.
- Whether the Type 1 armour-piercing projectile is a distinct design. NavWeaps treats Type 91 and Type 1 as separate projectiles differing in windscreen length, driving band and dye bag, the Type 1 superseding the Type 91 in 1941 [S5]. O-19 has no Type 1: its narrative and Table IX call the projectile Type 91 while Table XVIII labels the same design "A.P. Model 1", and it draws no distinction between them [S21, pp. 30, 53].
- 25 mm mount counts. Report O-47(N)-2 gives Yamato and Musashi 40 triples, no twins and 30 singles [S20, p. 14]. Report O-45(N) Figure 7 gives 46 mounts for Yamato and 52 for Musashi [S11, p. 13]. combinedfleet gives Yamato's final outfit as 50 triples and 2 singles [S1] and Musashi's last as 35 triples and 25 singles [S2]. The NHHC essay gives 54 triples for Yamato in 1945 [S24].
- 12.7 cm mount counts. Report O-45(N) Figure 7 tabulates two 12.7 cm anti-aircraft mounts for Yamato and six for Musashi [S11, p. 13]. That figure for Yamato cannot be reconciled with the twelve twin mounts recorded after her 1944 refit [S1], [S7]; the report is a Japanese sketch drawn from memory in 1945 and the cell is recorded here as printed.
