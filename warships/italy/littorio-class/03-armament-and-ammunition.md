## Main battery: 381 mm/50 Ansaldo Model 1934 and Model 1939

Nine guns in three triple turrets, two superfiring forward and one aft [S28, p. 169]. The guns were built in two batches of twenty. The Model 1934 armed Littorio and Vittorio Veneto, the Model 1939 armed Roma and Impero. Ansaldo built the guns for Littorio and Impero and three of Roma's; Odero-Terni-Orlando built six of Roma's. Littorio's guns were assembled from six major components against four in the others; all but Littorio's were built as an A tube in two layers over the chamber and part of the rifled bore, the outer layer continuing to the muzzle, with a jacket over about 72 per cent of the length and a breech bush screwing into the jacket. The later guns had a loose liner that could be withdrawn from the breech end. All used a Welin breech block, hydro-pneumatically operated, opening to the left in the left gun and to the right in the centre and right guns, with compressed-air scavenging of propellant gas after firing. Unlike the French and German 38 cm guns, the bore was a true 381 mm (15.0 in) [S1].

The mountings were Ansaldo designs; those on Littorio and two of Roma's were built by OTO. Elevation, training and hoisting were by electric motors through reduction gears. The guns sat in individual cast steel cradles separated by splinter bulkheads, and a further splinter bulkhead divided the turret rangefinder from the guns. The turret rode on 72 tapered rollers in groups of four, the lower roller path flat and the upper tapered, with a ring of 24 spring-loaded vertical rollers immediately below and eight centring rollers at the lower end of the turret axis. Training and elevation were follow-the-pointer, with stabilised aiming available to the layer [S1].

The train limits above are the mechanical maxima. At the extremes turrets 2 and 3 fired directly over the 152 mm mountings. After 1942 firing at elevations between 0 and +2 degrees was restricted, to limit blast on the weather deck, to training angles on either side between 50 and 120 degrees for turret 1, 45 and 120 degrees for turret 2, and 70 and 135 degrees for turret 3 [S1].

### Loading cycle from magazine to breech

The turret stalk carried an upper and a lower ring carousel at its lowest levels. Shell rooms lay below the magazines. Projectiles were moved by overhead gear to a ring conveyor running on rails in the shell room; the conveyor fed them down an inclined ramp to the lower ring carousel, which supplied the lower projectile hoists. Projectiles rose horizontally to a working chamber directly below the gunhouse. Propellant bags passed through scuttles to the upper ring carousel and were loaded by hand into bucket hoists running up to the working chamber. From the working chamber a combination cage carried both: a pair of three-level cages served each gun, one loading the gun in the up position while the other was stocked in the down position. The upper two levels of each cage held three propellant bags each and the lower compartment held the projectile. Electric winches drove the cages. In the working chamber the three levels were loaded from vertically arranged transfer trays; in the gunhouse the shell compartment was brought in line with the gun axis and rammed, then the two charge compartments were lowered and rammed in succession. The upper hoists were set back 14 degrees from the vertical, one degree off the loading angle so that the rammer chain stayed rigid over its whole run. Auxiliary hoists ran from the magazines and shell rooms directly to the gunhouse [S1].

The arrangement of shell rooms below the propellant magazines, both beneath the gunhouse in the turret structure, is confirmed independently [S27, p. 321].

The propellant charge was 490 lbs (222.2 kg) of NAC or F.C. powder, made up in six bags rammed three at a time. Reduced charges were provided for practice and coastal bombardment, and a training charge as well. The SAPBC projectile was unusual in carrying two burster cavities, each with its own fuze. The outfit was 495 APC and 171 SAPBC projectiles [S1]. Another statement of the same outfit gives 495 AP and 171 SAP, 4,320 propellant charges, 666 rounds in all, 74 per gun split 55 AP and 19 SAP [S25, p. 94].

An HE projectile of 1,706 lbs (774 kg) with a muzzle velocity of 2,854 fps (870 mps) appears in Campbell but in none of the other references; it may have been developed but did not enter service [S1]. The same conclusion, that the 774 kg HE shell was developed but never served on the class, is drawn from the Italian sources [S25, pp. 74, 94].

### Dispersion and barrel wear

The gun had a maximum range greater than that of any other battleship gun of its day despite a modest maximum elevation, and paid for it in excessive dispersion and a barrel life about half that of other nations' heavy guns [S1]. APC muzzle velocity was originally 2,854 fps (870 mps) and was cut to 2,789 fps (850 mps) to reduce dispersion and lengthen barrel life [S1]. The same reduction, from 870 to 850 m/s, is recorded in the Italian literature [S25, pp. 74, 94].

Quality of manufacture, not the gun, is given as the main cause of the scatter. Italian practice let the producing firm supply the sample for proof, so batches varied; some actions showed a run of good projectiles and others very bad ones. The contrast drawn is between Littorio's shooting at the First Battle of Sirte at 32,000 yards and Vittorio Veneto's at Gaudo on 28 March at 24,000 yards, Littorio's groups being the tighter despite the longer range [S1].

## Secondary battery: 152 mm/55 Models 1934 and 1936 in triple turrets

Twelve guns in four triple turrets, two abreast the No. 2 main turret and two on either side of the after turret [S29, p. 289]. The Model 1934 was an Ansaldo monobloc gun with a loose liner and a horizontal sliding breech block; the Model 1936 was an OTO gun of two tubes with a loose liner and the same breech. Littorio carried four Model 1934 triple mountings, Vittorio Veneto and Roma four Model 1936 each [S2].

The class outfit was 840 AP, 1,428 HE and 252 AA rounds. The HE round with a time fuze was the anti-aircraft projectile [S2].

In the three-gun mountings each gun had a lower endless chain hoist ending in a handling room below the gunhouse, and an upper endless bucket hoist running from there into the gunhouse. Training and elevation were electric, rammers pneumatic, and spent cartridge cases were ejected through tubes under the barrels [S2].

Turret armour for the class: face 11.0 in (28.0 cm) KC, front sides 5.1 in (13.0 cm) KC, rear sides and rear 3.1 in (8.0 cm), front roof 5.9 in (15.0 cm), rear roof 4.1 in (10.5 cm) [S2].

Dispersion with APC was 260 to 300 feet (80 to 90 m) at 19,140 yards (17,500 m); with HE it was 525 to 656 feet (160 to 200 m) at the same range, blamed on the nose shape and nose fuze of the HE shell. A redesign was studied in 1941 but did not enter service [S2].

Maximum range at 45 degrees was 28,150 yards (25,740 m) with APC and 27,231 yards (24,900 m) with HE [S2].

## Star shell guns: 120 mm/40 Model 1891

Four single mounts, one pattern of the Elswick 4.7-inch (12 cm) QF gun that Ansaldo bought in quantity for armoured cruisers around 1900; during the Second World War the Italians used a few as star shell guns on capital ships [S6]. They were mounted at the sides amidships [S19]. They could elevate to 32 degrees and fired a 29.3 kg (65 lb) semi-fixed round to an effective 5,000 m [S25, pp. 80-81].

## Heavy anti-aircraft battery: 90 mm/50 Model 1939 in stabilised single mounts

Twelve single mounts arranged closely amidships [S29, p. 289]. The gun was an autofretted monobloc barrel with a screwed-on breech ring carrying a horizontal sliding block, the barrel attached to the receiver by a bayonet joint; the design began at 48 calibres and production guns were 50 [S3].

The class outfit was 5,842 AA and 50 illumination rounds, with 90 ready rounds in the handling rooms [S3]. The Italian association sheet gives 5,500 HE and 240 illuminating rounds, a 10 kg projectile at 845 m/sec, and a maximum range of 13,000 m [S19].

The mountings were stabilised in four axes: training, elevation, roll correction and pitch correction, using eleven gyros. Roll correction was plus or minus 14 degrees and pitch correction plus or minus 5 degrees. Remote power control was fitted and retained on this class, whose mounts sat much higher than those of the Duilio class, but electrical and mechanical breakdowns were frequent. The gunhouses were oval and sharply raked to reduce main-battery overpressure. Shield and barbette plating was 4.0 cm face, sides and roof and 1.2 cm rear [S3].

Ammunition came up from the magazine to the main deck by an electric hoist rated at 30 rounds per minute, was passed by hand to a handling room forming the lower compartment of the mounting, and was then raised by a short hoist attached to the stabilised structure, which lifted the rounds vertically and rotated them horizontally into the gunhouse. That hoist was driven by a 2 hp electric motor and could run at 12, 16 or 30 rounds per minute; an automatic fuze setter sat at its top. Loading was by hand with a pantograph link rammer [S3].

The mounting platforms themselves were stabilised, so that the gun crews stood on a floor that stayed horizontal; the anti-pitch, anti-roll and anti-turn movements were made by electric motors driving worm screws set orthogonally beneath the platforms and beneath the 90 mm director turrets [S19].

## Light anti-aircraft battery: 37 mm/54 Breda

Twenty barrels on the class, in eight twin and four single mounts [S22]. Four single Model 1939 collapsible mounts were carried right forward on the forecastle and could not fire while turrets 1 and 2 fired ahead. Of the twin mounts, two sat on the roof of turret 2 and two on the roof of turret 3, and the remaining sixteen barrels were on the deckhouse to port and starboard of the funnel [S19].

Elevation and training were manual on every mount. The Model 1932 twin was water cooled with a stabilised line of sight through plus or minus 10 degrees of roll; the Model 1938 twin dropped the water cooling. Both twins vibrated badly, because the barrels were fixed and only the breeches recoiled, and their stabilisation, a weight and roller arrangement that let the trunnion axis list, did not work and was discontinued. Magazines held six rounds and fed vertically; they could be loaded in sequence to keep the rate up. The crew of a Model 1938 twin was a gun captain, layer, trainer, two first loaders and two ammunition handlers, supplied from ready lockers near the gun. The single mounts vibrated less, having an equilibrator linked to the cradle so the whole gun recoiled, and fed horizontally [S4].

Elevation and training were manual [S5].

## Aircraft and catapult

A single trainable catapult was fitted at the extreme stern for three aircraft [S19]. The aircraft facilities were on the quarterdeck, where six Cierva autogyros had first been planned; a single catapult was fitted instead [S28, p. 171]. The aircraft were three IMAM Ro.43 reconnaissance floatplanes or navalised Reggiane Re.2000 fighters [S31, p. 19]; the Re.2000 was a wheeled aircraft and had to land ashore [S28, p. 171]. Two of the three Ro.43 were later replaced by Re.2000 [S19].

## Conflicts between sources

The Marina Militare data sheets for Vittorio Veneto and Impero list ten 381 mm/50 guns [S8; S10], while the sheets for Littorio and Roma list nine [S7; S9] and every other source consulted gives nine in three triple turrets [S28, p. 169; S22; S17].

Main battery projectile weight: 884.8 kg APC and 824.3 kg SAPBC [S1], against 822 kg for the projectile without qualification [S19]. Muzzle velocity: 850 mps for APC after the reduction [S1], against 870 m/sec [S19].

Main battery ammunition outfit: 74 rounds per gun [S1; S25, p. 94], against 75 projectiles and 480 charge elements per gun [S19].

Main battery rate of fire: 1.3 rounds per minute [S1], one round every 45 seconds [S19; S27, p. 321].

Secondary battery muzzle velocity: 910 mps APC [S2] against 925 m/sec [S19]. Rate of fire: 4 to 5 rounds per minute [S2], slightly better than four rounds a minute [S27, p. 330], one round every 13 seconds [S19].

90 mm outfit: 5,842 AA and 50 illumination rounds [S3] against 5,500 HE and 240 illuminating [S19].

NavWeaps states in one sentence that Ansaldo built the guns for Littorio and Impero and three for Roma, and in the next that OTO built the guns for Littorio and six for Roma; the two statements cannot both be true of Littorio, and the page gives no third builder for Vittorio Veneto [S1].

20 mm outfit as completed: thirty-two on Roma against twenty-four on the first pair, or twenty-eight against sixteen, depending on the authority [S25, pp. 418-419; S28, pp. 171-172]; a class sheet gives twenty-eight on Littorio and Roma and thirty-two on Vittorio Veneto from 1942 [S20]; the Marina Militare sheets give thirty for all four ships [S7; S8; S9; S10].
