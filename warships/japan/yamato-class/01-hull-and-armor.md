# Yamato class: hull and armor

Status: covered

The tier 1 source for this facet is US Naval Technical Mission to Japan report S-06-2, "Reports of Damage to Japanese Warships, Article 2: Yamato, Musashi, Taiho, Shinano", written in January 1946 by Constructor Commander R. E. Holtzworth. It carries the ships' principal characteristics as the mission recorded them from Japanese officers and from the Fourth Section (Ship Construction) of the Navy Ministry, and it is the source of the finding about the belt-to-bulkhead joint. Because the Japanese destroyed most design records in August 1945, almost every figure in this facet comes from post-war interrogation rather than from a surviving drawing. <!-- no-claim -->

## Design history

The Japanese told the mission the class was designed between 1934 and 1937 [S12, p. 1]. Constructor Rear Admiral Fukuda Keiji's design team completed the final design in March 1937 [S1], [S2]. navypedia records that from 1934 to 1935 twenty-four designs were prepared and considered, ranging from 49,000 to 68,000 tons displacement, 24 to 31 knots, and 410 or 460 mm main guns; that by October 1935 the general characteristics were fixed at nine 460 mm guns, 27 knots, combined diesel and turbine machinery and protection against 457 mm shells; and that the cruising diesels were dropped during detailed design because replacing units that large would have been very difficult, the design going over to pure steam turbine machinery before it was finalised in March 1937 [S28].

The armour was designed to give an immune zone against 457 mm shells between 20,000 and 30,000 m and to withstand a 1,000 kg aircraft bomb dropped from 15,000 feet (4,572 m); navypedia puts the total armour mass at 22,534 tons [S28].

The Technical Mission's own summary is blunter: the class was designed 1934 to 1937 and built for world conquest, displacing some 73,000 tons fully loaded and carrying a 46 cm main battery [S12, p. 1].

### Design stability criteria

The Japanese design criteria as the mission set them out, with calculations that included volume up to the flying (forecastle) deck [S12, p. 8]:

1. With all unprotected structure flooded, the intact armoured box was to give flotation with a 22 degree range of stability. Unprotected structure meant both ends, all voids outboard of the side armour, and the hull above the armoured second deck.
2. With all outboard voids on one side flooded, the list was not to exceed 18 degrees, at which angle the main deck on the low side enters the water.
3. With either unprotected end flooded, the ship was not to plunge.
4. With three outboard firerooms, an outboard engine room and the voids on one side flooded, the ship was not to capsize.

## Torpedo protection

This is the best documented part of the class's protection, because the Technical Mission investigated it after four torpedo events across the three ships.

### Origin in model tests

The underwater protection system for the class, including Shinano, was designed after a series of model caisson tests at Yokosuka Navy Yard. The programme started in 1934 in connection with the modernisation of Nagato and with the design of Yamato. One-third scale models were used and attacked with 9 kg (19.8 lb) charges. The explosive was TNT at first and was changed toward the end of the programme to a mixture of 60 per cent TNT and 40 per cent hexanitrodiphenylamine. The Japanese took 9 kg at one third scale as equivalent to 200 kg of explosive at full size [S12, p. 7].

The last test, early in 1939, used a full scale model of Yamato fitted with a 75 mm (3 inch) Ducol steel internal armoured bulkhead and attacked with a 400 kg (880 lb) charge. The holding, innermost, bulkhead did not remain watertight but was not split open. The principal weakness was at the bottom connection of the holding bulkhead to the shell, and that section was redesigned for Yamato [S12, p. 7].

### The system as built

The system was designed to withstand a 400 kg charge and that was confirmed by full scale test. A three-inch bulkhead was to have been used. Before construction, considerations of the underwater trajectory of projectiles caused the bulkhead to be radically increased in thickness. As installed it was eight inches thick at the top, at the connection with the lower edge of the 16.1 inch main side belt, tapering to three inches at the bottom connection with the shell. The Japanese expected the armoured bulkhead to leak after a 400 kg charge but believed the two inboard longitudinal bulkheads would remain tight [S12, p. 7].

Total depth of the system was about 16.8 feet, and the armoured bulkhead stood about 3 m (9.8 feet) from the bulge at mid draft [S12, p. 8]. navypedia gives the depth as 8 m and the longitudinal bulkhead as 270 mm tapering to 75 mm, 270 mm abreast the magazines and 200 mm abreast the machinery, inclined 15 degrees [S28]. The mission's system description was published separately in report S-01-3 [S12, p. 8].

The two longitudinal bulkheads inboard of the armoured bulkhead were rigidly connected to each other by closely spaced vertical and horizontal floors. The two longitudinal bounding bulkheads of the outboard firerooms were tied by heavy H beams at the upper operating level, to strengthen the inboard bulkhead against air pressure in closed firerooms. When the holding bulkhead deflected under a hit, those beams punched holes in the inboard bulkhead, which is what happened aboard Shinano [S12, p. 8].

The outboard layers were carried void rather than liquid filled, on the evidence of small-scale under-bottom experiments [S12, p. 8]. Outboard voids and machinery spaces were fitted with ten-inch diameter sea valves operated remotely, and the outboard voids had air escapes that were used to indicate flooding [S12, p. 9].

### The joint between the main belt and the lower armoured bulkhead

The decision to use the heavy lower bulkhead brought the difficult design problem of joining the main side belt to it. After considerable discussion and debate the joint shown on Plate I was adopted. Its design was based primarily on what the steel makers could produce in the special shapes required within a reasonable length of time. Several officers felt that a delay in construction was acceptable and a more efficient joint should be adopted, but they were overruled. The mission's verdict: the joint is of poor design, being entirely dependent for transverse strength on the shearing strength of tap rivets and three-ply rivets, and in addition the joint itself was not adequately supported in the transverse direction [S12, p. 7].

That judgement was confirmed in service. When USS Skate torpedoed Yamato on 25 December 1943 the hit fell about 4 feet below the surface in way of the main belt above the joint. Water reached the upper magazine of No. 3 turret through a small hole in the longitudinal bulkhead caused by the caving in of the waterline armour, and about 3,000 tons came aboard, far more than the designers had anticipated [S1], [S12, p. 9]. The mission's conclusions were that the joint would rupture and that the first bulkhead inboard of the armoured bulkhead had ruptured [S12, p. 9].

Summarising, the mission wrote that a US warhead with 600 pounds of torpex would rupture Yamato's torpedo defence system, that the amount of inboard flooding might vary with the depth of the hit, that if the point of impact was at or below the joint between the armoured sections inboard flooding probably could not be controlled, and that if the point of impact was in way of the main side belt inboard flooding might be controllable but a hit in such a location would certainly be noticeable from within the space. It put the list from one torpedo hit at the order of 2 to 3 degrees if the ship was reasonably near upright and reasonably intact [S12, p. 15]. Those conclusions were substantiated by Shinano, where four torpedoes struck in way of the torpedo defence system and each produced definite, immediate flooding of inboard spaces [S12, p. 15].

### The palliative fitted to Yamato in 1944

As a result of the Japanese investigation of the December 1943 damage, the Fourth Section (Ship Construction) authorised a 45 degree sloping plate across the corner of the upper void between the two inboard bulkheads, shown on Plate IV in the midship section. It ran the full length of the machinery spaces. The avowed purpose was to maintain watertightness of the void between the two bulkheads, but the mission thought the measure hopelessly inadequate, and the Chief of the Design Branch openly said he thought it of no possible value [S12, pp. 10, 15]. combinedfleet records that the modification, proposed to run the full length of the citadel, was installed only in Yamato and only in the area affected by the torpedo damage, during the Kure docking of 16 January to 3 February 1944 [S1].

### Counterflooding capacity

The mission found counterflooding capacity limited to little more than righting the ship after three torpedoes on one side, that counterflooding the outboard engineering spaces was an extreme measure, that at a 16 degree list the outboard voids could be filled only to about 55 per cent from the sea, that there was no damage control pumping system of adequate capacity, and that liquid-filled outboard layers would have been preferable [S12, p. 16].
