# HMS Duke of York (pennant 17)

Ordered as Anson and renamed before launch [S23]. Third ship of the class, the one that fought the class's only successful gun action against a capital ship, and the one whose turret interiors and fire control spaces were most photographed. The timeline is in facet 08.

## Completion

Built by John Brown at Clydebank, laid down 5 May 1937, launched 28 February 1940, completed 4 November 1941 [S23]. She finished at Rosyth in October to November 1941, embarking Admiralty stores and completing with her full complement, of which 85 per cent were "hostilities only" men [S3].

Her sea trials at Rosyth found the hull fault that ran through the class: low forward sheer was found to cause excessive flooding even in moderate sea conditions [S3].

## North Cape, 26 December 1943

The radar sequence is set out hour by hour in facet 04 and the mounting failures gun by gun in facet 03. In summary, she shot for two hours, made 31 straddles out of 52 broadsides in the first phase and 21 out of 25 in the second, and fired 446 shells in 80 broadsides while still delivering less than 70 per cent of her possible output through mechanical failures and errors in drill, missing 241 firing opportunities [S6]. Her own chronology has fire checked after 77 broadsides at 1929, with at least 13 and possibly up to 30 hits achieved, after which Scharnhorst was a blazing wreck sinking by the stern [S3].

Three mechanical faults are named: the bridge flash tubes in the working chambers failing to close completely, the collapse of shell arresters in the lower hoists, and a shell-cage defect in 'A' turret [S6]. Every gun missed at least some broadsides. B1 was the most reliable, missing three of the 80; A1 missed 73 through poor loading drill and a shell cage fault; and three guns in 'Y' turret were unable to fire for a 15-minute period, missing 17 broadsides [S6].

The 'Y' turret failure has a precise cause. The ship was moving heavily, so the rammers were being worked carefully to avoid driving shells into the arrestors too hard. A roll and heavy lurch coincided with a ramming stroke; the rammers were stopped, but the shells had taken charge and ran into the arrestors before the stop bolt was in position behind them, and the arrestors collapsed into the In position. The shells then projected beyond the shell cage so it could not rise. It took 15 minutes to force them back and reset the arrestors, and the incident led directly to a design change: a hydraulic buffer cylinder replaced the stop bolt, with an interlock preventing ramming until the buffer was in position [S7].

Her gunnery radar was hit and repaired during the action. A shell from Scharnhorst's last salvo before the range opened passed through the foremast and damaged the Type 284M (3) gunnery set; Lieutenant H R K Bates RNVR climbed the mast and fixed the aerial, for which he was awarded a DSC. Another shell from the same salvo hit the after mast and damaged the cables to the Type 281 air warning set. She checked fire at 1824 because of the radar damage and reopened after the repair was completed at 1842 [S3].

Survivors of Scharnhorst were brought aboard; an official photograph shows them under guard on her catapult deck at Scapa Flow [S65].

## Refit for the Pacific, 1944 to 1945

Her 1944 to March 1945 refit removed the catapult and aircraft and renewed the radar; the Type 284 was removed and a target indication radar added with its aerial at the top of the mast [S3], [S23]. Navypedia lists the March 1945 outfit as two Type 274, Type 277, Type 281B, two Type 282 and Type 293, with Type 273, Type 281 and Type 284 removed, alongside two quadruple 40 mm Bofors, two octuple and six quadruple 2-pdr mountings, six twin and fifteen single Oerlikons [S23]. By September 1945 she carried six Mark VII*P quadruple pom-pom mountings, the late-war type with self-contained power and dual joystick control, and she was one of at least three British ships fitted with USN Mark 14 gyro sights on quadruple pom-poms in 1945 as an anti-kamikaze measure [S9].

She sailed for the Mediterranean with Anson on 25 April 1945 and was held at Malta for a major electrical defect [S4].
