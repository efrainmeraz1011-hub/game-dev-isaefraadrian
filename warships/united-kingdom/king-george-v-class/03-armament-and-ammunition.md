# King George V class: armament and ammunition

Status: partial (see gaps.md)

The mounting detail below rests on two transcriptions of the same Admiralty document, the Handbook for the 14-inch B.L. Mark VII gun on twin Mark II and quadruple Mark III mountings, ADM 234/271, which is held at Kew and is not online [S27]. The NavWeaps datapage [S6] and the NavWeaps essay by Matthew Warwick [S7] both state that they draw on it. Where the two disagree, both figures appear below.

## Why ten guns and not twelve

The class was designed to the 14-inch gun limit Britain was then pressing for in the naval treaty talks, and the guns had to be ordered in mid-1936 before the talks concluded, because gun and mounting production set the critical path for the whole ship [S69]. The first arrangement was twelve guns in three quadruple mountings [S69], [S23]. To pay for thicker protection around the magazines while staying inside the 35,000-ton treaty displacement, "B" mounting was cut from four guns to two, giving ten [S69]. Navypedia gives the same sequence, with the armour increase specified as defence against 15-inch shells [S23]. NavWeaps states only that the 14-inch calibre itself was chosen for treaty compliance, and calls the result arguably the weakest-armed battleship built between 1930 and 1946 [S6].

The decision left an odd battery: the twin "B" mounting sits superfiring over the quadruple "A" mounting forward, with the second quadruple aft [S18]. NavWeaps labels the after quadruple "X" in its train-limit table [S6]; the ADM 234/271 essay and the ADM 234/509 gunnery report both call it "Y" [S7], [S14]. This file uses "Y", the usage of the two Admiralty-derived sources.

## Main battery: 14-inch/45 Mark VII

Despite the mark number this was the first 14-inch gun designed for and accepted by the Royal Navy alone; earlier British 14-inch guns came from ships and guns building for other nations or were bought from the United States [S6]. The design reverted to the lower muzzle velocities of the First World War period because of the trouble experienced with the 16-inch/45 Mark I of the Nelson class [S6]. Construction was no-wire radial expansion: tapered inner A tube, A tube, jacket, rectangular breech ring, breech bush in the A tube, and a shrunk collar, with a Welin breech block and a hydraulic Asbury mechanism [S6]. These were the first heavy British guns to recoil in a cast steel cylindrical cradle rather than on a separate slide [S6]. Including two trial guns, 78 were made: 24 by the Royal Gun Factory, 39 by Vickers-Armstrong at Elswick, and 15 by Beardmore [S6].

The propellant was made up in four bags, handled as four quarter charges [S6]. Range table temperature was 70 degrees F, with a correction of 8 fps (2.4 mps) per 10 degrees F (6 degrees C) of charge temperature [S6]. From late 1942 to early 1943 the APC Mark 1b NT "K" shell was introduced, carrying dye and a small fuze and burster to disperse it, which added 5.4 lbs (2.5 kg) to the shell [S6]. A Fleet Order of 20 June 1946 lists the splash colours: King George V yellow, Duke of York green, Anson white, Howe red [S6]. The original outfit was 100 rounds of APC per gun; by mid-1943 five of those hundred had been replaced by time-fuzed HE [S6].

Training was through a worm gear driven by one of two swashplate engines; elevation was by hydraulic cylinder and piston connected to the rear of the cradle, with run-out by compressed air [S6]. The turret stalks carried a vertical roller race just below the main horizontal races, similar to the ones added to Nelson and Rodney in the 1930s [S6]. Remote power control was never fitted to these mountings during the war and the ships used follow-the-pointer control throughout; one source states that King George V had received RP 40 before joining the British Pacific Fleet in 1945 [S6]. The quadruple mounting is credited with over 3,000 working parts [S6], [S7].

The class used a vertical turret face rather than a sloped one. Moving the face back left an uncovered "D" shaped area of barbette top forward of the face plate, about 115 ft2 (10.7 m2), which had to be protected at least as well as the roof; NavWeaps gives a semi-circular plate about 7.9 inches (20 cm) thick per ADM 234/271, adding about 17 tons rather than the 13 tons a weight estimate quoted on the same page had suggested [S6]. Balance plates on the rear armour were 10 in thick on 'A', reduced to 8 in on 'Y' because an octuple pom-pom stood on that roof, and 3 in on 'B' [S7]. Vent holes were drilled through the rear armour and balance plates and covered by light vent plates designed to blow off if pressure in the gunhouse rose sharply [S6], [S7].

'A' gunhouse alone carried a navigating cabinet at the rear, with an all-round training periscope coupled to an azimuth repeater, a rudder indicator, a steering order transmitter, a speed indicator, and a four-position changeover switch for the lower steering position, the after steering position, 'X' engine room, and off [S7]. 'Y' had no navigating cabinet [S7]. A silent cabinet in the turntable compartment held an Admiralty Fire Control Box Mark IV for local control; the guns could be fired from the director control tower, from 'B' turret, or locally [S7]. In 'B', the smaller mounting, the local control position was in the silent cabinet on the left side of the gunhouse rather than in the turntable compartment, and 'B' also served as the tertiary fire control position, which is why its rangefinder was a duplex model [S7].

## The loading cycle, magazine to breech

Magazines lie below the shell rooms [S6]. In 'A', the magazine handing room is at hold level just above the double bottom, square in plan and ringed by eight magazines; the shell handling room and its shell rooms sit directly above, reached from the shell handling room by a hatch [S7]. The magazine deckheads and bulkheads, including those between magazine and handing room, carry 60 lb (1.5 in) of 'D' steel bolted on against splinters [S7]. Each of the four openings from the handing room into the Forward, Port, Starboard and Main magazines has two doors, an inner watertight door and an outer flash-tight door [S7].

1. In the magazine a quarter charge is taken from its case and run along a roller chute to the nearest cordite lift. 'A' and 'B' have four such lifts; 'Y' has two lifts and two flash-tight scuttles, because the after end of the ship gave less vertical room [S6], [S7].
2. The charge goes into the waiting cage through the lower flash door and a foot pedal raises the cage. A counterweight closes the lower flash door as the cage rises, and the ascending cage opens the upper flash door by contact. At handing-room level the cage flap opens and the charge rolls into a waiting tray. The cage descends, gravity closes the upper door and the cage opens the lower one for the next charge. An indicator in the magazine reads Empty or Loaded [S7].
3. In 'Y', where scuttles replace two of the lifts, a quarter charge is pushed through an open flash-tight door into the scuttle and an operating lever closes the magazine-side door and opens the handing-room-side door [S7].
4. In the cordite handing room the charges are carried from the waiting trays to the loading trays of the cordite hoppers, one hopper per gun on a circular platform connected to the hoist trunk. Inner guns are served from the front of the platform, outer guns from the rear. Each hopper is two side-loaded scuttles, one above the other, each holding two charges, so a hopper holds a full charge of four [S6], [S7].
5. The scuttles rotate through 120 degrees, which makes them flash-tight from the handing room, and power-rotated scuttles with chain rammers transfer the charges into the cordite cage of the central ammunition hoist. That cage has two horizontal tubes, each holding two quarter charges [S6], [S7].
6. In the shell rooms, projectiles are stowed horizontally, moved on overhead rails, picked up by a two-fork shell grab, raised and lowered by hydraulic lifting presses and moved by hydraulic traversing gear, two sets per main shell bin [S7]. NavWeaps describes the same movement as overhead trolleys [S6].
7. Each of the four main shell rooms has four shell waiting trays facing the shell handling room through watertight doors. Projectiles are rammed through those doors, over hinged trays, onto the revolving shell ring [S6], [S7].
8. The shell ring for a quadruple mounting holds 16 trays in four groups 90 degrees apart; the twin holds 8 trays in four groups 90 degrees apart [S6]. A full load is 16 shells, four from each main shell room [S7]. The ring can be coupled either to the ship, for loading from the shell rooms, or to the revolving stalk, for ramming into the hoist [S6], [S7]. When coupled to the ship the ring is driven by a 24 bhp hydraulic engine through a flexible coupling, friction clutch, worm gear and pinions, with allowance for eccentricity and for the ring rising or falling up to 1 inch (25 mm) relative to the mounting; when coupled to the stalk a similar engine drives it without that allowance [S6]. Allowance is made for a 1-inch rise or fall of the ship relative to the mounting on both rack and roller path [S7].
9. To ram a shell into the hoist, the control lever goes to "Open Flash Doors", which opens the flash doors between trunk and handing room and moves the telescopic shell arrestors to the Out position. Moving the lever to "Ram" inserts a stop bolt behind the arrestor. With the shell in position and its nose clear of the cage, bolt and arrestor withdraw and the cage can rise [S7].
10. Shell cage and cordite cage rise together up the central ammunition hoist to the working chamber, stacked with the shell on top and two cordite tubes of two charges each below. The lower hoists are worked by winches with two reel drums of different diameters on one shaft, so the two cages arrive together [S6], [S7]. An interlock prevents the cages rising unless the trunk flash doors in both handing rooms are closed [S7].
11. In the working chamber, shell and charges are rammed forward together onto traversers, which run on floor rails and carry them from the central stalk out into line with the guns. The first movement of the control lever opens the flash doors between hoist and traversing compartment; when they are fully open an interlock withdraws and the lever can go to "ram"; after the stroke the lever goes to "withdraw", and when the rammer head is clear of the flash doors a further interlock withdraws and the lever goes to "close flashdoors" [S7].
12. From the traversers, shell and charges are rammed backwards over a bridge tray into the gun loading cage, through another set of flash doors. With the cage lowered into its well it seals the working chamber from the spaces above; the flash doors between bridge tray and well are opened, then those on the front of the cage, the load is rammed in, and the doors close [S7].
13. The cage is raised hydraulically to the RAM SHELL position, a flash door at the rear of the shell compartment opens, and the shell is rammed into the breech. The cage rises to the first cordite ramming position, the flash doors at each end of that tube open and two charges are rammed; it rises again and the second pair follows. The cage then falls back to the well under its own weight [S6], [S7].

Cycle times, from ADM 234/271: central ammunition hoist 26 seconds, working chamber traversers 17 seconds, gun loading hoist 30 seconds including elevating and depressing the gun between 5 and 20 degrees. Those give the nominal maximum of 2 rounds per minute [S7].

Two IWM photographs show the gunhouse end of this chain. One, taken inside a King George V turret, looks forward from the gunloading cage [S40]. Another, in Duke of York in December 1943, shows the captain of the gun answering the working chamber through a voice pipe while working the control levers [S38]; a third looks from the loading cage into the rear of a Duke of York gunhouse, with the crew in anti-flash clothing [S39].

## Flash-tight arrangements

NavWeaps calls the flash precautions in these mountings very elaborate, "perhaps overly so given the problems encountered during World War II" [S6]. The chain from magazine to breech crosses a flash boundary at every stage: the paired watertight and flash-tight doors into the magazine, the counterweighted flash doors of the cordite lift, the 120-degree hopper scuttles, the trunk flash doors at the shell and cordite handing rooms, the flash doors between hoist and traversing compartment, those between bridge tray and gun loading well, those on the front of the gun loading cage, and those at each end of the cordite tubes at the breech [S7].

Drenching is worked separately from the ship's magazine flooding and spraying systems. The drenching system for 15-inch and 14-inch turrets runs off the hydraulic pressure main [S66]. Operating the drenching valve in the cordite handing room soaks every charge in the cordite hoppers and hoist cages at once [S7]. Nozzles in the traversers are worked by the Captain of Turret from an emergency handle in the gunhouse, with an alternative for the Second Captain of Turret; charges inside the gun loading cage at working-chamber level cannot be drenched directly, so sprayer pipes and jets play on the outside of the cage to keep them cool; and the Captain of the Gun can drench cordite in the gun loading cage at gunhouse floor level from a valve within reach under the roof [S7].

The ship-wide magazine rules those mountings sat inside are Admiralty general practice rather than anything specific to this class. Big-ship magazines are watertight compartments protected by armour, ventilated, and in hot positions cooled; temperatures are taken every watch where cooling is fitted and daily everywhere, and entered in the magazine log [S66]. Principal magazines are protected from flash by flash-tight doors and scuttles through which the cartridge must pass on its way to the gun; smaller magazines are not, to save weight [S66]. Magazines and shell rooms can be flooded from the sea and the cordite cases in the main magazines sprayed from the fire main, with valves workable from at least two positions [S66]. Knives, matches and anything that can spark are left outside, and rubber or felt-soled shoes are always worn in B.L. magazines [S66].

## Mounting failures in action

### Denmark Strait, 24 May 1941

Prince of Wales fired 55 of a possible 74 guns in 18 salvoes during the first action, a 25.65 per cent loss of output, at 1.895 salvoes per minute [S14]. In the second action that evening she fired 41 of a possible 48 in 12 salvoes, a 14.45 per cent loss [S14]. In the third action on 25 May she fired five rounds only [S14]. NavWeaps repeats the 55 of 74 figure and attributes her problems to jammed shell rings and associated fittings [S6].

The commanding officer's report names the faults [S14]. Before the action, while the cages were being loaded on the afternoon of 23 May, the front flash doors of No. 2 gun loading cage in 'A' could not be fully opened because the front casing had been burred by the guide-roller lugs on the gun loading rammer head during a withdrawing stroke; it was cleared by filing [S14]. In 'A', No. 1 gun's shell arrestor at shell ring level jammed out on the second ramming and could not be freed before the action, and that gun fired only one salvo [S14]. In 'B', the arrestor at shell ring level on No. 2 central ammunition hoist would not withdraw after ramming; it cannot be stripped in place in a Mark II mounting, so the whole arrestor was removed, and the axis pin of the pinion driving its inner tube was found seized with no effective way to lubricate it [S14]. Also in 'B', the hinge trays at the forward shell room fouled the locking bolt on the revolving shell ring and both trays were bent [S14].

During the action itself, in 'A' the shell ring rammers repeatedly fouled the brackets on the hinge trays for No. 11 interlock, so shell could not be rammed until the turret's bearing was changed; No. 24A interlock failed on No. 2 shell ring rammer after the second salvo; the tappets releasing the shell ring arrestor on No. 4 rammer failed because the shaft carrying their operating levers had twisted, and the rammer was kept going by striking the tappets at every stroke; and No. 4 shell room rammer then failed to clear No. 7 interlock when fully withdrawn, so the ring could not be locked and had to be worked with a pinch-bar at every stroke [S14]. Conditions in 'A' shell handling room were bad: water poured down from the upper part of the mounting, the single drain choked, and the accumulated water washed side to side with the roll, drenching the machinery [S14]. The report asks for a drain at each end of each shell room, larger drain holes in the bins, and a cross connection with two non-return valves so the revolving shell ring ship control can be fed from either side of the hydraulic ring main [S14].

'B' had no mechanical defects during the first action [S14]. In 'Y', No. 25 interlock failed to stop No. 3 central ammunition hoist rising with shell but no cordite at salvo 11, which cost that gun salvoes 15 to 20; the front flash doors of No. 2 gun loading cage failed to open at salvo 12, costing that gun salvo 14 onward, and were traced to the vertical rod working the palm levers needing adjustment; and at salvo 20 the ship's motion slid a shell out of the port shell room into the revolving shell ring while the ring was locked to the trunk and the turret was training, buckling hinge trays and putting the ring out of action until 0825 [S14]. The gunnery narrative records that when the fore director was wooded during the turn away after salvo 18 and the after director could not see through the ship's own smoke, the officer of 'Y' turret went into local control on his own initiative and fired three salvoes; the shell ring jammed during that turn away [S14]. The summary sheet counts four rounds fired in local control by 'Y' before the jam [S14].

Admiral Tovey's despatch records that within a few minutes of the Bismarck shifting fire to her, Prince of Wales had been hit by four 15-inch and three smaller, probably 8-inch, shells, her compass platform was damaged, both forward high-angle directors and the starboard after one were out of action, one four-gunned turret had jammed and she was holed underwater aft [S15]. By 0720 two guns of 'Y' turret were in action again and her best speed was reported as 27 knots [S15]. When the shadowing cruisers engaged from astern at 1840 on 24 May, Tovey writes that "the unreliability of the Prince of Wales' armament was demonstrated once more, as two guns again went out of action" [S15]. He judged that she had joined the fleet on 25 March with contractor's workmen still aboard, that the last of her turrets was not accepted from the contractor until 27 April, and that her turrets were "of a new and untried model, were known to be liable to teething-troubles and could already be seen to be suffering them" [S15].

### King George V against Bismarck, 27 May 1941

King George V fired 339 shells in the final action against 380 by Rodney [S6]. NavWeaps quotes an account of her turret performance: she began well at 1.7 salvoes per minute under radar control, then from 0920 'A' turret was completely out of action for 30 minutes after about 23 rounds per gun because of a jam between the fixed and revolving structure in the shell room, and 'Y' was out for 7 minutes through drill errors; both guns in 'B', guns 2 and 4 in 'A' and gun 2 in 'Y' were jammed and stayed out for the rest of the action, five guns of ten; gun 3 of 'A' misfired twice and was out for 30 minutes before the breech could safely be opened [S6]. NavWeaps also quotes Tovey's after-action report on the cause: "Comparatively little experience had previously been gained of the reliability of the turrets. The prolonged practice firing for the King George V had been carried out only in one turret. It was fortunate that the action was not prolonged, because the 25 rounds per gun practice previously planned would not have shown up so many of the defects." [S6].

### North Cape, 26 December 1943

Duke of York shot for two hours [S6]. She made 31 straddles out of 52 broadsides in the first phase and 21 out of 25 in the second, and fired 446 shells in 80 broadsides, while still firing less than 70 per cent of her possible output because of mechanical failures and drill errors [S6]. She missed 241 firing opportunities [S6]. The failures named are the bridge flash tubes in the working chambers not closing completely, the collapse of shell arresters in the lower hoists, and a shell-cage defect in 'A' turret [S6]. Every gun missed at least some broadsides; B1 was the most reliable, missing three of the 80; poor loading drill and a shell cage fault made A1 miss 73 broadsides; and three guns in 'Y' were unable to fire for a 15-minute period, missing 17 broadsides [S6].

The ADM 234/271 essay gives the mechanism of the 'Y' turret failure [S7]. Because of the heavy movement of the ship the rammers were being worked carefully to avoid driving shells into the arrestors too hard. A roll and heavy lurch coincided with a ramming stroke; the rammers were stopped but the shells had taken charge and ran into the shell arrestors before the stop bolt was in position behind them, and the arrestors collapsed into the In position. The shells then projected beyond the shell cage so it could not rise. This affected guns 1, 3 and 4 in 'Y', and it took 15 minutes to force the shells back into the cages and reset the arrestors [S7].

### Bombardments of Japan, 1945

At King George V's first bombardment mission against the Hitachi works she was to fire 10 rounds of HE per gun and missed 15 through trouble with flashtight doors and interlocks; at Hamamatsu on 29 July 1945 she was to fire 300 rounds with reduced charges and fired 265 because of misfires in the lock firing mechanisms [S6]. The naval-history.net chronology and the museum guide both put the Hamamatsu firing at 265 rounds of 14-inch [S1], [S18].

## Modifications after the failures

After the Bismarck battles, King George V and Prince of Wales had numerous changes made to the ammunition supply safety interlock system, the watertightness of the mantlet plates was improved, and the existing shell-room drains were enlarged and further drains fitted; the changes were worked into the remaining ships as they were built [S6]. After North Cape the stop bolt behind the shell arrestor was replaced by a hydraulic buffer cylinder able to take more force when shells were rammed on the incline, and an interlock was added so that ramming could not begin before the buffer was in position [S7].

## Secondary battery: 5.25-inch/50 QF Mark I in twin HA/LA Mark I mountings

Eight twin mountings, four a side, numbered P1 to P4 to port and S1 to S4 to starboard from forward [S24], [S18]. The calibre was chosen as the largest whose shell an average gun's crew could still handle by hand at all angles of elevation for sustained periods [S8], [S17]. The Gunnery Pocket Book states that in the King George V class these guns fill the combined role of high-angle long-range armament and secondary armament against surface craft, and that the Mark I mounting differs from the cruisers' Mark II mainly in the arrangement of shell rooms and magazines and in the ammunition supply [S17].

The gun is of all-steel construction, a loose barrel with jacket, removable breech ring and sealing collar, with a hand-operated horizontal sliding breech block and semi-automatic opening; the gun has to be dismounted to change the barrel [S8]. Firing is electric only, by director, by the gunlayer's pistol, or by the breechworker's push [S17]. Recoil is controlled by a cylinder under the cradle with a run-out recuperator in the upper part of the balance weight, reaching 1,800 lbs per square inch of air pressure at the end of recoil [S17]. Air blast from the ship's supply clears gas and residue through the muzzles after each round, with two air cylinders at motor-chamber level as a standby [S17]. Mark I and Mark II mountings are driven by an 80 bhp electric motor, peaking at 160 bhp, turning an oil hydraulic pump on the revolving structure; training is by hydraulic motor through two worm and pinion drives, elevation by a hydraulic motor per gun driving a worm gear, with hand gear for both and compressed air for run-out [S8]. Turret ventilation fans are powered from the same source as the main pump motor, so losing power to the mounting also loses the fans [S17].

Ammunition supply in the battleship Mark I was a short-trunk arrangement, with shells and cartridges delivered to a handing room just below the mounting [S8]. The two supply schemes differ between end and middle mountings [S8]:

- P1, P4, S1 and S4 had three fixed electric endless-chain hoists, for LA shell, HA shell and cartridges, running from the shell room and magazines to the handing room below the mounting. There, shells and cartridges fed into chutes leading to circular rings around the mounting at working-chamber level. Cartridges went to hand-ups to the gunhouse; HA and LA shells were raised by hydraulic pusher hoists, one per gun.
- P2, P3, S2 and S3 had two sets of fixed lower hoists with a break and transfer through chutes on the lower deck, the lower shell hoists being dredger type carrying the shells horizontally rather than the endless-chain type. Above the break the supply was the same as for the end mountings.

In the gunhouse the HA hoist raises the shell horizontally to a hand-controlled extension hoist near the inner trunnions; the shell rolls into the fuze setting tray and is then moved by hand to the loading tray. The LA hoist comes up behind the HA hoist with the shell vertical and it is also moved by hand to the loading tray. The loading tray is worked by hand and ramming is hydraulic [S8]. Twelve men worked in the gunhouse: three at the fuze setters and the turret captain, two gunlayers, a turret trainer, two breech workers, two projectile loaders and two cartridge loaders [S8]. An IWM photograph of a King George V Mark I gunhouse in 1943 shows the fuze setters, the ready rounds above them, and the LA shell hoists in the foreground [S41].

The mounting fell short in two ways that mattered in action. The gunhouse was cramped and the heavy projectile and cartridge case meant crews could not transfer shell and cordite from the hoists to the loading trays at the designed rate, so the usual rate of fire was 7 to 8 rounds per minute instead of 10 to 12 [S8]. Training and elevation were slow at 10 degrees per second, too slow to track fast aircraft, and the mountings were difficult to train by hand when unpowered [S8]. Both faults told during the Japanese attacks on Prince of Wales on 10 December 1941: with a 10 to 11 degree list some mountings could not be trained to engage the following attacks [S8]. The forensic analysis of her loss adds that the forward starboard mountings could not depress low enough to engage the torpedo aircraft, that the after group had no electric power at all, that only S1 and S2 were manageable, and that the crews could not train the other mountings by hand using chains [S24].

After the Bismarck battles the 5.25-inch mountings in King George V and Prince of Wales were modified to improve the watertightness of their mantlet plates, and the change was worked into the rest of the class during building [S8]. After the loss of Prince of Wales, the surviving ships had duplicated power leads installed and extra protection worked in around the cable entries into the mountings [S8]. Late in the war the two after mountings of the cruiser Argonaut and all eight mountings in Anson were converted to the RP10 standard, which added remote power control, vertical steadying rollers, smoothed training and elevating drives, and a power loading tray with an automatic ramming link [S8].

Outfit was SAP, HE and illuminating rounds; SAP "K" shells with splash dye came in after about 1942, without the separate fuze and burster used in larger calibres; anti-radar rounds filled with aluminium foil were issued late in the war to ships in the Indian and Pacific Oceans at about 200 rounds a ship; and VT-fuzed HE was introduced in 1944, equipping 25 to 50 per cent of HE rounds by 1945 depending on supply [S8]. Royal Marines worked part of the secondary battery; an IWM photograph shows Royal Marines at the secondary armament in Howe in August 1942 [S50].

## Close-range anti-aircraft: 2-pdr QF Mark VIII pom-pom

The eight-barrelled Mark VI and Mark VIA octuple mountings were the class's primary close-range weapon from completion [S69], [S9]. The gun is a redesign of the 2-pdr Mark II for an eight-gun mounting, using the same barrel with an improved automatic mechanism and steel-link belts in place of fabric [S9].

Octuple mountings were trained and elevated by a self-contained electro-hydraulic system driven by an 11 hp motor and had a crew of twelve; the Mark V differed from the Mark VI in having a heavier carriage and different controlled firing gear, and the Mark VA and Mark VIA, introduced in 1939, had motorised firing gear [S9]. "Controlled" firing means the guns were cranked so that they fired in a fixed sequence rather than freely; in the octuple they fired in quads and in the quadruple in pairs. In 1939 the octuples were changed to fire fully automatically, but the quadruples kept the controlled gear throughout the war [S9]. Ammunition came in 14-round steel-link belts that clipped together; the feed boxes held 140 rounds per gun on the octuple and 112 on the quadruple [S9]. The Gunnery Pocket Book describes the loading of the quadruple with eight belts per gun on the feed rails, the first and last round of each belt resting on the rails and the rest hanging in a bight [S68]. The internal mechanisms were complex and needed skilled maintenance; jams and stoppages were frequent, though the linked ammunition proved more reliable than the earlier belt feed [S9].

By the end of the war the King George V class carried as many as eight octuple mountings, the largest number in any British ship [S9]. By September 1945 Duke of York, Anson and Howe each carried six of the late-war Mark VII*P quadruple mountings, which had self-contained power and dual joystick control [S9]. USN Mark 14 gyro sights were fitted to some quadruples in 1945 as an anti-kamikaze measure, including at least Anson and Duke of York [S9].

Fire control for the pom-poms was the Pom-Pom Director Mark IV, in which a gyro rate unit measures the target's vertical and lateral movement, combines it with the shell's time of flight, and produces the deflections; transmission to the mounting is by remote power control, so the mounting follows the director automatically [S68]. The director crew is eight: control officer, gyro rate unit operator, director layer, director trainer, vertical rate follower, lateral rate follower and communication number, range follower, and the radar operator in the radar office [S68].

## Close-range anti-aircraft: 20 mm Oerlikon

The British Mark I entered service in 1939 and the Mark II in 1941 [S11]. The gun weighs 150 lbs (68.04 kg) including the breech mechanism, is 87 in (2.210 m) long overall with a bore length of 55.1 in (1.400 m), and has a cyclic rate of 450 rounds per minute and a practical rate of 250 to 320 [S11]. The museum statistics give shell weight 0.27 lb, muzzle velocity 2,770 ft/sec, maximum range 4,800 yards, maximum elevation 90 degrees and a rate of fire of 450 rounds per minute [S18]. Muzzle velocity is 2,770 fps (844 mps) for a new gun and 2,725 fps (835 mps) for an average gun, the British HE projectile weighs 0.272 lbs (0.123 kg), the propellant charge is 0.063 lbs (0.029 kg) of NC flake or tube, working pressure is 20 tons/in2, and barrel life is about 9,000 rounds [S11]. Effective range is 1,000 yards [S68].

The gun is blowback operated and the breech is not locked at discharge; the round fires a fraction of an inch before it is fully home and the neck of the case swells to seal the gases, and the barrel and casing do not recoil [S68]. Six drum magazines of sixty rounds are supplied with each gun, together with a loading stand and two ratchet levers for tensioning the magazine spring; a fully loaded magazine must be fully tensioned or stoppages follow, and a partly loaded one is tensioned to the round count plus two further clicks [S68]. Every round must be lightly greased by hand with Grease No. 0 before loading, and the grease visible at the mouth of a loaded magazine must not be wiped off [S68]. Because the gun vibrates heavily when firing, the aperture backsight is used like a bead sight with the head held well back and the rubber eyepiece removed [S68]. Two Oerlikons in Howe in August 1942 are the subject of an IWM photograph [S49].

## Close-range anti-aircraft: 40 mm Bofors

Bofors-built guns entered Royal Navy service in 1941 and British-built guns in 1942 [S10]. The British gun is listed at 56.3 calibres, with a bore length of 88.578 in (2.250 m), a gun weight of 1,120 to 1,163 lbs (508 to 528 kg) water-cooled depending on mark, and 16 grooves [S10]. The nominal cyclic rate is 120 rounds per minute per barrel, rising to 140 to 160 when firing horizontally with gravity assist, with a practical rate of 80 to 90 [S10]. British muzzle velocity is 2,800 fps (853 mps) with Cordite W and 2,890 fps (881 mps) with FNH/PO22, at a working pressure of 19.68 tons/in2 [S10]. The museum statistics give shell weight 1.98 lb, muzzle velocity 2,890 ft/sec, maximum effective range 2,500 yards, maximum altitude 18,000 ft, and 120 rounds per minute [S18]. Maximum effective range in local control is given as 1,700 yards [S68].

The Royal Navy considered the Bofors at least twice as effective as its own 2-pdr against torpedo bombers, but not much better against kamikazes [S10]. The United States supplied 393 each of its Mark 1 left-hand and Mark 2 right-hand guns to the Royal Navy for use in British RP Mark I twin and RP Mark II quadruple mountings, the quadruple first installed in HMS Phoebe in June 1943 [S10]. The single Mark III series was the Army hand-worked Land Service design adapted for naval use, with elevation limits of -5 to +90 degrees and a mounting weight including gun of 1.2 tons (1.22 mt) [S10]. The single Mark VII, an adapted Army mounting weighing 1.40 tons (1.42 mt), was not put on production order until 29 May 1945 [S10]. The Prince of Wales carried one 40 mm gun in a single mounting with an outload of 1,440 rounds [S26].

## Unrotated Projectile (UP) rocket mountings

The UP mounting fired an explosive charge carried aloft under a parachute on a line, and was fitted to the first two ships of the class; the tier 3 monograph judges them clearly useless and records that the later ships had 20 mm guns in their place [S69]. Navypedia describes the fitting as a 20-barrel projector for a 178 mm rocket, four in King George V and three in Prince of Wales [S23]. A NavWeaps photograph caption records a UP mounting on the roof of 'B' turret in King George V in March 1941, and another records that by the 1945 Rosyth refit the octuple pom-pom on the roof of Duke of York's 'B' turret had taken the place the UP rockets occupied in the earlier ships [S6].

## Aircraft: Supermarine Walrus and catapult

Two Supermarine Walrus amphibians were carried on an athwartships catapult with two hangars, for reconnaissance and for spotting the fall of shot; the aircraft were removed as radar took over both roles, which also removed a major fire hazard [S69]. Navypedia gives the catapult as a D-III-H and lists Walrus, Swordfish and Seafox as the aircraft types for the first three ships and Walrus alone for Anson and Howe [S23]. The Prince of Wales outload in December 1941 included 25 catapult zone charges and, for the two Walrus, eight 250 lb SAP, eight 100 lb AS and eight 40 lb GP bombs [S26]. Aircraft and catapult were removed during the 1944 to 1945 refits: from Howe in May 1944, King George V in July 1944, and Duke of York and Anson in March 1945 [S23]. Anson's aircraft facilities were removed in her 1944 to 1945 Devonport refit and the space given to accommodation and boat stowage [S4].
